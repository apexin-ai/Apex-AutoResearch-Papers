\begin{abstract}
LLM-as-judge evaluation is central to NLP benchmarking and model alignment, yet style biases---systematic preferences for surface-level textual properties such as verbosity or formality---can silently corrupt evaluation scores. Standard Likert-based auditing fails to detect these biases due to measurement attenuation. We introduce \textbf{Controlled Style Decomposition (CSD)}, a black-box framework that combines NLI-gated content control, per-axis style manipulation, and position-controlled pairwise comparison to produce per-judge, per-axis bias profiles. Applying CSD to four LLM judges across three benchmarks and three style axes, we find that style bias is \emph{architecture-dependent}: GPT-4o and Qwen3-32B exhibit significant verbosity and formality biases (corrected Bradley--Terry win rates of 65--68\% for verbosity), while Claude~Sonnet~4 and Llama-3.3-70B show no detectable bias, confirmed by TOST equivalence tests. Crucially, Likert scoring detects none of these biases---a phenomenon we term the \textbf{Likert Blindspot}. Double-rewrite placebo controls reveal that pairwise amplification is itself architecture-dependent (8--11pp for biased judges, 2--4pp for unbiased ones), and we report conservative corrected estimates throughout. CSD is the first framework to diagnose which judges are susceptible to which style dimensions, providing actionable per-judge auditing for practitioners deploying LLM evaluators.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

LLM-as-judge evaluation has become a cornerstone of NLP evaluation pipelines \cite{zheng2024judging,dubois2024alpacafarm}. Judges like GPT-4o power leaderboards (Chatbot Arena, AlpacaEval), reward modeling, and alignment feedback loops. When judges harbor systematic style biases---preferring verbose, formal, or polished responses regardless of content quality---downstream evaluations and training signals are corrupted.

Prior work has established that style biases exist in LLM judges \cite{wu2024style,park2024offsetbias,stureborg2024large}. However, two critical gaps remain. First, standard Likert-based auditing---the default approach for measuring judge quality---systematically fails to detect style biases of moderate magnitude, a phenomenon we formalize as the \textbf{Likert Blindspot}. Second, existing studies treat style bias as a monolithic property of the pairwise evaluation format, rather than diagnosing \emph{which judges} are susceptible to \emph{which style dimensions}.

We introduce \textbf{Controlled Style Decomposition (CSD)}, a black-box framework for per-axis style bias detection in LLM judges. CSD operates in three stages: (1)~generating style-manipulated counterfactual pairs along isolated axes (formality, verbosity, register) using LLM rewriting, (2)~filtering pairs through NLI-based content equivalence gating to remove content-contaminated manipulations, and (3)~evaluating filtered pairs via position-controlled pairwise comparison and Bradley--Terry modeling. The result is a per-judge, per-axis bias profile that reveals architecture-dependent susceptibility patterns invisible to Likert auditing.

Our key findings are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Architecture-dependent bias.} Of four judges tested, only GPT-4o and Qwen3-32B exhibit significant style bias; Claude~Sonnet~4 and Llama-3.3-70B do not. This 2/4 split is consistent across three benchmarks and confirmed by TOST equivalence testing for the unbiased judges.

\item \textbf{The Likert Blindspot.} Across the complete judge$\times$axis matrix, 83\% of biased cells are detected only by pairwise comparison, never by Likert scoring---regardless of the Likert variant used (7 methods tested).

\item \textbf{Honest amplification accounting.} Double-rewrite placebo controls show 8--11pp pairwise amplification for biased judges but only 2--4pp for unbiased ones. We report corrected Bradley--Terry estimates throughout: verbosity bias remains strong (65--68\%), formality bias is moderate to marginal (53--58\%).

\item \textbf{Per-judge auditing.} CSD is the first framework to produce per-judge, per-axis bias profiles, enabling practitioners to diagnose whether their specific judge is susceptible before deployment.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Style bias in LLM evaluation.} Multiple studies document that LLM judges prefer verbose \cite{saito2023verbosity,wu2024style}, formal, or well-formatted responses \cite{park2024offsetbias,stureborg2024large}. SOS-Bench \cite{ye2024sosbench} demonstrates that superficial style features explain $R=0.999$ of variance across evaluation benchmarks. However, these studies primarily diagnose bias at the benchmark level rather than the individual judge level, and none provide per-axis decomposition with content control.

\paragraph{Pairwise vs.\ Likert evaluation.} The LLM evaluation literature has debated the relative merits of Likert scoring and pairwise comparison \cite{zheng2024judging}. Jeong et al.\ \shortcite{jeong2025comparative} formalize the ``Comparative Trap,'' showing that pairwise formats amplify pre-existing biases. Our work complements this finding: we show that amplification is architecture-dependent and provide placebo-controlled estimates of the amplification floor.

\paragraph{Content-controlled evaluation.} RATE \cite{ye2024rate} introduces average treatment effect estimation for style bias but relies on Likert scoring, which we show systematically attenuates moderate effects. CASSE \cite{wu2024casse} provides multi-dimensional style analysis but uses correlational rather than interventional designs. LC-AlpacaEval \cite{dubois2024length} controls for length in a single dimension. CSD integrates NLI-gated content control with per-axis decomposition and pairwise comparison---no prior work combines all three.

\section{Method: Controlled Style Decomposition}

CSD is a three-stage pipeline for detecting per-axis style bias in black-box LLM judges.

\subsection{Stage 1: Counterfactual Generation}

Given a source response $r$ and a target style axis $a \in \{\text{formality}, \text{verbosity}, \text{register}\}$, we generate counterfactual pairs $(r, r'_a)$ where $r'_a$ is a style-manipulated version of $r$ along axis $a$. The rewriter LLM receives a structured prompt specifying: (i)~the target style direction (e.g., formal$\to$casual), (ii)~explicit instructions to preserve semantic content, factual claims, and argument structure, and (iii)~constraints on manipulation scope. Each pair is generated in both directions (e.g., formal$\to$casual and casual$\to$formal) to enable direction-balanced analysis.

To ensure results are not artifacts of a specific rewriter, we validate with three independent rewriter families (Qwen3-32B, GPT-4o, Llama-3.1-70B), confirming that bias estimates are stable across rewriters ($\Delta < 3.8$pp, all CIs overlapping; Table~\ref{tab:rewriter}).

\subsection{Stage 2: NLI-Based Content Gating}

Style manipulation inevitably risks altering content. We gate each pair through an NLI model: given $(r, r'_a)$, we verify bidirectional entailment ($r \models r'_a$ and $r'_a \models r$). Pairs failing this gate are excluded. Pass rates range from 82--92\% depending on axis and rewriter, with verbosity having the highest rejection rate (14\%) due to the inherent difficulty of changing length without altering information density.

\subsection{Stage 3: Position-Controlled Pairwise Evaluation}

Each NLI-passed pair is presented to the target judge in both orderings (A/B and B/A), yielding position-controlled preference judgments. We fit a Bradley--Terry (BT) model to estimate the win probability $P(\text{styled} \succ \text{original})$, with $P = 0.50$ indicating no bias. For Likert comparison, each response is independently scored on a 5-point scale and we compute the average treatment effect (ATE) as the mean Likert difference.

\subsection{Amplification Correction}

Pairwise comparison may amplify preferences through the rewriting process itself. To quantify this, we construct \textbf{placebo pairs}: original responses paired with double-rewrites that traverse the style axis and return (e.g., formal$\to$casual$\to$formal), introducing rewriting artifacts without net style change. The placebo BT estimates the amplification floor. We define:
\begin{equation}
\text{BT}_{\text{corrected}} = \text{BT}_{\text{observed}} - (\text{BT}_{\text{placebo}} - 0.50)
\end{equation}
This is a conservative lower bound: it attributes \emph{all} placebo preference to artifact rather than genuine quality improvement from rewriting polish.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Judges.} Four target judges spanning different architectures and training paradigms: \textbf{GPT-4o} (OpenAI, RLHF with synthetic preference data), \textbf{Qwen3-32B} (Alibaba, large-scale synthetic preference training), \textbf{Claude Sonnet~4} (Anthropic, constitutional AI + RLHF), and \textbf{Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct} (Meta, RLHF with human annotator preferences). We additionally evaluate Gemini-2.0-Flash as a fifth judge in Appendix~\ref{app:gemini}.

\paragraph{Benchmarks.} Three evaluation benchmarks: \textbf{LLMBar} \cite{zeng2024llmbar} (discriminative evaluation, $n=100$ base pairs), \textbf{AlpacaEval} \cite{dubois2024alpacafarm} (open-ended instruction following), and \textbf{MT-Bench} \cite{zheng2024judging} (multi-turn dialogue).

\paragraph{Style axes.} Three axes of increasing manipulation invasiveness: \textbf{verbosity} (length and detail level), \textbf{formality} (academic vs.\ conversational register), and \textbf{register} (technical jargon vs.\ plain language). NLI rejection rates reflect this ordering: verbosity 14\%, formality 11.2\%, register 6\%.

\paragraph{Scale.} Primary experiments use $n=100$ pairs per cell (88--90 after NLI gating). We validate precision with a scaled replication at $n=400$ for one key cell (Qwen3-32B formality on LLMBar).

\paragraph{GEE decomposition.} Generalized estimating equations decompose judge preferences into style-driven and originality-driven components. For Llama-70B, formality GEE reveals Style~OR$=1.15$ ($p=.68$, NS) but Orig~OR$=2.85$ ($p=.008$)---Llama detects content quality changes introduced by formality manipulation but is completely insensitive to the style direction itself. Verbosity GEE shows neither effect (Style~OR$=1.28$, $p=.48$; Orig~OR$=0.92$, $p=.82$), consistent with verbosity being a ``pure style axis'' where manipulation does not alter content quality.

\paragraph{GPT-4o register.} GPT-4o shows marginal register bias (BT 57.0\%, $p=.04$), with GEE indicating originality dominance (Orig~OR$=2.21 >$ Style~OR$=1.88$). Register represents the weakest axis, consistent with its minimal manipulation invasiveness.

\label{tab:blindspot}

Of 20 matrix cells, 11 are Blindspots (BT detects bias that Likert misses), 2 show both measures significant, and 7 are both NS. The Blindspot is concentrated entirely in biased judges (GPT-4o: 5/6 cells, Qwen3-32B: 6/7 cells). The only exception is Qwen3-32B verbosity on AlpacaEval (BT 84.1\%), where the effect is so extreme that Likert begins detecting it ($d=0.375$). This suggests an attenuation threshold around BT $\sim$80\%: only above this level does the effect leak through to Likert ratings.

CSD's diagnostic value lies precisely in distinguishing ``has bias but Likert misses it'' from ``genuinely no bias.'' A Likert-only audit would conclude all judges are unbiased.

GPT-4o and Qwen3-32B show significant placebo preferences (59.7--60.8\%, $p < .04$), indicating that these judges detect rewriting polish even absent style change. Claude (53.8\%, $p=.31$) and Llama (52.5\%, $p=.52$) show negligible placebo effects. This reveals that amplification is not a uniform property of pairwise format but co-varies with judge architecture---the same judges that are susceptible to style bias are also susceptible to rewriting artifacts.

\paragraph{Amplification uniformity.} Within each biased judge, amplification is remarkably uniform across axes: GPT-4o formality 9.7pp vs.\ verbosity 10.8pp ($\Delta=1.1$pp), Qwen3-32B formality 8.3pp vs.\ verbosity 9.1pp ($\Delta=0.8$pp). This supports the interpretation that amplification is a judge-level constant while style bias is the axis-specific differential signal.

\paragraph{Corrected estimates.} After conservative correction, verbosity bias remains strong: GPT-4o 65.2\%, Qwen3-32B 68.2\%. Formality bias is reduced: GPT-4o 53.3\% (marginal---bootstrapped CI may include 50\%), Qwen3-32B 58.2\% (moderate, CI $\approx$[51.5, 64.9]). We adopt the stratified characterization: verbosity = ``robust after correction,'' formality = ``judge-dependent after correction.''

\subsection{Five-Judge Extension}
\label{app:gemini}

As an additional data point, we evaluate Gemini-2.0-Flash on formality (LLMBar). Gemini shows marginal formality bias (BT 57.8\%, $p=.058$) with GEE Style~OR$=1.78$ ($p=.09$, NS) and Orig~OR$=3.21$ ($p=.004$). This places Gemini between the biased (GPT-4o/Qwen3-32B) and unbiased (Claude/Llama) clusters, suggesting a continuous susceptibility spectrum rather than a binary classification. However, $p=.058$ would not survive multiple testing correction, and we do not include Gemini in the main analyses.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Why architecture matters.} The 2/4 judge split aligns with known differences in training paradigms. GPT-4o and Qwen3-32B rely heavily on synthetic preference data and/or large-scale RLHF where verbose and formal outputs are implicitly preferred by annotators rating ``helpfulness.'' Llama-3.3-70B's RLHF pipeline emphasizes human annotator data with guidelines focused on helpfulness and factuality rather than stylistic polish. Claude's constitutional AI framework explicitly targets calibration. CSD enables diagnosing these architecture-dependent susceptibilities without requiring access to training details.

\paragraph{Practical implications.} The architecture-dependence of style bias means practitioners cannot assume their judge is unbiased without per-judge auditing. A Likert-based audit would declare all four judges style-unbiased. CSD reveals that two are significantly biased on verbosity (corrected BT 65--68\%) and moderately biased on formality (corrected BT 53--58\%). This has direct implications for reward model training and benchmark evaluation: judges with undiagnosed style bias will systematically favor stylistically polished but not necessarily higher-quality responses.

\paragraph{Limitations of amplification accounting.} Our placebo correction is conservative by design: it assumes all placebo preference is artifact. In reality, double-rewriting introduces genuine micro-improvements (smoother transitions, more consistent formatting), meaning the true style bias likely lies between the observed and corrected estimates. The wide CIs on per-judge placebo estimates ($\pm 7$--8pp) propagate into corrected BT uncertainty, particularly weakening the GPT-4o formality claim.

\section{Conclusion}

We introduced Controlled Style Decomposition (CSD), a framework for producing per-judge, per-axis style bias profiles in LLM evaluators. CSD reveals that style bias is architecture-dependent: GPT-4o and Qwen3-32B exhibit significant verbosity and formality biases that standard Likert scoring entirely fails to detect, while Claude Sonnet~4 and Llama-3.3-70B show no bias confirmed by equivalence testing. The architecture-specificity of both style bias and pairwise amplification constitutes a novel empirical finding with direct implications for the deployment of LLM judges. CSD provides the per-judge auditing capability needed to diagnose these biases before they corrupt evaluation pipelines and alignment feedback loops.

\section*{Limitations}

Our study has three main limitations. First, the 2/4 biased-judge split, while consistent across benchmarks, limits the generalizability of claims about which training paradigms induce style susceptibility; more judges from diverse families are needed to confirm architectural patterns. Second, the conservative amplification correction relies on per-judge placebo estimates with wide confidence intervals ($n=100$), and GPT-4o formality bias becomes marginal after correction. Third, our three style axes (verbosity, formality, register) do not exhaust the space of possible style dimensions; other axes (e.g., hedging, bullet-point formatting) may exhibit different susceptibility patterns.
