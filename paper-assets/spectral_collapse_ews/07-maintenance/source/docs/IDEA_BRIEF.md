## Core Problem

Model collapse during recursive synthetic training is increasingly well-diagnosed — SIGMA tracks log-determinant spectral contraction of embedding Gram matrices, calibration-based monitoring detects degradation before accuracy loss, topology-based methods detect representational anisotropy — and theoretically optimal static mixing ratios have been derived (converging to the reciprocal of the golden ratio in simplified settings). Meanwhile, adaptive data selection methods like DCES address collapse through intra-cluster rebalancing within the synthetic pool. Yet no existing system closes the loop from spectral diagnosis to adaptive global real/synthetic mixing. Static mixing ratios, whether theoretically optimal or empirically tuned, assume the collapse pressure is constant across training — but empirical evidence consistently shows otherwise: SIGMA's S2 experiments demonstrate that spectral contraction accelerates non-linearly, with the synthetic pool 'losing whole modes' around generation 20 and Track II drift intensifying sharply thereafter (final drift of -1536.562, slope -42.621); Shumailov et al. (Nature 2024) show that tail collapse compounds across generations with longer error tails appearing in later generations; arXiv 2510.05133 identifies inflection points at 20-25% synthetic data proportion where per-unit degradation rates double from 0.1-0.2 to 0.4-0.8 percentage points; and the Knowledge Collapse study (arXiv 2509.04796) confirms three distinct non-linear collapse stages on GEMMA 3 1B. This generation-dependent, non-linear collapse pressure is precisely what makes static mixing suboptimal — and what motivates a closed-loop controller that concentrates real data where collapse pressure is highest. Crucially, the question is not whether more real data helps (it trivially does), but whether the same fixed real-data budget achieves better collapse prevention when allocated adaptively across generations rather than uniformly.

## Opportunity / Why Now

SIGMA (2026) provides scalable spectral monitoring via embedding Gram matrix log-determinants during recursive training, but is purely diagnostic — it tracks collapse without prescribing interventions. Crucially, SIGMA's S2 experiments on OPT-125M across 50 recursive generations reveal that spectral contraction is non-linear and accelerating: Track II shows a final drift of approximately -1536.562 with slope -42.621, and around generation 20 the synthetic pool begins losing whole modes, with the metric dropping much faster — demonstrating clear generational acceleration rather than linear degradation. This non-linearity is the linchpin of our contribution: if collapse pressure were constant across generations, static mixing would be near-optimal, and budget redistribution would yield no advantage. But the empirical evidence consistently shows acceleration. Shumailov et al. (Nature 2024) demonstrate that tail collapse compounds across generations — early model collapse is subtle (losing tail information), while late collapse is catastrophic (producing samples never seen in the original distribution), with longer error tails appearing in later generations. The calibration-based monitoring literature corroborates this: arXiv 2510.05133 finds that degradation below 20-25% synthetic data averages 0.1-0.2 percentage points per unit synthetic proportion, but accelerates to 0.4-0.8 pp/pp beyond 30%, with ECE increasing from 0.08 to 0.14 at iteration 1 (while accuracy remains flat) and reaching 0.21-0.28 in subsequent iterations as accuracy degrades sharply. The Knowledge Collapse study (arXiv 2509.04796) further confirms three distinct non-linear stages: knowledge preservation, knowledge collapse ('confidently wrong'), and instruction-following collapse — with prompt-dependent transition points rather than uniform degradation. The theoretical static-mixing literature has matured rapidly: Golden Ratio Weighting (He et al. 2025) proves the optimal real-data weight converges to 1/φ ≈ 0.618 across Gaussian, GLM, and nonparametric settings; Garg et al. (2025) extend this to ridge regression under overparametrization, showing optimal mixing ≥ 0.5; and recent theoretical work on iterative training with contaminated data (arXiv 2602.10531) shows that even decaying real-data mixing weights can avoid collapse under appropriate sample size conditions (∑αₜ = ∞, ∑1/nₜ < ∞), providing theoretical grounding for dynamic mixing strategies. However, these theoretical optima apply to idealized settings with constant collapse pressure — they cannot account for the empirically observed generation-dependent acceleration. The closest adaptive approach is DCES (Dynamic Center-Edge Sampling, MDPI Electronics 2026), which uses intra-cluster dispersion to adaptively rebalance center-edge sampling ratios within the synthetic pool — but DCES operates at the sample selection level within the synthetic data, not at the global real/synthetic mixing ratio level, and does not connect to spectral Gram matrix health or theoretical mixing optima. Alternative prevention strategies — data accumulation (Gerstgrasser et al. 2024), verification (He et al. 2025), pluralistic curation (Falahati et al. 2026) — address collapse through data quality or quantity but not through adaptive real-time mixing control. Budget-constrained adaptive allocation has been studied in curriculum learning contexts (ADAPT, arXiv 2512.04555, uses meta-gradients to learn task sampling proportions under explicit token budgets for instruction tuning), but never applied to the problem of redistributing a fixed real-data budget across recursive training generations based on spectral collapse pressure. The gap: connecting spectral degradation monitoring to budget-constrained adaptive global data mixing decisions in a closed-loop system that dynamically redistributes a fixed real data budget across recursive training generations, exploiting the empirically established non-linearity of collapse pressure to achieve better collapse prevention than uniform allocation at the same total real-data cost.

## Landscape (SOTA & Limitations)

SIGMA (arXiv 2601.03385, Mar 2026) benchmarks model collapse through embedding Gram matrix spectral analysis, deriving deterministic bounds (Track I: log det(Iₘ + Gₐ/(β̂+δ)), computed efficiently via Cholesky decomposition on shifted PSD matrices, where β̂ₖ = (nₖ - nₐ)·ρ is the tail-energy budget estimator) and stochastic bounds (Track II). Validated on OPT-125M across 50 recursive generations using a frozen sentence encoder with embedding dimension m=384. The framework is 'black-box friendly' requiring only output embeddings — the frozen sentence encoder encodes generated text rather than internal activations, making it architecturally portable in principle. However, the Track I bound's calibration (β̂ estimator relying on ρ = maximum embedding norm squared, and δ regularization) was tuned for OPT-125M outputs, and may require re-calibration for different architectures (e.g., Pythia) whose output distributions could differ in scale and geometry. No public code repository is available as of May 2026. DCES (Dynamic Center-Edge Sampling, MDPI Electronics 15(4):869, Feb 2026) is the closest adaptive approach to collapse prevention: it monitors intra-cluster dispersion of semantic clusters and dynamically adjusts center-edge sampling ratios during iterative self-training, tested on GPT-2, Qwen, and OPT-6.7B across 10 rounds. Key distinctions from our proposal: (a) DCES adapts sample selection within the synthetic pool (which center vs. edge samples to include), whereas we adapt the global real/synthetic mixing ratio; (b) DCES uses semantic cluster dispersion while we use mathematically grounded spectral Gram matrix bounds; (c) our method connects to theoretical mixing optima (golden ratio) that DCES does not; (d) DCES's token-level editing and anchor-model filtering are complementary mechanisms that could be combined with spectral-guided mixing in future work. Characterizing Model Behavior Under Synthetic Data Training (arXiv 2510.05133, Oct 2025) demonstrates on Pythia 410M-12B that calibration degradation (ECE) precedes accuracy loss by 1-2 iterations with 91% precision and 87% recall using a 50% ECE increase threshold — and critically shows that degradation accelerates non-linearly beyond 20-25% synthetic data proportion (from 0.1-0.2 to 0.4-0.8 pp/pp), providing both a competing monitoring signal and direct empirical evidence for generation-dependent collapse pressure. Knowledge Collapse in LLMs (arXiv 2509.04796, Sep 2025) demonstrates three distinct non-linear collapse stages on GEMMA 3 1B under recursive synthetic training at α ∈ {0.25, 0.50, 1.0}: knowledge preservation → knowledge collapse ('confidently wrong') → instruction-following collapse, with prompt-dependent transition points, further confirming that collapse pressure is generation-dependent and non-uniform. Strong Model Collapse (ICLR 2025) proves that even tiny synthetic fractions cause collapse under scaling. Entropy Collapse (arXiv 2512.12381, Dec 2025) proves entropy collapse is a first-order discontinuous phase transition without critical slowing down — ruling out ecological EWS but not spectral log-determinant contraction as a gradual, actionable feedback signal. Golden Ratio Weighting (arXiv 2502.18049, He et al., Feb 2025) proves the optimal real-data weight converges to 1/φ for Gaussian, GLM, and nonparametric settings — establishing theoretical static optima but assuming constant collapse pressure. Preventing Model Collapse Under Overparametrization (arXiv 2509.22341, Garg et al., Sep 2025) extends the golden ratio result to min-ℓ2-norm interpolation and ridge regression, showing the optimal real-data proportion is ≥ 0.5. From Collapse to Improvement (arXiv 2602.10531, Feb 2026) analyzes iterative training on contaminated sources and proves that decaying real-data mixing weights (αₜ → 0) still avoid collapse when ∑αₜ = ∞ and sample sizes grow appropriately, with the central recurrence Rₜ = (n₀/nₜ)R₀ + ((nₜ₋₁)/nₜ)(1-αₜ)²Rₜ₋₁ — providing direct theoretical support for dynamic mixing strategies. A Theoretical Perspective on Preventing Model Collapse in Self-consuming Training Loops (arXiv 2502.18865, ICLR 2025) introduces recursive stability theory and shows that a constant-sized proportion of real data ensures convergence, with both model architecture and data composition critically influencing STL success. ADAPT (arXiv 2512.04555, Dec 2025) uses meta-gradients to learn task sampling proportions under explicit token budgets for multi-task instruction tuning — demonstrating budget-constrained adaptive allocation in a curriculum learning setting, but not addressing synthetic-data collapse or spectral signals. Gerstgrasser et al. (2024) show data accumulation avoids collapse but treats the real/synthetic ratio as static. Curated Synthetic Data Doesn't Have to Collapse (arXiv 2605.07724, Falahati et al., May 2026) demonstrates pluralistic reward curation prevents collapse — a complementary mechanism to mixing-ratio control. Monitoring Neural Training with Topology (arXiv 2604.26984, Apr 2026) detects representational anisotropy via persistent homology but targets single-run fine-tuning. Spectral Lens (arXiv 2605.05683, May 2026) uses activation/gradient spectra for optimization diagnostics, not collapse prevention. RankMe (Garrido et al., ICML 2023) provides a well-established, hyperparameter-free, architecture-agnostic effective rank metric via spectral entropy of singular values — we use this as our primary fallback and complementary signal to SIGMA's Track I, with existing implementations readily available. Standardizing the Measurement of Text Diversity (ICLR 2025) recommends reporting self-BLEU alongside compression ratios and self-repetition for comprehensive diversity assessment, confirming self-BLEU as an essential diversity metric. The adaptive data mixing literature (Data Mixing Laws, DoReMi, arXiv 2603.14712) optimizes domain proportions using loss-based feedback but has not addressed synthetic-data collapse or spectral health signals. The specific gap: no existing work uses spectral health signals as a closed-loop feedback mechanism for budget-constrained adaptive global real/synthetic data mixing during recursive synthetic training.

## Proposed Approach

We present the first empirical study connecting spectral collapse monitoring to budget-constrained adaptive global real/synthetic data mixing in a closed-loop system. Targeting an EMNLP 2026 short paper, we adopt an aggressive but feasible scope. **Core Experimental Design — Budget-Fixed Redistribution**: The central comparison is allocation efficiency, not total real-data consumption. All conditions (spectral-guided, calibration-guided, fixed 50/50) consume exactly the same total real data budget: R_total = T × r_base = 5 × 0.50 = 2.50 real-data units across 5 generations. The static baseline allocates r_t = 0.50 at every generation. The adaptive conditions redistribute this same budget across generations based on monitoring signals — using less real data when spectral health is stable (early generations) and more when collapse pressure intensifies (later generations). This design ensures that any performance difference is attributable to allocation timing, not total real-data quantity, making the contribution maximally clear: given a fixed real-data budget, adaptive temporal allocation outperforms uniform allocation. **SIGMA Reimplementation Plan**: Since no public code repository exists for SIGMA (arXiv 2601.03385) as of May 2026, we will reimplement the Track I log-determinant bound from the paper's formulas (log det(Iₘ + Gₐ/(β̂+δ)) via Cholesky decomposition on shifted PSD matrices, with β̂ₖ = (nₖ - nₐ)·ρ). The math is standard linear algebra; we estimate 1-1.5 days of engineering. **SIGMA→Pythia Transfer Risk**: SIGMA was validated exclusively on OPT-125M with a frozen sentence encoder (m=384). While the sentence encoder approach is black-box (encoding generated text, not internal activations), the β̂ estimator's calibration (ρ = maximum embedding norm squared) may differ for Pythia-generated text, since output distribution scale and geometry can vary across architectures. We mitigate this with: (a) re-calibrating δ on the first generation's outputs, and (b) using effective rank (RankMe, Garrido et al. ICML 2023) as a primary fallback — RankMe is architecture-agnostic, hyperparameter-free, and has existing implementations, providing a robust alternative if Track I calibration proves problematic across architectures. **Controller Specification — Proportional Controller with Budget Constraint**: We specify the exact control law for reproducibility. Let S_t denote the spectral health signal (Track I log-determinant or effective rank) computed from the outputs of the model trained at generation t. The controller operates as follows: (1) Signal smoothing: S̃_t = α_ema · S_t + (1 - α_ema) · S̃_{t-1}, with α_ema = 0.5 (a 2-generation exponential moving average to reduce sensitivity to per-generation estimation noise; see Signal Noise discussion below). Initialize S̃_0 = S_0. (2) Normalized spectral decline rate: Δ_t = (S̃_{t-1} - S̃_t) / |S̃_0|, measuring the relative decline in spectral health normalized by the initial health level. (3) Target real-data fraction for generation t+1: r_{t+1}^raw = B_rem / (T - t) + k · max(0, Δ_t - τ), where B_rem is the remaining real-data budget, T is the total number of generations, k is the proportional gain, and τ is the decline-rate threshold below which no adjustment is made. The first term (B_rem / (T-t)) distributes the remaining budget uniformly over remaining generations; the second term adds a proportional boost when spectral decline exceeds the threshold. (4) Budget-constrained clipping: r_{t+1} = clip(r_{t+1}^raw, r_min, B_rem - (T - t - 1) · r_min), where r_min = 0.20 ensures every generation receives at least 20% real data. The upper clip ensures sufficient budget remains for future generations. (5) Budget update: B_rem ← B_rem - r_{t+1}. For the final generation: r_T = B_rem (all remaining budget). (6) Generation 1 uses r_1 = r_base = 0.50 (no prior signal available). Parameters k and τ are calibrated from Phase 1 data: τ is set at the inflection point where downstream metrics (perplexity, HellaSwag) begin degrading, and k is set such that the maximum single-generation allocation does not exceed r_max = 0.80. The calibration-guided controller uses the identical control law but substitutes ECE (Expected Calibration Error) for S_t, with τ and k re-calibrated for the ECE signal's scale. **Signal Noise Discussion**: SIGMA's Track I log-determinant is computed on finite samples, introducing estimation variance. Based on SIGMA's reported experiments (50 generations on OPT-125M with consistent monotonic contraction visible in their plots), the generation-over-generation signal appears smooth relative to the overall contraction trend, suggesting adequate signal-to-noise ratio at reasonable sample sizes. We compute the spectral signal on 10K generated samples per generation, providing substantial averaging. The 2-generation EMA (α_ema = 0.5) further smooths the signal, reducing the risk of controller oscillation from per-generation noise. During Phase 1, we explicitly measure the per-generation variance of the spectral signal across 3 seeds to verify that the signal-to-noise ratio supports threshold-based control; if variance is high relative to the threshold τ, we will increase α_ema (reduce responsiveness but increase stability) or increase the sample size. Phase 1 — Spectral and calibration contraction characterization (days 1-5): Run recursive training on Pythia 410M at 2 mixing ratios (0% synthetic = pure collapse, 50% synthetic) × 5 generations × 3 seeds = 30 generation-steps. At each generation checkpoint, compute: (a) SIGMA's Track I log-determinant bound (or effective rank fallback), (b) effective rank of the embedding Gram matrix as a complementary signal, (c) Expected Calibration Error (ECE) following arXiv 2510.05133 as a calibration-based monitoring baseline — this requires near-zero additional compute (just binning model predictions on the held-out set) and directly addresses whether spectral signals provide earlier or more fine-grained warning than simpler calibration tracking, (d) held-out perplexity, distinct-4, and self-BLEU (computed as average pairwise BLEU-4 over 1000 generated samples, following the Texygen benchmark protocol; lower self-BLEU indicates higher diversity) as downstream baselines, (e) HellaSwag accuracy (0-shot, via lm-evaluation-harness) as a downstream task quality metric — Pythia 410M achieves ~40.9% baseline (well above chance ~25%), providing meaningful dynamic range to detect collapse-induced degradation; HellaSwag evaluation adds minimal compute overhead (inference-only on ~10K examples per checkpoint). We explicitly omit MMLU: Pythia 410M scores ~27.3% (near-random for 4-choice at 25%), providing insufficient dynamic range for meaningful collapse detection at this scale; MMLU evaluation on larger models (2.8B+) is deferred to the AAAI 2027 extended version. Additionally, during Phase 1 we measure the per-generation variance of the spectral signal (Track I log-determinant and effective rank) across the 3 seeds to characterize signal-to-noise ratio and validate that the EMA-smoothed signal is sufficiently stable for threshold-based control. Run 1 mixing ratio (0% synthetic) × 5 generations × 1 seed on Pythia 1B to validate cross-scale transfer of spectral contraction patterns (~5 generation-steps). Total Phase 1: ~35 generation-steps, ~35 H100-hours (including SIGMA reimplementation time). Phase 2 — Budget-constrained adaptive mixing (days 5-9): Implement the proportional controller with budget constraint as specified above. Evaluate 4 conditions on Pythia 410M × 5 generations × 3 seeds = 60 generation-steps, all subject to the same total real-data budget (R_total = 2.50): (a) spectral-guided adaptive mixing (Track I log-determinant or effective rank signal, proportional controller with budget constraint), (b) calibration-guided adaptive mixing (ECE signal, same proportional controller and budget constraint but with ECE-calibrated τ and k — this isolates the value of spectral vs. calibration monitoring), (c) fixed 50/50 mixing (uniform allocation of the same total budget, near the 1/φ theoretical optimum), (d) no intervention (pure collapse trajectory, no real data — serves as collapse reference, not budget-matched). At each generation, evaluate held-out perplexity, distinct-4, self-BLEU, and HellaSwag accuracy. Additionally, run the spectral-guided adaptive controller on Pythia 1B × 5 generations × 1 seed = 5 generation-steps to validate that threshold calibration transfers across scales (not just spectral contraction patterns). Total Phase 2: ~65 generation-steps, ~65 H100-hours. **Statistical Power**: We use 3 seeds per condition to balance statistical reliability with compute budget. While the model collapse literature ranges from 3 seeds (e.g., 'Escaping Collapse: The Strength of Weak Data,' NeurIPS 2025) to 5 seeds (Shumailov et al., Nature 2024), 3 seeds represents a pragmatic minimum for a short paper. We adopt paired seeds across conditions (identical seeds for spectral-guided, calibration-guided, fixed 50/50, and no-intervention), following the Paired Seed Evaluation methodology (arXiv 2512.24145), which provably reduces variance and yields tighter confidence intervals when seed-level correlations are positive — as expected when the same initialization encounters different mixing strategies. We report mean ± standard deviation across seeds and apply paired t-tests for pairwise significance. We explicitly acknowledge that 3 seeds limits our power to detect small effect sizes (<2 perplexity points or <1.5% HellaSwag), and frame this as a limitation: if observed effects are small and not statistically significant at 3 seeds, we report the trend and effect size with confidence intervals, deferring conclusive claims to the extended version with 5+ seeds. **Calibration-Parity Contingency**: If the calibration-guided controller performs comparably to the spectral-guided controller in Phase 2, the paper's contribution remains

【主要风险】:

1. **Novelty 深度质疑（最高风险）**：核心技术组件是教科书级 P-controller + 已有的 SIGMA spectral signal，审稿人如果偏 methodological，可能判定为 "systems engineering integration without algorithmic advance"。2×2 taxonomy 和 problem formulation 重新定位只能改善 perception，无法改变底层技术创新量。这是本文的结构性天花板，取决于 reviewer panel 是偏 empirical（有利）还是偏 methodological（不利）。

2. **Layer 2 效应量可能过小**：closed-loop vs. best open-loop 的增量差异（本文最核心的 claim）可能仅 1-2 ppl，即使 5 seeds 也难以达到统计显著。如果 linear-increasing schedule 已经接近最优（因为"后期多用真实数据"这个 heuristic 本身就足够好），闭环反馈的边际价值将难以用 aggregate metrics 证明——此时需要靠 controller response dynamics 的定性分析和 trajectory-level consistent patterns 来补充论证。
