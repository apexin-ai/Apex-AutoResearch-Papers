# 3. Method

## 3.1 Pipeline Overview

ACR-VLA operates as a two-stage sample-then-correct pipeline applied at inference time on a frozen Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). In the first stage, conformal sample selection, we draw $K{=}10$ action chunks from the VLA's flow-matching distribution and rank them by a Mahalanobis-based nonconformity score computed from the sample set's empirical mean and covariance. The candidate with the lowest score (argmin) is selected as the initial action, representing the sample most consistent with the collective distribution. In the second stage, bounded residual correction, a lightweight Conformal Residual Module (CRM) predicts an additive residual $\Delta$ conditioned on the selected action and auxiliary features, and the final output is $\hat{a} = a_{\text{selected}} + \text{clip}(\Delta,\; \|\Delta\|_2 \leq \varepsilon)$, where $\varepsilon$ controls the maximum correction magnitude. The ordering of stages is deliberate: correction operates on an already high-quality candidate identified by conformal selection, rather than on a single uncurated VLA sample. This decomposition allows each stage to address a distinct failure mode: selection filters out outlier samples, while correction compensates for systematic biases shared across all candidates. The CRM is trained offline via supervised learning on pairs of (observation, selected action), with the residual target defined as $\Delta^* = a_{\text{expert}} - a_{\text{selected}}$, minimizing mean squared error. At deployment, the frozen VLA, conformal selector, and CRM compose a modular pipeline that requires no retraining or architectural modification of the base policy.

## 3.2 CRM Architecture

The CRM is a 3-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) with hidden dimension 256, mapping from the input feature vector to a 30-dimensional action residual: input $\rightarrow$ 256 $\rightarrow$ 256 $\rightarrow$ 30. Input features are normalized before the first layer to stabilize training across heterogeneous feature scales. The total parameter count is approximately 330K (varying slightly by conditioning variant: obs\_only $\sim$330K, perdof $\sim$340K, scalar $\sim$330K), making the module orders of magnitude smaller than the frozen VLA backbone. Inference overhead is approximately 3ms per step, negligible relative to the VLA forward pass and the $K{=}10$ sample generation cost. The VLA encoder and policy weights remain entirely frozen; the CRM is the only trainable component and is trained independently on offline demonstration data. This design choice is intentional: a minimal correction head limits overfitting risk and ensures that the CRM cannot override the base policy's learned behavior beyond what the magnitude bound $\varepsilon$ permits.

## 3.3 Conditioning Strategies

A central question in residual correction design is what information the CRM should receive as input. We systematically compare three conditioning strategies that span a spectrum from pure observation features to fine-grained conformal statistics (Table~\ref{tab:conditioning}).

The first variant, **obs\_only**, concatenates the 960-dimensional observation embedding extracted from the frozen VLA encoder with the 30-dimensional selected action, yielding a 990-dimensional CRM input. This variant receives no explicit uncertainty or conformal signal; the correction direction is determined entirely by the observation context and the selected action itself. The observation embedding, produced by the VLA's pretrained vision-language encoder, provides a compressed summary of the current scene state.

The second variant, **perdof** (per degree of freedom), augments the obs\_only input with a 30-dimensional vector of per-dimension nonconformity scores derived from the Mahalanobis distance decomposition across the $K{=}10$ samples. Each component of this vector quantifies how atypical the selected action is along a single action dimension relative to the sample distribution. The intuition is that dimensions with high nonconformity scores should receive larger corrections. However, estimating a reliable 30-dimensional covariance matrix from only $K{=}10$ samples is statistically ill-conditioned, and Section~3.5 discusses how this noise propagates into correction failures.

The third variant, **scalar**, replaces the per-dimension scores with a single scalar nonconformity score (the full Mahalanobis distance) appended to the observation embedding and selected action. This provides a global uncertainty signal without any directional information about which action dimensions are unreliable. As a result, the CRM must infer correction direction solely from observation context, with the scalar score serving only as a confidence modulator. Experiments in Section~4 reveal that obs\_only consistently outperforms both perdof and scalar, establishing that the observation embedding alone provides sufficient, and more robust, conditioning for residual correction.

## 3.4 Magnitude Control

The correction norm bound $\varepsilon$ determines the maximum $L_2$ norm of the residual applied to the selected action. Before the CRM output $\Delta$ is added to $a_{\text{selected}}$, it is clipped: $\hat{\Delta} = \Delta \cdot \min(1, \;\varepsilon / \|\Delta\|_2)$. This single hyperparameter governs the "aggressiveness" of the correction and spans a wide behavioral range. Conservative values ($\varepsilon = 0.03$--$0.05$) restrict the CRM to near-identity refinements that barely perturb the selected action. Moderate values ($\varepsilon = 0.1$--$0.2$) allow meaningful corrections and represent the optimal operating range for obs\_only, which achieves its peak performance at $\varepsilon{=}0.2$ (Table~\ref{tab:magnitude}). Aggressive values ($\varepsilon = 0.3$+) grant the CRM substantial authority to reshape the action, while the uncapped default ($\varepsilon \approx 1.42$, the maximum observed residual norm in training data) imposes no effective constraint.

Magnitude control is critical because the CRM is trained with MSE loss on offline data, but executes in a closed-loop setting where errors compound across timesteps. A correction that reduces single-step MSE may introduce systematic drift that accumulates over an episode, and without a norm bound, even a well-trained CRM can destabilize execution. The bound $\varepsilon$ functions as a safety mechanism: it caps the maximum per-step deviation from the conformal-selected action, ensuring that even if the CRM has learned incorrect correction patterns on out-of-distribution inputs, the damage per step remains bounded. Our magnitude sweep (Figure~\ref{fig:magnitude_curve}) reveals that this sensitivity is not merely theoretical. With perdof conditioning, performance drops from 14\% at $\varepsilon{=}0.03$ to 0\% at $\varepsilon{=}1.42$, a complete collapse. In contrast, obs\_only maintains $\sim$16--17\% across the full range, including the uncapped setting, demonstrating that the interaction between conditioning features and magnitude control is the primary determinant of correction safety.

## 3.5 Why Per-DOF Conditioning Fails at High Magnitude

The per-DOF nonconformity scores are computed from the empirical covariance of $K{=}10$ action samples in a 30-dimensional space. With only 10 samples, the sample covariance matrix is rank-deficient (rank $\leq$ 9 for 30 dimensions), and the per-dimension Mahalanobis components extracted from it carry substantial estimation noise. During CRM training, this noise is present but tolerable: the MSE objective can partially learn to ignore unreliable score dimensions when sufficient training data provides averaging. The problem emerges at deployment, where the per-DOF score distribution shifts relative to training due to different observation contexts, VLA sampling stochasticity, and environment variation.

At low $\varepsilon$, the norm bound masks this problem. Even if the CRM interprets noisy per-DOF scores as confident directional signals and produces a correction pointing in a suboptimal direction, the clipping operation limits the step size to a small neighborhood around the selected action. The resulting corrections are too small to cause harm. At high $\varepsilon$, the mask is removed. The CRM applies large corrections along directions dictated by noisy per-DOF features, and because these features do not reliably indicate which dimensions need correction at test time, the large-magnitude adjustments push the action away from the viable region. The magnitude sweep confirms this mechanism: perdof performance degrades monotonically from $\varepsilon{=}0.03$ to $\varepsilon{=}1.42$, with catastrophic collapse (0\% success) at the uncapped setting (Figure~\ref{fig:magnitude_curve}). The obs\_only variant avoids this failure because the 960-dimensional observation embedding is a stable, low-noise representation produced by the VLA's pretrained encoder, and its information content does not depend on the stochastic properties of a small sample set.
