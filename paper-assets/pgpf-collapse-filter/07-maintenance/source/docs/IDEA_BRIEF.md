## Core Problem

Existing per-sample data selection methods (EUDS, QuaDMix, D3, OptimSyn) optimize for single-round training quality using generic quality and diversity metrics, but they are never evaluated across recursive training generations where distribution shift accumulates and causes model collapse. Confidence-based filtering (Self-Verification, NeurIPS 2025) addresses the recursive setting but uses a single generic signal — model confidence — which is a composite scalar that conflates lexical familiarity, semantic coherence, and quality, destroying the ability to selectively control individual collapse dimensions. Entropy-based selection (Shi et al., NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight) demonstrates that entropy-driven filtering can mitigate collapse in diffusion models, and Latent Space Filtering (Cai et al., AAAI 2026) validates per-sample geometric scoring in self-consuming diffusion loops, but both operate in the vision domain with generic signals rather than collapse-specific textual properties. Kovac et al. (EMNLP 2025) identified specific per-sample data properties that modulate collapse in LLMs — lexical diversity amplifies distribution shift while semantic diversity and quality mitigate it — but only as a diagnostic finding, not as an operational filtering criterion. Can per-sample filtering guided by these collapse-relevant properties, with thresholds learned per generation to track distribution shift, yield better multi-generation stability than both generic confidence-based selection and static quality/diversity-based selection? And which individual property dimensions contribute most to collapse prevention, and how does their relative importance evolve across generations?

## Opportunity / Why Now

The gap is narrow and precisely positioned between bodies of work that do not connect. (1) Kovac et al. (EMNLP 2025) ran 20-generation recursive training chains on 4 base models (1-2B parameters) and identified which per-sample properties predict collapse via regression analysis — lexical diversity (Self-BLEU) amplifies distribution shift, semantic diversity and quality mitigate it — but proposed no filtering algorithm. (2) Fu et al. (Self-Verification, NeurIPS 2025) introduced per-sample confidence-based filtering with theoretical guarantees showing it prevents collapse in recursive synthetic training, but uses a single generic signal (model confidence) without testing property-specific criteria or decomposing which data properties drive collapse prevention. Crucially, confidence is a composite scalar that cannot distinguish between lexically divergent high-confidence samples and lexically conforming high-confidence samples — making it a strictly less informative selection criterion when collapse is driven by specific property dimensions. (3) Shi et al. (NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight) demonstrated that entropy-based data selection mitigates collapse in diffusion models by maintaining generation diversity — validating the diagnostic-to-operational bridge pattern — but operates in vision with a generic entropy signal, not LLM-specific collapse properties. (4) Cai et al. (Latent Space Filtering, AAAI 2026) applies per-sample geometric scoring in self-consuming diffusion loops to filter out degraded synthetic samples, validating the per-sample filtering principle alongside Shi et al.'s entropy approach, but again in the vision domain. (5) DCES (Electronics 2026) uses adaptive clustering-based sampling with real-time intra-cluster dispersion monitoring to dynamically adjust data selection during iterative self-training — demonstrating the adaptive feedback principle — but uses a different mechanism (clustering) in a semi-supervised setting without property-specific collapse criteria. (6) ForTIFAI (arXiv 2509.08972) proposes per-token Truncated Cross-Entropy loss that ignores high-confidence tokens during training, achieving 2.3x more synthetic data tolerance before collapse — a complementary approach operating at training-objective granularity rather than data-selection level. (7) Single-round per-sample selection methods — EUDS, QuaDMix, OptimSyn, D3 — all optimize for single-round quality without recursive evaluation. (8) Bilevel optimization methods for data selection (ScaleBiO, ACL 2025) optimize data weights for single-round training but have not been applied to recursive generation settings where optimal selection changes per generation. The diagnostic-to-operational gap remains for LLMs: no method connects Kovac et al.'s LLM-specific collapse property measurements to an operational per-sample filter with learned per-generation thresholds, evaluates it across recursive generations, and compares property-specific filtering against confidence-based filtering.

## Landscape (SOTA & Limitations)

Model collapse in recursive synthetic training is well-established (Shumailov et al., Nature 2024; Dohmatob et al., ICLR 2025). Mitigation strategies span several categories:

**Corpus-level strategies**: Data accumulation (Gerstgrasser et al., ICLR 2025), pluralistic curation (Falahati et al., arXiv 2605.07724), multi-source generation (Cegin et al., ACL 2026 Findings), corpus-level mixing ratios (Li et al., EMNLP 2025 — ~33% rephrased synthetic as optimal), and self-correcting loops via expert corrector functions (Gillman et al., ICML 2024).

**Training-objective modifications**: ForTIFAI (arXiv 2509.08972) proposes Truncated Cross-Entropy (TCE) loss that sets gradient to zero for tokens with predicted probability above a threshold (γ=0.9), preventing overfitting to high-confidence synthetic artifacts and achieving 2.3x greater synthetic data tolerance before collapse onset. This is a complementary approach operating at per-token training-objective level rather than per-sample data-selection level; combining TCE with data filtering remains unexplored.

**Verification-based per-sample filtering for recursive training**: (1) Fu et al. (Self-Verification, NeurIPS 2025) use model confidence as the sole per-sample criterion, providing theoretical finite-sample error bounds — but use a single generic signal without decomposing which properties matter; (2) 'Escaping Model Collapse' (arXiv 2510.16657) uses binary verifiers for synthetic data filtering across iterations but shows long-term convergence to the verifier's bias; (3) 'Beyond Model Collapse' (ICLR 2025) shows imperfect verifiers can prevent collapse, deriving measurable proxies for verifier effectiveness.

**Entropy, latent-space, and property-based collapse mitigation**: (1) Shi et al. (NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight, arXiv 2509.16499) identify a generalization-to-memorization transition in diffusion models driven by declining entropy of synthetic training data, and propose entropy-based data selection to maintain generation diversity — same diagnostic-to-filter bridge as PGPF but in vision domain with generic entropy rather than LLM-specific collapse properties; (2) Latent Space Filtering (Cai et al., AAAI 2026, arXiv 2511.12742) applies per-sample geometric scoring in self-consuming diffusion loops to filter degraded synthetic data based on latent representation quality degradation across generations — validates the per-sample filtering principle in vision alongside Shi et al.'s entropy-based approach; (3) DCES (Electronics 2026) proposes adaptive clustering-based center-edge sampling for iterative self-training, monitoring intra-cluster dispersion via EMA and dynamically adjusting sampling ratios — shares the adaptive feedback principle but uses clustering-based mechanism in a semi-supervised setting rather than property-specific thresholds in self-consumption.

**Single-round per-sample selection**: (1) EUDS uses three entropy metrics for interval-based data selection, evaluated only on BERT-base for single-round fine-tuning; (2) QuaDMix balances per-sample quality and domain diversity via parameterized sampling; (3) OptimSyn (ICLR 2026) optimizes synthetic data generation rubrics using influence scores; (4) D3 (IJCAI 2025) scores samples by diversity, difficulty, and dependability; (5) ResoFilter (NAACL 2025 Findings) uses data-parameter resonance; (6) IFD (NAACL 2024) scores per-sample instruction-following difficulty.

**Bilevel optimization for data selection**: ScaleBiO (ACL 2025) applies bilevel optimization to LLM data reweighting at scale, optimizing sample weights via outer-loop validation loss. This establishes the principle of learning data selection parameters against a validation objective, but operates in single-round pretraining, not recursive generation where optimal selection changes per generation.

**Diagnostic findings on collapse properties**: Kovac et al. (EMNLP 2025) identified through regression analysis over 20 generations on 4 base models (LLaMA-3.2-1B, Qwen2.5-1.5B, SmolLM-1.7B, Falcon3-1B-Base) that per-sample lexical diversity (Self-BLEU) amplifies distribution shift while semantic diversity and quality mitigate it — but only as diagnostics, not as filtering criteria.

**Theoretical frameworks**: 'From Collapse to Improvement' (arXiv 2602.10531) derives exact trajectories of recursive training evolution; Fu et al. (ICLR 2025) introduce recursive stability theory for self-consuming training loops.

Critically, no existing method: (a) uses Kovac et al.'s LLM-specific collapse property measurements as operational per-sample filtering criteria with generation-adaptive learned thresholds, (b) compares property-specific filtering against confidence-based filtering across recursive generations in LLMs, or (c) decomposes the marginal contribution of individual collapse-relevant properties and tracks how their relative importance evolves across generations.

## Proposed Approach

We propose Property-Guided Per-Sample Filtering (PGPF), which operationalizes Kovac et al.'s diagnostic findings into a filtering criterion for recursive synthetic training, and tests whether collapse-specific properties with learned per-generation thresholds provide better selection criteria than generic confidence-based or quality/diversity-based metrics across recursive generations. We evaluate two variants — static thresholds (PGPF-S) and learned-adaptive thresholds (PGPF-L) — across 5 recursive generations.

**Research Question.** We investigate which per-sample selection criteria provide the best collapse prevention across recursive generations, and whether the optimal selection changes across generations as distribution shift accumulates. Our ablations decompose the contribution of each property dimension and track how their relative importance evolves. Both possible outcomes are informative: if a single property (e.g., lexical conformity) suffices across all generations, this identifies the causal bottleneck in collapse and provides a simple practical recipe; if the optimal property weighting shifts across generations, this reveals that collapse is a multi-phase phenomenon requiring adaptive selection that generic signals like confidence cannot capture.

**Theoretical Motivation: Why Property-Specific Filtering Outperforms Confidence.** Model confidence (token-level log-probability) is a composite scalar that aggregates information about lexical familiarity, semantic coherence, and fluency into a single value. This aggregation destroys the ability to selectively control individual collapse dimensions. A sample may have high model confidence (familiar topic, fluent text) yet low lexical conformity (unusual n-gram patterns that happen to be probable under the model's distribution). Confidence-based filtering (Self-Verification) would retain this sample; property-specific filtering can flag it. If collapse is primarily driven by lexical divergence (as Kovac et al.'s regression analysis suggests, with Self-BLEU change being the strongest predictor of distribution shift magnitude), then the optimal filter should directly target lexical conformity rather than rely on confidence as a noisy, conflated proxy. Property-specific filtering provides orthogonal selection criteria that capture distinct collapse mechanisms, enabling selective control: tightening lexical conformity without sacrificing semantic diversity, or vice versa. Confidence filtering operates on a one-dimensional projection that cannot make such distinctions.

**Per-Sample Lexical Conformity Metric.** For each synthetic sample s, we compute its average BLEU-4 score against a random subset of k=50 samples drawn from the current training corpus: LC(s) = (1/k) Σᵢ BLEU-4(s, rᵢ). This per-sample score measures how lexically similar s is to the existing corpus. We filter out samples with low LC(s) — those lexically divergent from the corpus — because such samples increase corpus-level Self-BLEU change (intra-corpus dissimilarity), which Kovac et al.'s regression analysis showed predicts collapse magnitude.

**Property Scoring.** For each synthetic sample, we compute three per-sample scores: (1) Lexical conformity LC(s) — as defined above; (2) Semantic novelty — cosine distance of the sample's embedding (using a frozen stella_en_1.5B_v5 encoder, matching Kovac et al.'s embedding pipeline) from the running corpus centroid in embedding space; (3) Quality — perplexity under the base model as a lightweight quality proxy.

**PGPF-S (Static).** Apply fixed percentile-based thresholds across all generations: remove samples below a lexical conformity threshold (filtering out lexically divergent samples), retain samples above semantic novelty and quality thresholds. Threshold selection uses a grid search (2 percentile levels per dimension = 8 configurations) evaluated on a held-out validation split via 500-step proxy training at generation 1.

**PGPF-L (Learned-Adaptive).** At each generation g, we optimize the three property thresholds (τ_LC, τ_SN, τ_Q) to directly minimize held-out validation perplexity using Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) Bayesian optimization. Each generation runs 15 TPE evaluations: each applies a candidate threshold configuration to filter the synthetic data, mixes the filtered synthetic data with real data, trains a proxy model for 500 LoRA steps with the same hyperparameters, and measures validation perplexity on a 2K held-out set from the original real data. The optimized thresholds are then used for the full training run at that generation. This design makes PGPF-L an instance of bilevel optimization for data selection in the recursive setting: the outer objective (validation perplexity) is optimized over data selection parameters (thresholds) while the inner objective (training loss) defines the model trained on the selected data. Unlike single-round bilevel data selection (ScaleBiO, ACL 2025), PGPF-L re-solves the outer problem at each generation, capturing how the optimal selection shifts as distributions evolve.

**Why PGPF-L's Threshold Trajectories Are Informative.** The sequence of learned thresholds (τ_LC^g, τ_SN^g, τ_Q^g) across generations g=1,...,5 reveals the evolving importance of each property dimension. For instance, if τ_LC tightens monotonically (admitting only more conforming samples) while τ_SN relaxes (admitting more semantically diverse samples), this indicates that lexical divergence becomes increasingly dangerous while semantic diversity becomes increasingly valuable as recursive training progresses — directly validating and extending Kovac et al.'s static regression analysis to a dynamic setting.

**Recursive Training Protocol.** Using Qwen2.5-1.5B as the primary base model, we adopt Kovac et al.'s LoRA configuration (rank 16, alpha 16, batch size 16, learning rate 2e-4, linear schedule with 5 warmup steps) and stella_en_1.5B_v5 encoder for metric consistency. We train for 3 epochs per generation, matching the default Unsloth SFTTrainer configuration used by Kovac et al. We use larger sample sizes (~20K generated per generation vs. Kovac et al.'s 4K) and fewer generations (5 vs. 20) to test PGPF's filtering under more data-rich conditions representative of practical synthetic data pipelines. The self-consumption loop: (1) Fine-tune base model on domain data with LoRA; (2) Generate ~20K synthetic samples from the fine-tuned model; (3) Score and filter synthetic samples; (4) Mix filtered synthetic with real data; (5) Fine-tune a fresh base model on the mixture → next generation. We run across 2 domains: general text (C4 subset) and math (GSM8K/MATH training data).

**Cross-Model Validation.** To test generalizability beyond Qwen2.5-1.5B, we run a spot-check experiment with LLaMA-3.2-1B (another model from Kovac et al.'s study with different tokenizer and pretraining distribution) on the general text domain across 5 generations, comparing PGPF-L against Self-Verification.

**Mixing Ratios.** Primary experiments use 50% synthetic / 50% real. An additional ablation uses 33% synthetic / 67% real (motivated by Li et al., EMNLP 2025) on 1 domain (general text) for PGPF-S, PGPF-L, and unfiltered baselines, testing robustness across realistic mixing ratios.

**Baselines (4).** (1) Unfiltered: all synthetic data mixed without filtering; (2) Quality-only: filter by perplexity threshold only; (3) EUDS-adapted: apply EUDS's entropy-based interval selection adapted to our generative setting; (4) Self-Verification: per-sample confidence-based filtering using the generating model's own token-level log-probabilities as confidence scores, implementing the approach from Fu et al. (NeurIPS 2025) — this is the primary comparison testing whether property-specific filtering outperforms confidence-based filtering.

**Ablations.** (1) Lexical-only: filter by lexical conformity threshold alone; (2) Semantic-only: filter by semantic novelty alone. These decompose which property dimension drives collapse prevention. The quality-only variant is already captured by baseline (2).

**Evaluation.** Per-generation metrics across all 5 generations: (a) distribution shift — corpus-level Self-BLEU, embedding diversity (average pairwise cosine distance), and KL divergence from original data distribution; (b) downstream performance — perplexity on held-out test sets, MMLU subset accuracy (general), GSM8K accuracy (math); (c) collapse indicators — vocabulary coverage, tail-distribution mass (fraction of probability assigned to tokens below rank-1000), generation diversity of the trained model; (d) threshold evolution — for PGPF-L, we report the learned thresholds (τ_LC, τ_SN, τ_Q) at each generation, visualizing their trajectories to reveal the evolving relative importance of each property dimension.

**Limitations.** We evaluate 5 generations, which tests collapse prevention in the early-to-mid recursive regime. Long-term behavior (>10 generations) as studied by Kovac et al. (20 generations) remains an open question — PGPF-L's per-generation optimization is designed for sustained stability but its effectiveness beyond generation 10 requires future verification. Additionally, our 2-domain evaluation does not cover the full breadth of Kovac et al.'s 5-dataset, 3-domain study. We note that ForTIFAI's per-token TCE loss is a complementary mechanism that could potentially be combined with PGPF's per-sample filtering, but evaluating this combination is beyond our current scope.

**Why This Differs from Existing Work.** Unlike Self-Verification (NeurIPS 2025), which uses model confidence as the sole criterion, PGPF uses collapse-specific properties derived from Kovac et al.'s regression analysis with thresholds learned per generation, and decomposes the contribution of each property dimension — showing how their relative importance evolves. Unlike Shi et al. (NeurIPS 2025 Spotlight) and Latent Space Filtering (Cai et al., AAAI 2026), who apply generic filtering signals to diffusion models, PGPF uses LLM-specific collapse properties in a text self-consumption setting. Unlike DCES (Electronics 2026), which uses clustering-based dispersion monitoring in semi-supervised self-training, PGPF-L uses validation-loss-optimized property-specific thresholds with interpretable connections to known collapse mechanisms. Unlike ScaleBiO (ACL 2025), which applies bilevel data optimization in single-round pretraining, PGPF-L re-optimizes selection per generation in a recursive setting. Unlike EUDS/QuaDMix/D3/OptimSyn, PGPF evaluates across 5 recursive generations. Unlike Kovac et al., PGPF operationalizes their diagnostic findings into a learned filter.

【主要风险】:

1. **Novelty 天花板**：即使做了所有上述优化，核心概念跳跃仍然有限——从 Kovac et al. 明确指出的三个预测性属性到将它们用作过滤阈值，审稿人可能仍评价为 "expected next step rather than surprising insight"。这是结构性约束，无法通过打磨消除。最佳缓解策略是让阈值轨迹分析产出真正 surprising 的发现（如明确的相变结构、反直觉的跨域差异），将审稿人注意力从方法的 straightforwardness 转移到发现的 insightfulness 上。

2. **实验结果风险**：(a) 如果 PGPF 相对 oracle single-scalar baseline 优势不显著，"property-specific 不可约简" 的核心 claim 崩塌；(b) 如果三属性高度正相关（r > 0.8），多维度独立阈值的必要性被严重削弱；(c) 如果 5 代内 collapse 信号过弱导致所有方法差异不显著，整篇论文的实验支撑力不足。这些是 empirical contribution 型论文固有的结果依赖风险。

【实施评估】:

- **计算资源**: 核心实验需 2×模型（1B, 1.5B）× 2×域 × 7×方法（含 No Filter）× 5 代 = 140 次 LoRA 训练（~20K 样本，rank 16，每次约 20-40 分钟/单 A100）。Extended 10-代 case study 增加约 70 次训练。PGPF-L 的 TPE 搜索每代 50 trials × proxy 训练（1 epoch，约 5-10 分钟/trial）≈ 每代 4-8 GPU-hours。总计估算 **200-300 A100 GPU-hours**，单卡约 2-3 周，4 卡并行约 4-5 天。stella embedding 编码 20K 样本约 10-15 分钟/次，BLEU-4 计算（20K×50）约 30-60 分钟/次，均为 CPU-bound 可并行。
- **数据需求**: OpenHermes-2.5（公开）、MetaMathQA（公开）、GSM8K 测试集（公开）。无需私有数据或额外标注。
- **人工介入**: 不需要人工标注或专家评审。实验全自动化。需要研究人员投入时间设计 ablation、调试 pipeline、分析结果和撰写论文。
- **训练需求**: 需要模型训练（LoRA fine-tuning），但模型规模小（1-1.5B），训练轻量。不需要从头预训练。PGPF-L 需要额外的 proxy model 训练（同架构、fewer epochs）。
- **新 Benchmark**: 不需要构建新 benchmark。使用现有数据集和标准评测指标。需要构建递归自消费的实验 pipeline（生成→过滤→训练→再生成的循环），但这是实验基础设施而非 benchmark。
- **预估周期**: Pipeline 搭建和调试 2-3 周；主实验运行 1-2 周（取决于 GPU 可用性）；extended case study 1 周；分析和可视化 1-2 周；论文撰写 2-3 周。**总计约 8-12 周**（从启动到可投稿稿件）。关键路径是 PGPF-L 的 TPE 搜索计算时间和多代递归实验的串行依赖（每代必须等前一代完成）。
