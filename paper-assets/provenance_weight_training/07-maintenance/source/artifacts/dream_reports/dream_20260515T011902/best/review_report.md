### R2 Review: Novelty and Related Work

**Summary:** The paper proposes replacing the fixed exponent b in the detect-and-reweight formula w=(1-q)^b (Drayson et al., EMNLP 2025) with a per-batch adaptive b* derived by maximizing an "effective tail index" α_eff(b) subject to an ESS constraint. The core contribution is narrow but real: automating b selection eliminates grid search. However, the theoretical framework is underdeveloped, the novelty is incremental over Drayson, the related work omits critical connections to importance sampling literature, and no head-to-head comparison is run against the most relevant baselines (DoReMi, DSIR, Golden Ratio Weighting).

**Strengths:**
- S1: The adaptive b* idea addresses a genuine practical annoyance. Drayson requires per-model validation-based tuning (b=10 for small models, b=1 for 1.7B), and eliminating this via automatic selection is useful. The "zero tuning cost" framing is legitimate modulo n_min.
- S2: The b* distribution analysis (Table 5) is the paper's most compelling contribution. Showing that b* shifts rightward with contamination rate (median 0.87 at rho=0.4 vs 1.42 at rho=0.6), remains invariant to model scale (0.87 vs 0.88), and decreases with better detectors (0.45 for ModernBERT) provides genuine insight into the interaction between detector quality, contamination severity, and optimal weighting. This is the strongest evidence that alpha_eff captures meaningful structure.
- S3: The lightweight detector sufficiency finding (TF-IDF+LR at AUC=0.939 achieves 77% of ModernBERT's reweighting value) is practically useful and well-demonstrated. The observation that b* adapts to detector quality (lower b* for more precise detector) is elegant.
- S4: The experimental coverage is reasonably thorough for a workshop paper: two model scales, two contamination rates, cross-generator, domain-skewed, detector ablation, and n_min sensitivity. The 3-seed protocol with explicit reporting of per-seed results is commendable.

**Weaknesses:**
- W1: **Incremental novelty over Drayson et al.** The base formula, the detect-and-reweight paradigm, the weighting function, and the overall framework are all from Drayson. The paper's contribution reduces to: (a) replace grid search over b with a 1D constrained optimization, and (b) do loss reweighting instead of SIR. Contribution (b) is a standard implementation choice, not a research contribution. Contribution (a), while useful, is a hyperparameter automation technique applied to a single scalar — this is thin for a top venue full paper.

- W2: **Theoretical framework is superficial.** The paper claims a "theoretical framework" (Contribution 1), but the actual theoretical content is minimal. α_eff(b) is defined as a weighted mean of (1-q_i), and b* is found by grid search over 1000 points + bisection refinement (line 102-137 of alpha_eff.py). The connection to the "convergence bound d(P_hat_t, P_0) ≲ t^{-min{p,α}}" from the cited "Convergence Theory 2025" (arXiv 2602.16065) is purely motivational — there is no theorem proving that maximizing α_eff(b) optimizes the convergence rate. The paper_framing.md itself acknowledges this internally: the convergence theory is "motivation not contribution" and "α_eff(b) is testable sufficient condition not theorem." Yet the paper presents this as a "principled derivation" and "theoretical framework." The ESS constraint is textbook importance sampling (Kish's effective sample size from the 1960s) applied without novel modification. Algorithm 1 is 4 lines. The concentration bound in the code (compute_concentration_bound) is not even mentioned in the paper. This is not a theoretical contribution — it is an engineering heuristic motivated by theory.

- W3: **The α_eff(b) definition is poorly justified.** α_eff(b) = Σ(1-q_i)^{b+1} / Σ(1-q_i)^b is simply a weighted average of (1-q_i) with weights (1-q_i)^b. This is monotonically non-decreasing in b (as stated in the code). Maximizing it subject to ESS >= n_min will always push b to the ESS boundary. So the actual algorithm is: "increase b until ESS = n_min." The α_eff framing adds conceptual overhead without adding discriminating power — any monotone function of b would produce the same result under an ESS constraint. The paper does not demonstrate that α_eff specifically (vs. any other monotone objective) yields better b* values.

- W4: **Missing baselines are a critical gap.** The paper cites DoReMi, DSIR, Golden Ratio Weighting, and Dynamic Loss-Based Reweighting in Related Work but compares against none of them experimentally. The actual baselines (Table 8) are: uniform, random reweight, detect-and-remove, and grid-best. Random reweighting and detect-and-remove are straw men. The meaningful comparison — adaptive b* vs. DoReMi, DSIR, or even a simple "set b to the ESS-constrained maximum" without the α_eff machinery — is absent. Golden Ratio Weighting is dismissed as requiring known k, but at rho=0.4 the contamination ratio is known in the experimental setup, so a comparison is feasible and informative.

- W5: **ESS literature connection is unacknowledged.** The ESS constraint ESS(b) = (Σw_i)²/Σw_i² is Kish's effective sample size (1965), a foundational concept in survey sampling and importance sampling. The paper treats it as if it were a novel component of the method without citing or discussing the extensive literature on ESS-based diagnostics in importance sampling (Owen 2013, Vehtari et al. 2024 PSIS — the latter appears in the project's internal literature map but is not cited in the paper). The connection between this work and importance sampling / self-normalized importance weighting is completely absent from the Related Work, despite the method being a direct instance of importance-weighted empirical risk minimization with ESS regularization.

- W6: **"Zero tuning cost" is overclaimed.** n_min is a hyperparameter that required tuning (n_min=3 caused seed42 to fail completely; n_min=2 fixed it — see the paper's own n_min ablation). The ablation (Table 6) shows n_min=1 and n_min=2 give different results (17.668 vs 17.650). tau is another implicit hyperparameter set to 0.8. b_max=20.0 and resolution=1000 are algorithmic choices that could affect results. Claiming "zero tuning cost" while having a hyperparameter that caused complete method failure at one setting is misleading.

- W7: **Effect sizes are very small and statistical significance is unclear.** The core result at rho=0.4 is 0.04 PPL improvement (0.23% relative) on 0.6B. The aggregate ratio is 88.6% of grid-best, meaning adaptive captures less than 90% of the improvement from a simple grid search. The paper points to amplification at higher rho and larger models, but these "amplification" results (Tables 3, 4) are single-seed only — no error bars, no significance tests. The "flat plateau" finding (Section 5.1, 0.014 PPL range over b=0.1-0.5) arguably undermines the paper: if any b in [0.1, 0.5] works about equally well, the value of a sophisticated adaptive method over simply setting b=0.3 is questionable.

- W8: **The positioning vs. Drayson is strained.** The paper frames itself as solving a different problem (one-pass web pretraining vs. recursive training). But the method is mechanically identical: same formula, same detector-based q scores, just different b selection. The "loss reweighting vs SIR" distinction (Section 3.4) is a 3-sentence paragraph presenting a standard technique as a contribution. The claim that adaptive b* is a "data property, not a model property" (Section 5.4) is interesting but only supported by one cross-model comparison (two models, one seed).

**Questions for Authors:**
- Q1: Since α_eff(b) is monotonically non-decreasing and ESS(b) is monotonically non-increasing, the optimization always saturates at the ESS boundary. Have you verified that a simpler rule — "find the largest b such that ESS(b) >= n_min" — produces equivalent b* values? If so, the α_eff framework is unnecessary machinery.
- Q2: The "Convergence Theory 2025" (arXiv 2602.16065) is cited as motivation but the reference is "Anonymous." Is this a real published work? The arXiv ID format 2602.xxxxx would correspond to February 2026. Can you provide the actual reference?
- Q3: Why not compare against Golden Ratio Weighting in the controlled setting where rho is known? This would clarify whether adaptive b* offers advantages beyond the unknown-proportion scenario.
- Q4: The paper reports b* median ~0.87 at rho=0.4. The grid-best is b=0.5. If the adaptive method systematically overshoots the grid optimum, what evidence is there that the α_eff objective is selecting the right b rather than just a reasonable one constrained by ESS?
- Q5: How sensitive are results to the batch size? With batch_size=4 and n_min=2, the ESS can only range over [2, ~3.9]. Does the method behave differently with batch_size=32 or 64, which are more typical for pretraining?
- Q6: The concentration bound function exists in the code but is not discussed in the paper. Was this a deliberate omission, and if included, would it strengthen the theoretical contribution?

**Minor Issues:**
- The bibliography has incorrect formatting: Drayson is cited as "Drayson, N." but the actual first author is George Drayson. The title in the bib entry ("Detect and remove") does not match the actual paper title ("Machine-Generated Text Detection Prevents Language Model Collapse").
- "Convergence Theory 2025" and "Golden Ratio 2025" are cited as "Anonymous" — proper attribution is needed.
- The Dynamic Loss reference cites Mindermann et al. 2024 ("Prioritized training...") which is a different paper from the Dynamic Loss-Based Reweighting (Sow et al. 2025) discussed in the literature map. The Related Work conflates two distinct works.
- Table 2 reports seed456 ratio as 80.0%, but internal records indicate 72.7%. The numbers should be reconciled.
- The paper claims effective batch size 16 (micro-batch 4 x gradient accumulation 4), but adaptive b* operates on micro-batches of 4. The ESS constraint operates on batches of 4, not 16. This should be clarified as it affects the interpretation of n_min.

**Score:** 3/6
**Confidence:** 4/5
**Justification:** The paper addresses a real practical problem (automating b selection in detect-and-reweight) but the contribution is too incremental for a top venue. The "theoretical framework" claim is not supported — α_eff is a monotone function that always pushes b to the ESS boundary, making the α_eff machinery potentially unnecessary (Q1 is the critical question). The absence of head-to-head comparisons with DoReMi, DSIR, and Golden Ratio Weighting is a significant experimental gap. The very small effect sizes (0.04 PPL at the primary setting), combined with the flat b-plateau showing that naive fixed b=0.3 works almost as well, undermine the motivation for a sophisticated adaptive method. This work would be a reasonable workshop paper or short paper demonstrating a useful engineering trick, but it lacks the novelty depth and experimental rigor for a top venue full paper.
