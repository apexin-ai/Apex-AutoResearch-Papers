## Core Problem

LLM user simulators exhibit systematic behavioral miscalibration for dialectal speaker groups in agentic task-completion settings: they are excessively cooperative, over-polite, and question-asking at rates that diverge sharply from real users in groups like AAVE and Indian English speakers. Seshadri et al. (AFAA@ICLR 2026; arXiv 2601.17087) document that AAVE speakers ask questions in only ~10% of turns versus 18.8% for LLM simulators, use politeness markers at ~19.7% versus 39.2%; Indian English speakers (India-based Prolific participants, 18–34 age group) show nearly identical behavioral profiles — 9.9% question rate and 20.1% politeness rate — both groups sharply divergent from simulator baselines. This behavioral miscalibration is not merely noisy: Seshadri et al. report a concrete downstream consequence — AAVE real users achieve approximately 39% agent task-completion rate versus ~49% for SAE speakers; India-based participants achieve approximately 46.2%. If this gap is partly attributable to simulator over-cooperativeness producing artificially inflated success rate estimates for dominant-group speakers, then calibrated simulators should produce success rate estimates closer to the real-user baselines. Seshadri et al. demonstrate in Appendix A.6 that a 'minimal intervention' behavioral constraint prompt (BCP) limiting politeness to 'at most one or two times across the entire conversation' shifts 11 of 18 tasks' difficulty bins and reduces aggregate simulated success rate from 50.0% to 46.7% — partially improving calibration for AAVE, Indian, and Kenyan groups. However, the BCP constrains only politeness frequency, leaving the question-asking rate gap (18.8% simulated vs. ~10% real for both AAVE and Indian English) entirely unaddressed; aggregate success rate remains at 46.7%, far above the AAVE real-user baseline of ~39%. No method exists to adapt a general-purpose LLM simulator's full behavioral distribution to match the empirically observed behavioral profile of a target dialectal group given a small reference sample — addressing both question-asking and politeness dimensions without degrading calibration for other groups — and no prior work has validated such adaptation against downstream agent evaluation consistency. Importantly, because assembling a genuine Prolific reference corpus requires IRB approval and participant recruitment (minimum 6–7 weeks), the framework must be demonstrable via a synthetic pilot that uses empirically-documented behavioral rates from published studies as calibration targets — enabling proof-of-concept results within a typical submission window, with full human validation as a planned follow-up contribution.

## Opportunity / Why Now

'Lost in Simulation' (Seshadri et al., AFAA Workshop @ ICLR 2026; arXiv 2601.17087) conducted a Prolific-recruited user study (~40 participants per group from the US, India, Kenya, Nigeria) running the full τ-Bench protocol, and documented sharp behavioral gaps between real users and LLM simulators. Critically, Table 6 of Seshadri et al. provides group-level behavioral rate breakdowns for both AAVE and Indian English speakers: AAVE speakers asked questions in only ~10% of turns versus 18.8% for LLM simulators; politeness marker usage was ~19.7% for AAVE real users versus 39.2% for simulators. India-based participants (18–34 age group, the only Indian age cohort recruited due to Prolific availability constraints) asked questions in 9.9% of turns and used politeness markers in 20.1% of turns — strikingly similar to AAVE rates and equally divergent from the 18.8%/39.2% simulator baseline. This convergence of AAVE and Indian English rates, both sharply below simulator baselines, provides a strong empirical foundation for a two-group calibration study: both groups have published target rates from the same study using the same methodology, enabling a direct comparison of calibration difficulty and transferability. Table 3 reports per-group agent success rates: AAVE real users achieved approximately 39% agent task-completion versus approximately 49% for SAE real users; India-based participants achieved approximately 46.2%. These per-group success rates anchor the proxy downstream evaluation: if behavioral miscalibration is a primary driver of biased success rate estimates, calibrated simulators should produce estimates closer to these real-user baselines.

Critically, Seshadri et al. also document (Appendix A.6) a 'minimal intervention': a behavioral constraint prompt (BCP) limiting politeness to 'at most one or two times across the entire conversation' shifts 11 of 18 tasks' difficulty bins and reduces aggregate simulated success rate from 50.0% to 46.7%. The BCP improves simulator calibration for AAVE, Indian, and Kenyan groups but worsens it for SAE and Nigerian participants — demonstrating that a universal politeness constraint is not group-neutral and that single-dimension prompting falls short of full calibration. The BCP constrains only politeness; it leaves the question-asking rate divergence (18.8% simulated vs. ~10% real) entirely unaddressed, and aggregate success rate at 46.7% remains far above the AAVE real-user baseline of ~39%. The Indian English target (~46.2%) is closer to BCP's 46.7% aggregate, but this proximity is coincidental: a group-specific Indian English calibration targeting 9.9% question rate and 20.1% politeness rate requires matching both behavioral dimensions simultaneously and without degrading other groups. DemoCalib extends from BCP's single-dimension prompting to a full data-efficient behavioral distribution matching framework — addressing both dimensions, providing a concrete sample complexity characterization, and enabling group-specific calibration without universal constraint.

AVAVE participants also encountered argument errors at 45.8% versus 32.2% for simulators; the Indian English group showed an Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of 18.9% versus 13.0% for SAE — confirming simulator miscalibration is worst for both groups. These are large effect sizes in agentic task language, far exceeding the ~1.5% demographic-variance figure from SCOPE (arXiv 2601.07110), which measured variance in survey opinion responses, not language-production behavior during task completion. Importantly, the 9-point agent-success-rate variation documented in 'Lost in Simulation' is primarily a model-to-model spread across 31 different LLM simulators; the demographic disparity is a secondary finding: simulated users are differentially unreliable proxies, performing worst for AAVE and Indian English speakers, with disparities compounding with age. 'Mind the Sim2Real Gap' (arXiv 2603.11245, CMU, March 2026) corroborates simulator over-cooperativeness across 451 real participants in aggregate (no per-dialect breakdown) but provides no adaptation method and has no dialect-group-specific rate data. Neither study has released participant interaction logs publicly: Seshadri et al. include no data availability statement or GitHub repository in the paper; 'Mind the Sim2Real Gap' likewise omits a data release. The CC-BY 4.0 license on the Seshadri et al. paper applies to the paper text only, not to participant interaction data — reuse of participant traces requires a separate formal data-sharing agreement and likely participant reconsent for secondary use, a process requiring 4–8 weeks. MirrorBench (arXiv 2601.08118) provides metrics for evaluating human-likeness (MATTR, HD-D, GTEval) without any training signal to produce calibrated simulators. The LLM-based conversational user simulation survey (arXiv 2604.24977) identifies demographic calibration as an open challenge. 'Toward Scalable Verifiable Reward: Proxy State-Based Evaluation for Multi-turn Tool-Calling LLM Agents' (arXiv 2602.16246) demonstrates LLM-driven proxy evaluation for τ-bench-style agentic tasks with >90% human-judge agreement, providing methodological grounding for the proxy downstream evaluation component. Crucially, the published empirical rates from Seshadri et al. Table 6 — AAVE: 10% question rate, ~19.7% politeness rate; Indian English (18–34): 9.9% question rate, 20.1% politeness rate; simulator: 18.8%, 39.2% — provide concrete, IRB-free calibration targets that directly parameterize synthetic reference corpus construction and DPO contrastive pair generation for both groups. The 18–34 age constraint on Indian English participants (a Prolific recruitment limitation in Seshadri et al., not a design choice) means the calibration targets represent younger adult Indian English speakers; age-stratified calibration for Indian English across broader demographics is a planned extension. This synthetic pilot validates the calibration pipeline; a full Prolific human-validation study is planned as a follow-on contribution. Seshadri et al.'s Prolific methodology (racial/ethnic + dialect self-identification screening) has been validated for recruiting AAVE speakers in LLM user studies (corroborated by Black LLMirror at CHI 2026), making the planned human validation study concrete and feasible at ~$1,200–2,200 (~120–180 participants × $7–10 each including platform fees). DialectLLM/MDial (arXiv 2601.22888, KAIST and Amazon, January 2026; updated May 2026) is used as a source of Indian English linguistic transformation rules for synthetic corpus generation in Phase 1 and as Baseline (e) for the Indian English group.

## Landscape (SOTA & Limitations)

The user simulation landscape centers on τ-Bench and related agentic task benchmarks. 'Lost in Simulation' (AFAA Workshop @ ICLR 2026; arXiv 2601.17087) and 'Mind the Sim2Real Gap' (CMU, 2026) both quantify simulator-to-human gaps but provide no adaptation methods and have not released participant data. MirrorBench evaluates human-likeness without producing calibrated simulators. Liu et al. (arXiv 2605.02624, May 2026) propose 'realsim,' an 8-dimensional framework for evaluating user simulation quality across communicative functions, user states, and message surface forms, finding that simulated users systematically underrepresent communication frictions that real users introduce; unlike DemoCalib, it focuses on evaluation metrics rather than adaptation methodology, and does not address dialect-group behavioral calibration. ConvApparel (Meshi et al., EACL 2026; arXiv 2602.16938) measures the realism gap in conversational recommender simulators and validates data-driven approaches over prompting; unlike DemoCalib, it does not address dialect-group-specific behavioral calibration or agentic task-completion evaluation, and provides no adaptation methodology for closing the gap for target demographic groups.

A critical related method emerges from Seshadri et al. themselves: in Appendix A.6, the authors demonstrate a 'Behavioral Constraint Prompt' (BCP) that limits politeness to 'at most one or two times across the entire conversation.' The BCP reduces aggregate simulated success rate from 50.0% to 46.7% and shifts 11 of 18 tasks' difficulty bins, improving calibration for AAVE, Indian, and Kenyan groups while worsening it for SAE and Nigerian participants. The BCP is a zero-compute single-dimension prompting intervention that partially achieves DemoCalib's goal. Its limitations are threefold: (1) it constrains only politeness frequency, leaving the question-asking rate divergence (18.8% simulated vs. ~10% real for both AAVE and Indian English) entirely unaddressed; (2) aggregate success rate at 46.7% remains far above the AAVE real-user baseline (~39%), indicating that politeness-only calibration is insufficient for the hardest miscalibration cases; (3) it applies a universal constraint affecting all groups, degrading calibration for already well-calibrated groups (SAE, Nigerian), whereas DemoCalib provides group-specific calibration. DemoCalib includes BCP as Baseline (f) — extended to constrain both politeness and question rate explicitly — to provide a direct comparison between zero-compute prompting and data-efficient training. This comparison is informative regardless of outcome: if BCP (extended) achieves comparable behavioral rate matching without training, DemoCalib identifies the condition under which prompting suffices; if DPO outperforms extended BCP on held-out test traces and/or proxy downstream evaluation, the paper demonstrates added value of data-efficient training for multi-dimensional behavioral calibration.

Several concurrent works partially address related problems but do not close the specific gap:

UserLM-R1 (arXiv 2601.09215, Meituan and collaborating Chinese universities — Meituan, Peking University, Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications, University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, Dalian University of Technology — January 2026): The current frontier in user simulation model training. Trains user LMs with multi-reward RL combining rule-based (format, field verification) and rubric-based (reasoning quality, strategic alignment, conversational initiative) reward signals, using Qwen-3 8B/32B base models with adaptive user profiles that combine static persona traits (background, personality, expression style) and dynamic scenario-specific goals. Trained on 90,000 user profiles from AlignX and 450 task SOPs across 6 business scenarios; achieves strong performance particularly on adversarial agent-interaction tasks. Two key distinctions from DemoCalib: (1) UserLM-R1 is explicitly trained on Chinese-language samples with no English dialect or demographic calibration, and does not address the simulator-to-human behavioral gap for any dialect group; (2) it targets strategic reasoning capability and cross-domain generalization rather than matching the behavioral distribution of a specific real dialectal group in English agentic settings. UserLM-R1 is included as Baseline (c): zero-shot with an AAVE/Indian English dialect system prompt, testing whether RL-trained adaptive persona and reasoning capability — without explicit behavioral calibration — closes the miscalibration gap documented by Seshadri et al.

UserLM-8b ('Flipping the Dialogue,' arXiv 2510.06552, NAACL 2025, Microsoft Research): Fine-tunes an 8B model on WildChat for general-conversation user simulation with no dialect-group targeting, no sample complexity analysis, and no downstream agent evaluation consistency metric.

DialectLLM/MDial (arXiv 2601.22888, KAIST and Amazon, January 2026; updated May 2026): Rule-based framework for generating dialect-accurate multi-turn dialogues across 9 English dialects (SAE/US, Australian, British, Canadian, Indian, Irish, Nigerian, Philippine, Scottish) using linguist-curated lexical/orthographic/morphosyntactic transformation rules applied to SAE seed dialogues. Produces DialectLLM-Bench with 50k+ dialogues and 97k+ QA pairs; evaluation of 17 LLMs shows frontier models achieve under 70% dialect accuracy. Three critical distinctions from DemoCalib: (1) DialectLLM targets linguistic form accuracy — surface dialect features in generated text — while DemoCalib targets behavioral interaction dynamics (question rates, politeness frequency, repair patterns) in multi-turn agentic task flows; (2) DialectLLM generates generic conversational exchanges, not agentic task-completion traces under τ-Bench protocol with tool-use and policy constraints; (3) DialectLLM applies rule-based SAE-to-dialect transformation without any feedback loop for behavioral distribution matching. Note: DialectLLM covers Indian English but does not include AAVE among its 9 dialects (Nigerian English is present; AAVE as a US variety requires separate feature sources such as CORAAL and AAVENUE). DialectLLM-conditioned generation is included as Baseline (e) for the Indian English group: applying its IN transformation rules to uncalibrated simulator outputs tests whether rule-based linguistic form calibration alone suffices versus DemoCalib's behavioral distribution matching.

The PAL framework (arXiv 2511.10215, TACL): Uses DPO for persona-conditioned dialogue generation, optimizing persona alignment in general dialogue rather than matching the behavioral distribution of a specific real dialect group in agentic task settings.

PersoDPO (arXiv 2602.04493, February 2026): Applies DPO with multi-LLM automatic evaluation signals to construct preference pairs for persona-consistent dialogue generation, optimizing for coherence, personalization, and instruction adherence on the FoCus conversational dialogue benchmark. Three distinctions from DemoCalib: (1) PersoDPO targets generic persona consistency (narrative coherence and identity alignment) rather than measurable behavioral rate matching (question-asking frequency, politeness marker frequency) for specific dialect groups; (2) it evaluates on open-domain conversational dialogue rather than multi-turn agentic task-completion under τ-Bench protocol with tool-use and policy constraints; (3) it does not address the simulator-to-human behavioral gap for any dialect group and includes no dialect or demographic variation analysis. PersoDPO is methodologically complementary: DemoCalib extends preference optimization from persona coherence to empirically grounded behavioral rate calibration in agentic evaluation settings.

Socrates/SocSci210 (arXiv 2509.05830, EMNLP 2025, Stanford): Fine-tunes on 2.9M social science experiment responses — a survey/experiment domain rather than multi-turn agentic task completion, and with orders of magnitude more training data than DemoCalib's small-sample setting.

Population-Aligned Persona Generation (arXiv 2509.10127, September 2025, Microsoft Research): Methodologically closest prior work — uses importance sampling to align LLM persona distributions to Big Five psychometric reference distributions for social simulation. Operates on self-report survey data (not agentic interaction traces), targets population-level psychometric distributions (not dialect-group behavioral fingerprints), and does not validate against downstream task evaluation consistency.

HUMAINE (arXiv 2603.04409, ICLR 2026): Stratifies the evaluation dataset across 22 demographic groups, calibrating what we measure rather than how we simulate.

SCOPE (arXiv 2601.07110): Argues demographic-only personas explain ~1.5% of variance in survey response similarity — a finding about opinion responses, not the language-production behavioral patterns (question rate, politeness frequency, repair sequences) that differ substantially between dialect groups in agentic task completion.

Haq and Saldías (arXiv 2604.21152, April 2026) study implicit dialect signals (AAVE, Singlish) versus explicit demographic profiles in LLM responses, documenting a 'dialect jailbreak' phenomenon where implicit dialect cues reduce model refusal rates toward near-zero — an input-side bias in how LLMs respond to dialect users, distinct from DemoCalib's simulator-side behavioral calibration, but corroborating that implicit dialect features produce measurable and consequential LLM behavioral differences.

Toward Scalable Verifiable Reward (arXiv 2602.16246, 2026): Introduces proxy state-based evaluation for multi-turn tool-calling agents using LLM-driven state tracking and goal-completion judges, achieving >90% human-judge agreement. Provides methodological grounding for the proxy downstream evaluation component of DemoCalib but does not address dialect-group behavioral calibration.

The gap: given a small reference corpus or empirically-documented target behavioral profile for a specific dialect group, no existing method closes the loop from behavioral fingerprint extraction → simulator adaptation → proxy downstream agent evaluation consistency validation, with quantified sample complexity, in the τ-Bench agentic evaluation context. Seshadri et al.'s BCP (Appendix A.6) represents the strongest prior intervention — zero-compute, sourced from the primary motivating paper — but its single-dimension (politeness-only) scope and universal (non-group-specific) application demonstrate that it falls short of the full calibration problem DemoCalib addresses.

## Proposed Approach

DemoCalib is a data-efficient framework that adapts a general-purpose LLM simulator (Qwen 2.5 7B-Instruct as primary base model, selected for strong instruction-following capability, extensive open-weight documentation, and active HuggingFace community; Qwen 3.5 9B evaluated as a secondary ablation, contingent on publicly released weights being available on HuggingFace at time of submission) to match the behavioral distribution of a target dialect group. The framework operates in two phases: a proof-of-concept synthetic pilot (primary, 16-day feasible, IRB-free) and a planned human validation study (follow-up, providing downstream agent evaluation consistency validation and a genuine reference corpus contribution).

**Phase 1 — Synthetic Pilot (Primary, Submission-Ready):**

Target behavioral profiles for each dialect group are directly specified from published empirical rates in Seshadri et al. (AFAA Workshop @ ICLR 2026; arXiv 2601.17087), Table 6: AAVE (question rate = 10% per turn, politeness marker rate = ~19.7%); Indian English (question rate = 9.9% per turn, politeness marker rate = 20.1%; India-based Prolific participants, 18–34 age group — the only Indian English cohort recruited in Seshadri et al. due to platform availability constraints, so calibration targets represent younger adult speakers; age-stratified Indian English calibration is a planned extension); SAE control (uncalibrated simulator baseline rates of 18.8% question rate, 39.2% politeness rate, from the same study).

**Two-Group Design: Testing Cross-Feature Calibration Under Matched Behavioral Targets.** AAVE (10%/~19.7%) and Indian English (9.9%/20.1%) share nearly identical published calibration targets. Rather than treating this as a limitation, the two-group design explicitly tests two questions distinct from differential behavioral fingerprint matching: (a) whether DemoCalib can achieve matched behavioral targets across groups that differ substantially in linguistic surface features — AAVE calibration draws on CORAAL/AAVENUE morphosyntactic features (copula deletion, habitual be, negative concord, preverbal aspect marking), while Indian English calibration uses DialectLLM IN transformation rules (retroflex markers, topic-prominent constructions, distinct lexical items) — and (b) whether calibration difficulty, sample complexity curve, and method effectiveness (DPO vs. retrieval vs. BCP prompting) differ across groups despite identical numerical targets. If calibration converges at different rates or requires different N for AAVE vs. Indian English despite identical targets, this indicates that surface linguistic feature complexity mediates calibration difficulty — a theoretically important finding about the coupling between form and behavioral adaptation. The Indian English group additionally enables unique comparison with Baseline (e) (DialectLLM-conditioned generation), which has no AAVE analogue. This framing is stated explicitly in the paper to preempt the concern that a two-group study with matched targets only tests one behavioral profile: the multi-group contribution is cross-feature calibration under shared targets, not differential fingerprint matching.

A synthetic reference corpus is generated for each group using a dialect-conditioned LLM (GPT-4o or Llama-3.1 70B) prompted with dialect-appropriate system instructions on τ-Bench retail tasks: AAVE instructions are informed by CORAAL-documented morphosyntactic features and AAVENUE benchmark patterns; Indian English instructions use DialectLLM/MDial's (KAIST and Amazon) Indian English transformation rules. This produces 100–200 τ-Bench interaction traces per group (~300–600 total). Generated turns are then post-processed to match target behavioral rates (e.g., excess question-asking turns identified by a simple utterance-type classifier and replaced with non-question continuations until the target rate is achieved). This post-processing step is critical: it prevents the circularity of using LLM-generated corpus to calibrate LLM behavioral rates, since the LLM generator itself exhibits the same over-questioning and over-politeness miscalibration being corrected. Linguistic texture comes from the dialect-conditioned generator; behavioral rates are independently grounded in Seshadri et al.'s published human data.

Post-processing coherence constraint: Each replacement turn must pass a slot-filling consistency check against the task state before being accepted. Specifically, (1) the replacement must not introduce an open question that the agent has already answered in the preceding turn, and (2) it must reference only task variables (product IDs, order numbers, return policies) that have been established in the dialogue history up to that point. This check is implemented as a regex-based slot tracker operating on the τ-Bench structured task state, requiring no additional model calls and adding negligible latency to the post-processing pipeline. Replacement candidates failing this check are discarded and regenerated until a coherent candidate is found or the turn is flagged for manual review.

The entire generation and post-processing pipeline takes 1–2 days via API calls at ~$20–50 in API costs.

Behavioral fingerprints are extracted from each group's reference traces: question-asking rate per turn, politeness marker frequency (detected via a curated lexicon), turn-length histograms, lexical diversity (MATTR, HD-D), clarification-request frequency, and error-repair patterns. As a validity criterion (not a completed finding), the fingerprint extraction is designed so that fingerprints from the synthetic reference traces should separate dialect groups at >50% 3-class classification accuracy; this threshold confirms that the synthetic corpus exhibits sufficient behavioral divergence to ground calibration. If this criterion is met, the calibration training proceeds as planned. If not, a two-stage contingency applies: (a) First fallback — increase dialect feature density in the generation prompt using stricter CORAAL-derived morphosyntactic constraints for AAVE and more restrictive DialectLLM IN transformation rules for Indian English, then regenerate the corpus and re-evaluate fingerprint classification; (b) Second fallback — if the criterion still fails after corpus revision, reduce the behavioral gap being matched from the full dialect–SAE divergence to the most robustly reproducible subset (e.g., politeness marker rate only, which shows the largest absolute divergence), report partial calibration results, and frame the incomplete fingerprint separation as a substantive finding about the limits of current dialect-conditioned LLM generation — a publishable negative result about the circularity constraint in synthetic dialect calibration. This contingency turns a binary pass/fail into a graceful degradation with interpretable findings at each stage.

Meeting the primary validity criterion demonstrates that behavioral fingerprints in agentic task data capture group differences beyond SCOPE's 1.5% survey-response variance figure — because the behavioral rate targets were independently specified from human data rather than derived from the LLM generator.

Two calibration strategies are compared: (1) DPO fine-tuning with contrastive pairs opposing reference group turns against uncalibrated simulator outputs on identical task prompts, implemented with LoRA (rank=64) for data-efficient adaptation; (2) retrieval-augmented few-shot conditioning injecting the k most behaviorally similar reference turns as in-context demonstrations at inference time, requiring no gradient updates. Six baselines:

(a) Uncalibrated Qwen 2.5 7B-Instruct with demographic system prompt (HUMAINE-style persona prompting) — the generic demographically-aware prompting baseline;

(b) UserLM-8b conditioned on dialect-group system prompt;

(c) UserLM-R1 (Qwen-3 8B, from Meituan and collaborating Chinese universities, if weights are publicly available at submission time; otherwise approximated as zero-shot Qwen-3 8B with identical dialect system prompt) with AAVE/Indian English dialect system prompt — testing whether RL-trained adaptive persona and reasoning capability without explicit behavioral calibration closes the gap;

(d) Population-Aligned Persona Generation adapted to use behavioral fingerprints instead of Big Five traits for importance sampling;

(e) DialectLLM-conditioned generation (KAIST and Amazon; arXiv 2601.22888) applied to τ-Bench interactions for the Indian English group only (DialectLLM does not cover AAVE), testing whether rule-based linguistic form calibration alone suffices versus DemoCalib's behavioral distribution matching;

(f) Behavioral Constraint Prompt (BCP) — Qwen 2.5 7B-Instruct prompted with explicit per-turn behavioral rate targets derived directly from Seshadri et al. Table 6: 'Ask questions in approximately 10% of turns. Use politeness markers (e.g., please, thank you, sorry) in approximately 20% of turns.' Applied independently to each dialect group (AAVE and Indian English). This extends the 'minimal intervention' documented in Seshadri et al. Appendix A.6 — which constrained only politeness to 'at most one or two times across the entire conversation' and reduced aggregate success rate from 50.0% to 46.7% — to a full two-dimensional behavioral rate constraint. BCP is zero-compute (no training, no data), and is the critical comparison point for assessing whether DemoCalib's DPO fine-tuning adds value over simply encoding target rates in the system prompt. It is also the most natural baseline a reviewer familiar with Seshadri et al. would expect. Expected outcome: BCP achieves partial behavioral rate compliance on both dimensions but produces turns with degraded pragmatic coherence — abrupt non-questions that disrupt task flow — because instruction-following for rate constraints does not guarantee coherent substitution. DemoCalib's DPO/retrieval approach learns the underlying behavioral distribution, producing calibrated turns that maintain agentic pragmatic function. The comparison is informative regardless of direction: if BCP achieves comparable rate matching, DemoCalib identifies the condition under which prompting suffices; if DPO outperforms BCP on fingerprint match rate and/or proxy downstream evaluation, the paper demonstrates the added value of data-efficient training. Note: the Seshadri et al. BCP (politeness-only) worsens calibration for SAE and Nigerian groups while improving for AAVE, Indian, and Kenyan — demonstrating that universal behavioral constraint prompting is not group-neutral. DemoCalib's group-specific calibration avoids this: it targets AAVE and Indian English specifically without requiring a universal constraint applied to all groups.

Calibration quality is validated via MirrorBench metrics (MATTR, HD-D, GTEval), behavioral fingerprint match rate (do calibrated outputs achieve target question/politeness rates on held-out synthetic test traces?), and task-completion preservation (does calibration maintain agentic task functionality?). Sample complexity is characterized by varying reference corpus size from N=10 to N=100 synthetic dialogues, reporting the minimum N needed to achieve fingerprint match within a target threshold — giving practitioners a concrete sample requirement curve for any dialect group.

**Proxy Downstream Evaluation (Within Phase 1, Submission-Ready):**

To validate that behavioral rate calibration produces consequential changes in agent evaluation outcomes — directly addressing the gap between demonstrating behavioral rate matching and claiming calibration reduces evaluation bias — we run a proxy downstream evaluation within the synthetic pilot, using Seshadri et al.'s published real-user success rates as ground truth. Setup: (1) Fix a single agent (GPT-4o with τ-Bench retail tool access, using the standard τ-Bench retail tool configuration); (2) run N=50 τ-Bench retail task episodes per condition (250 total) using five simulator variants: uncalibrated Qwen 2.5 7B with AAVE demographic system prompt (Baseline a), BCP-extended AAVE simulator (Baseline f), DemoCalib DPO-calibrated AAVE simulator at optimal N, DemoCalib retrieval-augmented AAVE simulator at optimal N, and the DemoCalib DPO-calibrated Indian English simulator at optimal N; (3) compute simulated agent success rate per condition; (4) compare directionally to Seshadri et al.'s published human-grounded baselines: AAVE real users achieved approximately 39% agent task-completion; India-based participants achieved approximately 46.2%; SAE real users achieved approximately 49% — with the AAVE–SAE gap (approximately 10 percentage points) and the India–SAE gap (approximately 3 percentage points) anchoring the fairness claims for their respective groups. Including both real-user baselines — AAVE (~39%) and Indian English (~46.2%) — allows the proxy evaluation to cover both calibration arms: a calibrated AAVE simulator should produce success rate estimates directionally closer to ~39%, while a calibrated Indian English simulator should produce estimates directionally closer to ~46.2%.

Hypothesis: uncalibrated simulators, being excessively cooperative (over-polite, over-questioning), give agents more opportunities to clarify intent and recover from errors, artificially inflating estimated success rates above the real-user baselines; BCP (extended, two-dimensional) reduces cooperativeness via prompting but produces less pragmatically coherent turns; DemoCalib calibrated simulators produce behaviorally calibrated, pragmatically coherent interactions, generating success rate estimates directionally closer to the real-user baselines than either the uncalibrated simulator or BCP. Even directional confirmation of this hierarchy (uncalibrated → BCP → DemoCalib, each closer to real-user baselines) validates both that behavioral miscalibration is consequential for downstream evaluation fairness and that data-efficient training adds value over zero-compute prompting. Since we post-process only question rate and politeness frequency, calibration reduces but does not eliminate the full behavioral gap — remaining divergence in argument error rates (AAVE: 45.8% vs. simulator 32.2%) and repair sequences is expected to produce residual sim-real success rate discrepancy, motivating Phase 2. This proxy evaluation draws on the methodology established by 'Toward Scalable Verifiable Reward' (arXiv 2602.16246), which demonstrates LLM-driven proxy state tracking and goal-completion assessment for τ-Bench-style tasks with >90% human-judge agreement.

Agent comparability caveat: Our proxy evaluation fixes GPT-4o with the standard τ-Bench retail tool configuration; Seshadri et al. do not report their exact agent setup, so the directional comparison to their real-user baselines may partly reflect agent-side differences independent of simulator calibration — we treat this comparison as indicative, with full agent-controlled comparison deferred to Phase 2.

API cost: ~$75–300 at 250 episodes × $0.30–1.20/episode (GPT-4o agent calls plus simulator inference, including BCP baseline episodes); no GPU required. Runtime: 2–3 days concurrent with main experiment analysis, well within the 16-day pilot window.

Contribution claims are scoped explicitly to match results: if directional alignment is confirmed for both groups, the paper claims DemoCalib 'reduces simulation bias in agent success rate estimates for AAVE and Indian English speakers, producing estimates directionally closer to Seshadri et al. real-user baselines, outperforming both uncalibrated prompting and BCP behavioral constraint prompting'; if the proxy evaluation confirms directional improvement over uncalibrated but not over BCP, the paper claims DemoCalib is comparable to zero-compute BCP with the added benefit of behavioral distribution generalization; if the proxy evaluation does not confirm directional alignment, contribution claims are rewritten to strictly address 'behavioral rate matching in simulation,' downstream evaluation consequence is moved entirely to Phase 2, and the absence of directional alignment is itself a substantive finding about the limits of behavioral rate calibration as a proxy for evaluation consequence.

**Sample Complexity and Synthetic Corpus Fidelity Caveats:**

The N=10–100 sample complexity curve characterizes data efficiency under synthetic-to-synthetic transfer conditions: both the calibration training signal and the held-out test traces are synthetic. Efficiency under real-to-real transfer (Phase 2 Prolific corpus) may differ because genuine human interaction traces exhibit natural behavioral variation beyond the rate targets specified as calibration targets — including idiosyncratic repair sequences, code-switching, and pragmatic patterns not captured by the two-dimensional fingerprint. This caveat is stated explicitly in the paper's limitations section. Phase 2 is the mechanism for characterizing real-to-real sample complexity.

The post-processing step guarantees target behavioral rates on the two measured dimensions (question rate, politeness frequency); however, other behavioral dimensions in the synthetic corpus — including code-switching patterns, error-repair sequences, pragmatic hedging, and clarification phrasing — remain subject to the same LLM miscalibration that the framework aims to correct, and may not fully reflect real AAVE or Indian English speaker patterns. Specifically, dialect-conditioned LLM generators that over-ask questions in unconditioned settings may also produce stylistically uniform repair sequences and formulaic hedging that differs from naturally occurring AAVE or Indian English pragmatic variation. Phase 2 human validation is the mechanism for assessing the full scope of behavioral fidelity beyond the two post-processed rate dimensions — the comparison between synthetic-corpus-calibrated and human-corpus-calibrated simulators in Phase 2 will directly quantify the gap introduced by uncontrolled LLM miscalibration on non-post-processed behavioral dimensions.

(AFAA Workshop @ ICLR 2026) methodology recruits AAVE speakers (~40–60, US-based), Indian English speakers (~40–60, India-based, spanning a broader age range than Seshadri et al.'s 18–34 constraint), and SAE controls (~40), each completing 4 τ-Bench retail tasks (order management, product inquiry, return processing, cancellation), yielding 160–240 real interaction dialogues per group at ~$1,200–2,200 total. This study provides: (a) a genuine human reference corpus replacing the synthetic corpus (enabling direct comparison of synthetic vs. human reference corpus calibration quality and real-to-real sample complexity), (b) downstream agent evaluation consistency validation — calibrated-simulator-estimated agent success rate versus real-user success rate on held-out τ-Bench tasks using a matched agent configuration, targeting reduction of the sim-real behavioral calibration gap documented by Seshadri et al., (c) age-stratified Indian English behavioral rates beyond the 18–34 window, and (d) a public curated dataset contribution (AAVE and Indian English τ-Bench interaction traces).
