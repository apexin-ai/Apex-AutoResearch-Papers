# 2 Related Work

## 2.1 LLM-Based User Simulation for Agent Evaluation

LLM-based user simulators have become central to evaluating tool-calling agents. Yao et al. (2024) introduce τ-bench, a benchmark pairing an LLM user simulator with a tool-calling agent in retail and airline domains, and propose the pass^k reliability metric. Liu et al. (2024) and Wang et al. (2024) establish complementary multi-environment and multi-turn evaluation paradigms, though neither incorporates demographic variation in their simulated users. Naous et al. (2025) demonstrate that purpose-built User LMs outperform repurposed assistant models, with GPT-4o success rates dropping from 74.6% to 57.4% under realistic simulation. Sekulić et al. (2024) show that domain-aware fine-tuning improves user goal fulfillment, while Ahmad et al. (2025) generate diverse user profiles with varied demographics and conversational styles. Nakash et al. (2026) address evaluation efficiency through diversity-guided branching trajectories. Most critically, Zhou et al. (2026) conduct the first full τ-bench evaluation with 451 real humans, finding that LLM simulators are excessively cooperative and stylistically uniform, inflating agent success rates. Seshadri et al. (2026) further document that AAVE and Indian English speakers experience the worst calibration errors. However, none of these works attempt to *correct* the identified sim-to-real gaps through behavioral calibration—they diagnose without prescribing, leaving open whether calibration methods can close the fidelity gaps they identify.

## 2.2 Behavioral Calibration of Language Models

Prompt-based calibration has a rich history: Zhao et al. (2021) show that contextual calibration of output distributions can improve few-shot accuracy by up to 30%, but their method targets classification biases, not behavioral rates in generation. In multi-turn settings, Abdulhai et al. (2025) apply multi-turn RL to reduce persona inconsistency by over 55%, demonstrating that off-the-shelf LLMs drift from assigned personas across conversation turns. A growing body of work, however, documents fundamental limitations of persona and role prompting. Basil et al. (2025) find that expert personas *degrade* factual accuracy—MMLU drops from 71.6% to 66.3% with detailed persona instructions—because persona prompting activates instruction-following mode at the expense of factual recall. Hu et al. (2026) systematically confirm this trade-off, reporting accuracy degradation of up to 30 percentage points, with more instruction-tuned models being more susceptible. Li et al. (2025) show that LLM-generated personas drift toward socially desirable traits, introducing bias at the persona creation step itself. Our work extends these findings to a previously unstudied setting: multi-turn agent dialogue with tool-calling requirements, where we find that prompt-based behavioral calibration (BCP) triggers a more severe failure—global suppression of task completion—because the model must simultaneously follow calibration instructions and execute structured tool calls.

## 2.3 Preference Optimization for Bias Mitigation

Direct Preference Optimization (Rafailov et al., 2023) offers a computationally lightweight alternative to PPO-based RLHF by directly optimizing policy from preference pairs without an explicit reward model, and has been widely adopted for alignment tasks (Wang et al., 2024). Recent work applies preference optimization to bias mitigation: Khan (2026) proposes decoding-time debiasing via Process Reward Models, achieving bias score improvements of up to +0.40 without weight changes—a complementary inference-time approach to our training-time DPO strategy. However, DPO's alignment mechanism has known fragility. Lee et al. (2024) provide mechanistic evidence that DPO *bypasses* rather than *removes* pre-training capabilities—toxic behavior can be recovered through simple un-alignment with minimal parameter changes. Paraschou et al. (2026) document a related phenomenon: targeted debiasing on one social dimension causes unintended *bias spillover* on other dimensions, indicating that multi-attribute fairness dynamics must be considered jointly. Our work reveals a failure mode at the intersection of these concerns: when DPO-aligned models are combined with BCP prompting, the calibration direction *reverses*—an interaction effect not predictable from studying either method in isolation.

## 2.4 Dialect and Sociolinguistic Bias in NLP

AAVE and Indian English represent two well-studied but underserved varieties in NLP. Rickford (1999) provides the authoritative description of AAVE phonological and pragmatic features, while Kachru and Smith (2008) document Indian English politeness strategies rooted in distinct face-saving norms. Joshi et al. (2025) survey NLP for dialects comprehensively, noting systematic performance degradation on dialectal data across both NLU and NLG tasks. Several benchmarks quantify these disparities: Faisal et al. (2024) cover 281 language varieties across 10 NLP tasks in DialectBench; Lin et al. (2025) introduce ReDial, the first human-annotated SE–AAVE parallel benchmark, finding that LLMs struggle more with AAVE than with misspelled Standard English; and Gupta et al. (2025) present EnDive, a cross-dialect benchmark covering both our target dialects (AAVE and Indian English) across understanding, reasoning, and math tasks. Haq and Saldias (2026) demonstrate that dialect cues alone trigger differential LLM behavior—a "dialect jailbreak" reducing refusal probability to near zero—establishing that bias operates through implicit linguistic signals, not just explicit identity markers. Gallegos et al. (2024) provide a comprehensive taxonomy of bias metrics and mitigation methods across the LLM pipeline. Critically, existing work evaluates dialect bias in classification, QA, and reasoning tasks. No prior study measures *behavioral rate disparities* (e.g., question-asking frequency, politeness levels) in multi-turn agent dialogue, nor documents what happens when calibration methods are applied to correct such disparities.
