\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Temporal abstraction in model-free RL.}
The options framework \cite{sutton1999options} provides a foundational formalism for temporally extended actions, decomposing policies into initiation sets, intra-option policies, and termination conditions. Subsequent model-free approaches learn action repetition durations directly: FiGAR \cite{sharma2017figar} augments the policy output with a repetition count, TempoRL \cite{biedenkapp2021temporl} learns a skip-policy that decides when to act, and dynamic action repetition \cite{lakshminarayanan2017dynamic} selects per-step durations via an auxiliary network. Even fixed frame skip significantly impacts learning outcomes \cite{braylan2015frame}, and recent work extends action repetition to per-dimension decisions for finer temporal control \cite{nie2025sdar}. Theoretical analysis shows that Q-learning performance is highly sensitive to time discretization \cite{tallec2019making}, and achieving discretization-invariant action repetition requires explicit architectural modifications \cite{park2021sar}. These methods succeed in model-free settings where the value function trains on real environment transitions. Our work investigates why analogous temporal abstraction fails when transferred to imagination-based training in world-model RL, where the critic trains entirely on model-generated data.

\paragraph{World-model reinforcement learning.}
The Dreamer family learns latent dynamics via the Recurrent State-Space Model (RSSM) and trains actor-critic networks entirely in imagination: DreamerV1 \cite{hafner2020dreamerv1} introduced imagination-based policy optimization, DreamerV2 \cite{hafner2021dreamerv2} adopted discrete latent representations, and DreamerV3 \cite{hafner2023dreamerv3} achieved robust cross-domain performance through symlog transforms and fixed hyperparameters. An alternative paradigm, TD-MPC2 \cite{hansen2024tdmpc2}, uses learned dynamics for online model-predictive control (MPC) rather than imagination-based actor-critic training---a distinction central to our analysis. For temporal abstraction within world models, Director \cite{hafner2022director} extends Dreamer with hierarchical planning via learned subgoals, THICK \cite{gumbsch2024thick} discovers adaptive temporal abstractions via context-change detection in discrete latent dynamics, and Hieros \cite{mattes2024hieros} constructs multi-scale imagination hierarchies with structured state-space models. Our experiments build on DreamerV3 as the base system, inheriting its RSSM dynamics, symlog value transforms, and default hyperparameters. The field's convergence toward hierarchy-based temporal abstraction---rather than single-scale variable-$dt$ approaches---motivates our diagnostic question: why does the simpler approach of variable-$dt$ imagination fail?

\paragraph{Temporal conditioning in world models.}
Most directly related to our work, TAWM \cite{nhu2025tawm} explicitly conditions a world model on time-step size $\Delta t$ and trains over diverse $\Delta t$ values, demonstrating improved performance and data efficiency on continuous control tasks. Critically, TAWM is built on TD-MPC2---an MPC-based planner that re-plans from real environment states at each decision step. Our work reveals that the same $\Delta t$-conditioning mechanism becomes pathological in imagination-based actor-critic training (DreamerV3), where the critic trains on complete imagined rollouts without grounding in real states. This distinction is the central axis of our contribution: \emph{$\Delta t$-conditioning is not inherently broken; the vulnerability is specific to imagination-based actor-critic training, where accumulated model imprecision at $dt>1$ is amplified by the learned conditioning into systematic value overestimation.} The architectural difference is decisive: in MPC, each planning step starts from a real observed state, limiting error accumulation to the local planning horizon; in imagination-based actor-critic training, the critic's value estimates propagate through the entire imagined rollout, and the actor optimizes against these estimates without any real-state anchoring, creating a positive feedback loop between imprecise $dt>1$ predictions and policy optimization. LS-Imagine \cite{li2025lsimagine} extends Dreamer's imagination horizon via goal-conditioned jumpy transitions, and MTS3 \cite{shaj2023mts3} proposes multi-time-scale state-space models for variable-rate dynamics prediction, but neither addresses the downstream value overestimation we identify in imagination-based actor-critic training.

\paragraph{Value overestimation in model-based RL.}
Value overestimation from function approximation was first identified in early RL work \cite{thrun1993issues} and has motivated a range of mitigation strategies, including clipped double-Q estimation \cite{fujimoto2018td3} and entropy regularization \cite{haarnoja2018sac}. In model-based RL, overestimation takes a distinct form: the agent can exploit inaccuracies in the learned dynamics to find spuriously high-value imagined trajectories. MBPO \cite{janner2019mbpo} formalizes this model exploitation and bounds the policy improvement guarantee by model error, while MOPO \cite{yu2020mopo} proposes pessimistic reward penalties scaled by model uncertainty. Recent work demonstrates that even oracle dynamics yield diminishing returns with longer rollout horizons \cite{palenicek2025diminishing}, and that trajectory-sampling rollouts can diverge from ground truth within a few steps \cite{frauenknecht2025rollouts}, suggesting that the bottleneck lies in value estimation rather than dynamics quality. Independent evidence from offline RL confirms that temporal extension via action sequences similarly induces value overestimation in actor-critic algorithms \cite{kim2025deas}.

Our conditioning-induced value overestimation (CIVO) is related to but mechanistically distinct from the delusional bias formalized by \cite{lu2018nondelusional} and further addressed by \cite{su2020conqur}. Delusional bias arises when the Q-update uses a maximizing action that the constrained policy class cannot execute, producing unrealizable value estimates---a property of the policy-value interaction. CIVO instead arises from model imprecision amplified by learned temporal conditioning: the trained $dt$-embedding enables the critic to produce precise but incorrect value estimates for $dt>1$ transitions where the RSSM's prediction quality is insufficient to support such precision. The mechanism is architectural rather than representational: the $dt$-embedding allows the value function to \emph{condition on} temporal information that the dynamics model cannot \emph{deliver on}, creating a systematic positive bias for temporally extended predictions.

Within the world-model training pipeline, gradient flow analysis reveals a structural asymmetry relevant to this mechanism. DreamerV3's own ablation study shows that performance predominantly rests on unsupervised reconstruction loss rather than reward or value gradients \cite{hafner2023dreamerv3}, and HarmonyDream \cite{ma2024harmonydream} quantifies that the reward loss is two orders of magnitude smaller than the observation loss across visually demanding domains. Using per-head gradient decomposition in the spirit of multi-task gradient analysis \cite{chen2018gradnorm}, we extend these observations quantitatively: the $dt$-embedding receives 93\% of its gradient from observation decoders, 7\% from KL dynamics, and 0\% from the reward head---establishing that the temporal conditioning is shaped entirely by observation reconstruction, not reward prediction. This gradient structure explains why reward-targeted interventions (e.g., stop-gradient on the reward head's contribution to the $dt$-embedding) are architectural no-ops.

\paragraph{Multi-step prediction error.}
Compounding prediction error in learned dynamics is a well-studied challenge: multi-step models can bypass sequential error accumulation \cite{asadi2019combating}, incorporating long-term future into training objectives improves dynamics learning \cite{ke2019learning}, and empirical analysis characterizes how prediction errors compound across rollout steps \cite{lambert2022investigating}. Our factorial experiments rule out compounding error as the primary cause of the observed performance degradation. The controlled comparison between trained and untrained $dt$-embedding configurations---with identical dynamics architectures and rollout lengths---isolates the temporal conditioning mechanism rather than multi-step prediction quality as the driver of collapse. Compounding error degrades prediction fidelity uniformly across the trajectory; CIVO selectively corrupts value estimation for conditioned $dt>1$ transitions while leaving $dt=1$ estimates relatively intact.

\paragraph{Diagnostic methodology.}
Our work follows a tradition of diagnostic studies in deep RL that reveal how specific design choices---rather than algorithmic innovations---determine empirical outcomes. Large-scale factorial studies have shown that implementation details drive most of PPO's gains over TRPO \cite{engstrom2020implementation}, and that non-obvious interactions among design choices shape actor-critic performance in ways that standard evaluations miss \cite{andrychowicz2021whatmatters}. We adopt a similar factorial approach, using a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ experiment grid to isolate the contributions of $dt$-embedding training status, temporal subsampling, and imagination horizon to the observed failure mode. Non-method contributions of this kind---identifying \emph{why} a widely-attempted approach fails rather than proposing a fix---are increasingly recognized as valuable when they address poorly understood failure modes and provide actionable insight for future method design. The resulting causal chain---from trained $dt$-embedding to conditioning-induced value overestimation to stochastic performance collapse---exemplifies how controlled diagnostic analysis can identify specific architectural vulnerabilities that standard benchmark evaluations cannot detect.
