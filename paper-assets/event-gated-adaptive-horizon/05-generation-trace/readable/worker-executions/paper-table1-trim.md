161	\subsection{Online CIVO Detection}
162	\label{sec:detector}
163	
164	\paragraph{Motivation.}
165	The Normalized Value Excess ($\nve$) quantifies CIVO severity by comparing the value-to-evaluation ratio against a reference baseline that does not employ temporal abstraction. This comparison is informative for post-hoc diagnosis but unusable during deployment: no practitioner has access to a converged baseline run while training a new agent. A metric that requires a completed control experiment cannot serve as an early warning.
166	
167	We therefore ask whether the \emph{dynamics} of value overestimation, rather than its absolute magnitude relative to an external reference, contain sufficient signal to detect CIVO onset in real time. By tracking the trajectory of the value-to-evaluation ratio $r_t = \bar{V}_t / \max(\bar{E}_t, \varepsilon)$ (with $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$ for numerical stability) across checkpoints, a lightweight rule-based detector can flag dangerous overestimation using only quantities available within a single run.
168	
169	The causal structure of CIVO motivates acceleration as the key detection signal. CIVO operates as a positive feedback loop: the trained embedding biases reward predictions, which inflate value estimates, which in turn distort actor gradients toward trajectories that reinforce the bias (\S\ref{sec:civo_mechanism}). This self-amplifying cycle produces superlinear growth in $r_t$. In healthy training, a natural negative feedback counteracts value growth: improved value estimates yield a better actor, which raises evaluation returns, stabilizing the ratio. CIVO breaks this negative feedback, so sustained positive acceleration in $r_t$ serves as a causal signature that distinguishes pathological divergence from normal value learning.
170	
171	\paragraph{Algorithm.}
172	The detector monitors $r_t$ and fires an alert only when four conditions hold simultaneously at checkpoint $t$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:civo_detector}). The conjunction makes the detector conservative: any single noisy checkpoint is unlikely to satisfy magnitude, velocity, acceleration, and decline together. The computational cost is negligible (four scalar comparisons per checkpoint, no additional model inference).
173	
174	\begin{algorithm}[t]
175	\caption{Online CIVO Detector}
176	\label{alg:civo_detector}
177	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
178	\REQUIRE Stream of $(\bar{V}_t, \bar{E}_t)$ at each checkpoint $t$
179	\STATE $\tau_\text{mag} \leftarrow 0.49$, \quad $\tau_\text{vel} \leftarrow 0.1$, \quad $\varepsilon \leftarrow 10^{-6}$
180	\STATE Initialize $r_\text{prev} \leftarrow 0$, $\Delta r_\text{prev} \leftarrow 0$, $\mathcal{H} \leftarrow []$
181	\FOR{each checkpoint $t$}
182	  \STATE $r_t \leftarrow \bar{V}_t \,/\, \max(\bar{E}_t,\, \varepsilon)$
183	  \STATE $\Delta r_t \leftarrow r_t - r_\text{prev}$
184	  \STATE $c_\text{mag} \leftarrow (r_t \geq \tau_\text{mag})$
185	  \STATE $c_\text{vel} \leftarrow (\Delta r_t > \tau_\text{vel})$
186	  \STATE $c_\text{acc} \leftarrow (\Delta r_\text{prev} > \tau_\text{vel}) \wedge (\Delta r_t > \Delta r_\text{prev})$
187	  \STATE $c_\text{dec} \leftarrow \bigl|\{i : \mathcal{H}[i] < \mathcal{H}[i{-}1],\; i \in \text{last 3}\}\bigr| \geq 2$
188	  \IF{$c_\text{mag} \wedge c_\text{vel} \wedge c_\text{acc} \wedge c_\text{dec}$}
189	    \STATE \textbf{Alert:} CIVO onset at step $t$
190	  \ENDIF
191	  \STATE $r_\text{prev} \leftarrow r_t$, \quad $\Delta r_\text{prev} \leftarrow \Delta r_t$
192	  \STATE Append $\bar{E}_t$ to $\mathcal{H}$
193	\ENDFOR
194	\end{algorithmic}
195	\end{algorithm}
196	
197	The four conditions capture complementary signals: (1)~\emph{Magnitude} ($r_t \geq 0.49$) confirms that the value-to-evaluation ratio has reached a level indicative of overestimation; (2)~\emph{Velocity} ($\Delta r_t > 0.1$) rules out slow drift or plateau behavior; (3)~\emph{Acceleration} ($\Delta r_t > \Delta r_{t-1}$, both $> 0.1$) filters oscillatory paths where the ratio rises and falls without sustained divergence; (4)~\emph{Evaluation decline} ($\geq 2$ of 3 recent checkpoints decline) confirms that overestimation coincides with actual performance degradation. A 2-of-3 majority threshold balances noise robustness against single-checkpoint evaluation variance with sensitivity to sustained decline.\footnote{A full comparison of window sizes (3/3, 2/3, 2/5) and their trade-offs appears in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_sensitivity}.}
198	
199	Sensitivity analysis over $\tau_\text{mag} \in [0.30, 0.70]$ confirms that $\tau_\text{mag} = 0.49$ maximizes Youden's $J = 0.714$; raising the threshold to $0.50$ loses one true positive (seed~3 triggers at $r_t = 0.497$) with no reduction in false positives, since both false alarms have $r_t > 0.70$ at trigger time. The velocity threshold $\tau_\text{vel} = 0.1$ is stable across $[0.05, 0.18]$ (TPR remains $3/3$); we retain $0.1$ to avoid overfitting to $n{=}10$ runs.

174	\subsection{The CIVO Mechanism}
175	\label{sec:civo_mechanism}
176	
177	The RSSM fidelity analysis (Table~\ref{tab:rssm_fidelity}) reveals the mechanistic core of CIVO.  The trained $\dt$-embedding enables the reward head to learn temporally differentiated predictions, but these predictions are asymmetrically accurate: reward MAE at $\dt{=}1$ is $0.0568$, while at $\dt{=}2$ it rises to $0.1579$, a $2.78\times$ gap.  This gap is not an artifact of prediction difficulty alone; the no-embedding configuration (008f) exhibits a $1.01\times$ gap, and the baseline shows $1.56\times$.  The trained embedding actively amplifies the fidelity discrepancy by allowing the reward head to co-adapt with the temporal signal.
178	
179	This asymmetric fidelity initiates a feedback loop with four stages: (1) the trained $\dt$-embedding causes RSSM predictions at $\dt > 1$ to carry systematically biased reward estimates; (2) the critic, trained entirely on imagined trajectories, internalizes these biased rewards as optimistic value estimates; (3) the actor exploits the overestimated regions of the value landscape, shifting its policy toward states that appear high-value only under the biased model; and (4) this policy shift further skews the distribution of imagined states, reinforcing the bias.  The diagnostic signature is a sustained $\nve$ above $1.5\times$, followed by evaluation collapse.
180	
181	A random-policy control experiment ($N{=}100$ rollouts, 95\% bootstrap CI) decomposes CIVO into two necessary stages.  \textbf{Stage~1 (dynamics bias):} even under a random policy, $\dt{=}2$ imagination produces $1.68\times$ higher mean reward than $\dt{=}1$ in the trained-embedding model (008d): the RSSM prior already generates reward-favorable trajectories at $\dt > 1$, independent of actor behavior.  \textbf{Stage~2 (actor exploitation):} the trained actor learns to exploit these biased regions, amplifying the ratio to $2.90\times$.  In the no-embedding control (008f), neither stage is present: the random-policy ratio is $1.11\times$ and the trained-actor ratio is $0.98\times$.  The decomposition attributes $\approx 36\%$ of the total effect to dynamics bias and $\approx 64\%$ to actor exploitation, confirming that both stages are necessary for CIVO to manifest.
182	
183	This mechanism is analogous to delusional bias \citep{lu2018nondelusional}, but differs in origin: delusional bias arises from policy-class constraints, while CIVO is model-induced: the reward head's co-adaptation with the temporal embedding produces systematically upward-biased predictions at $\dt > 1$.  The consequence (policy exploitation of overestimated regions) is similar, but the causal pathway is distinct.

Full-SGS targets this causal chain by preventing observation-decoder gradients from updating $e_{\Delta t}$, though its 50\% prevention rate (1/2 seeds) indicates that breaking the gradient pathway alone does not fully eliminate the vulnerability.

226	\subsubsection{Implications for the CIVO Mechanism}
227	\label{sec:sgs_implications}
228	
229	The gradient decomposition reveals that gradient magnitude and causal importance are decoupled in CIVO.
230	
231	\paragraph{Gradient importance $\neq$ gradient magnitude.} The reward head contributes the largest gradient share (38--49\%) to $e_{\Delta t}$, yet reward-only SGS fails.  The observation decoder contributes the smallest share (16--22\%), yet its removal (via Full-SGS) is necessary for CIVO mitigation.  This dissociation arises because the reward head's gradient, while large, does not encode $\dt$-specific structural information: it adjusts $e_{\Delta t}$ to minimize a scalar prediction error that is largely $\dt$-invariant.  The observation decoder's gradient, though smaller, encodes how the RSSM should differentially reconstruct 24-dimensional proprioceptive states across temporal scales. This is the channel through which $e_{\Delta t}$ learns scale-dependent latent biases.
232	
233	\paragraph{Reframing the intervention target.} Any gradient-level strategy that targets the reward head alone (reward prediction regularization, reward-conditioned value clipping, or reward-only SGS) targets the largest pathway but not the informationally critical one.  The failure of exp\_016e (NVE $10.42\times$) despite detaching the dominant gradient source confirms this.  Full-pathway SGS (exp\_018) detaches $e_{\Delta t}$ from all non-KL gradients, retaining only the KL dynamics contribution (34--40\%).  However, this intervention yields mixed results: seed~1 avoids collapse (eval 448.3) while seed~2 collapses (eval 40.8), indicating that removing the observation decoder gradient channel is necessary but not sufficient.  The 50\% prevention rate suggests that CIVO involves additional mechanisms (e.g., forward-pass conditioning effects, residual capacity-dependent instabilities) beyond those capturable by gradient-level interventions alone.
234	
235	\paragraph{Connection to RSSM fidelity analysis.} The trained-embedding condition (exp\_008d) shows a $2.78\times$ reward gap between $\Delta t{=}1$ and $\Delta t{=}2$ transitions (Table~\ref{tab:rssm_fidelity}).  This gap arises because the observation decoder gradients shape $e_{\Delta t}$ to produce latent states that are spatially biased at $\dt{>}1$, and the reward head inherits these biases \textit{through the latent state} rather than learning them directly.  The reward head's large gradient contribution reflects sensitivity to $e_{\Delta t}$'s parameters but not causal responsibility for the fidelity gap.

273	\subsection{Why MPC Is Immune}
274	\label{sec:mpc_immune}
275	
276	\citet{nhu2025tawm} show that $\dt$-conditioning benefits TD-MPC2, raising the question of why it fails in DreamerV3.  The critical difference is that MPC re-plans from real states at every step, so imagination errors do not accumulate into the training signal because there is no critic bootstrapping from imagined trajectories.  In DreamerV3, the actor-critic trains entirely within imagination, and any systematic reward-prediction bias at $\dt > 1$ is amplified through the closed actor-critic loop.

Restricting imagination to $\dt{=}1$ while retaining the trained embedding (008e\_v3) prevents CIVO in 1 of 2 seeds but the other collapses via Pathway~B (\S\ref{sec:f6}).  Neither approach alone provides a complete solution.

283	\subsection{Multiple Vulnerability Pathways}
284	\label{sec:pathways}
285	
286	The two collapse mechanisms identified in \S\ref{sec:f6}, CIVO (Pathway~A, value-driven, entropy decreasing) and non-CIVO instability (Pathway~B, eval-driven, entropy increasing), have distinct diagnostic signatures but a shared root cause: the trained $\dt$-embedding's learned features affect both reward prediction across temporal scales (Pathway~A) and the representation space in which the actor operates (Pathway~B).  This explains why $\nve$ is sensitive but not perfectly specific: Pathway~B's late evaluation decline inflates $\nve$ through the same arithmetic as CIVO, yielding the sole false positive ($3.78\times$ for 008e\_v3 s2).  Distinguishing the two requires examining the temporal profile and entropy direction of collapse.

288	\subsection{Implications for Temporal Abstraction in World Models}
289	\label{sec:implications}
290	
291	CIVO identifies a structural incompatibility between flat temporal conditioning and imagination-based actor-critic training.  Hierarchical approaches (THICK \citep{gumbsch2024thick}, Hieros \citep{mattes2024hieros}, LS-Imagine \citep{li2025lsimagine}) may avoid this by maintaining separate dynamics per temporal scale.  Whether CIVO transfers to transformer-based world models (DreamerV4; \citealt{hafner2025dreamerv4}) depends on whether attention weights co-adapt with temporal embeddings analogously.

The trained $\\dt$-embedding enables the reward head to learn temporally differentiated predictions, but these predictions are asymmetrically accurate: reward MAE at $\\dt{=}1$ is $0.0568$, while at $\\dt{=}2$ it rises to $0.1579$, a $2.78\\times$ gap.  This gap is not an artifact of prediction difficulty alone; the no-embedding configuration (008f) exhibits a $1.01\\times$ gap, and the baseline shows $1.56\\times$.  The trained embedding actively amplifies the fidelity discrepancy by allowing the reward head to co-adapt with the temporal signal.\n\nThis asymmetric fidelity initiates a feedback loop with four stages: (1) the trained $\\dt$-embedding causes RSSM predictions at $\\dt > 1$ to carry systematically biased reward estimates; (2) the critic, trained entirely on imagined trajectories, internalizes these biased rewards as optimistic value estimates; (3) the actor exploits the overestimated regions of the value landscape, shifting its policy toward states that appear high-value only under the biased model; and (4) this policy shift further skews the distribution of imagined states, reinforcing the bias.  The diagnostic signature is a sustained $\\nve$ above $1.5\\times$, followed by evaluation collapse.\n\nA random-policy control experiment ($N{=}100$ rollouts, 95\\% bootstrap CI) decomposes CIVO into two necessary stages.  \\textbf{Stage~1 (dynamics bias):} even under a random policy, $\\dt{=}2$ imagination produces $1.68\\times$ higher mean reward than $\\dt{=}1$ in the trained-embedding model (008d): the RSSM prior already generates reward-favorable trajectories at $\\dt > 1$, independent of actor behavior.  \\textbf{Stage~2 (actor exploitation):} the trained actor learns to exploit these biased regions, amplifying the ratio to $2.90\\times$.  In the no-embedding control (008f), neither stage is present: the random-policy ratio is $1.11\\times$ and the trained-actor ratio is $0.98\\times$.  The decomposition attributes $\\approx 36\\%$ of the total effect to dynamics bias and $\\approx 64\\%$ to actor exploitation, confirming that both stages are necessary for CIVO to manifest.\n\nThis mechanism is analogous to delusional bias \\citep{lu2018nondelusional}, but differs in origin: delusional bias arises from policy-class constraints, while CIVO is model-induced: the reward head's co-adaptation with the temporal embedding produces systematically upward-biased predictions at $\\dt > 1$.
