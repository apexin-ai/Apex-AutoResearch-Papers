161	\subsection{F3: Unexposed Temporal Embedding Suppresses Value Drift}
162	\label{sec:f3}
163	
164	\begin{figure}[t]
165	\centering
166	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/paper/fig3_factorial_grid.pdf}
167	\caption{Factorial isolation of CIVO trigger. Only the combination of trained $e_{\dt}$ and multi-rate data (upper-left cell) produces pathological NVE. Neither factor alone is sufficient.}
168	\label{fig:factorial_grid}
169	\end{figure}
170	
171	The causal claim that trained embedding \emph{plus} multi-rate exposure jointly trigger CIVO rests on two complementary controls that each remove one factor while holding the other constant.
172	
173	\paragraph{Evidence A: exposure is necessary (exp\_008a).}
174	Exp\_008a uses a trained embedding ($d{=}32$, gradients enabled) that is never exposed to $\dt{>}1$ transitions during world-model training (subsample OFF). Despite sharing the same embedding architecture as exp\_008d, its NVE remains in the $0.59$--$0.93\times$ range across 105K steps. Performance follows two V-shaped recovery cycles, reaching 777.7 at 105K (85\% of baseline). The critical trigger is not the embedding's capacity to represent temporal information, but its adaptation to multi-rate data that enables precise but inaccurate $\dt{>}1$ state predictions within the RSSM.
175	
176	This contrasts with exp\_006 (trained $e_{\dt}$, subsample ON, $H{=}15$), where NVE accelerates from $1.61\times$ at 45K to $2.31\times$ at 55K before catastrophic collapse to 132.9 at 65K. Both experiments use identical trained embeddings ($d{=}32$, gradients enabled) and $H{=}15$, differing only in whether the world model sees $\dt{>}1$ transitions during training, confirming that multi-rate data exposure, not embedding architecture, is the operative variable.
177	
178	\paragraph{Evidence B: trainability is necessary (exp\_015b).}
179	Exp\_015b uses a frozen random-projection embedding ($d{=}32$, \texttt{requires\_grad=False}, subsample ON, $H{=}8$), the same multi-rate data regime as exp\_008d, but with a non-trainable temporal signal. Despite full exposure to $\dt{>}1$ transitions, the NVE peaks at $1.45\times$ at 55K but does not sustain escalation: it retreats to $0.65\times$ by 65K and settles at $0.55\times$ by 105K (trajectory: $0.81 \rightarrow 1.26 \rightarrow 1.45 \rightarrow 0.65 \rightarrow 1.05 \rightarrow 0.60 \rightarrow 0.59 \rightarrow 0.55$). The transient peak approaches but does not exceed the $1.5\times$ CIVO threshold, and critically, the ratio self-corrects rather than exhibiting the monotonic escalation seen in trained-embedding runs. Final evaluation reaches only 204.2 at 105K, low but through a qualitatively different failure mode than CIVO. This demonstrates that a $\dt$-embedding must be able to \emph{co-adapt} with RSSM dynamics to produce the systematic reward-prediction bias that drives CIVO; a static random projection, regardless of its exposure to multi-rate data, cannot create this bias.

234	\subsection{The CIVO Mechanism}
235	\label{sec:civo_mechanism}
236	
237	The RSSM fidelity analysis (Table~\ref{tab:rssm_fidelity}) reveals the mechanistic core of CIVO.  The trained $\dt$-embedding enables the reward head to learn temporally differentiated predictions, but these predictions are asymmetrically accurate: reward MAE at $\dt{=}1$ is $0.0568$, while at $\dt{=}2$ it rises to $0.1579$, a $2.78\times$ gap.  This gap is not an artifact of prediction difficulty alone; the no-embedding configuration (008f) exhibits a $1.01\times$ gap, and the baseline shows $1.56\times$.  The trained embedding actively amplifies the fidelity discrepancy by allowing the reward head to co-adapt with the temporal signal.
238	
239	This asymmetric fidelity initiates a feedback loop with four stages: (1) the trained $\dt$-embedding causes RSSM predictions at $\dt > 1$ to carry systematically biased reward estimates; (2) the critic, trained entirely on imagined trajectories, internalizes these biased rewards as optimistic value estimates; (3) the actor exploits the overestimated regions of the value landscape, shifting its policy toward states that appear high-value only under the biased model; and (4) this policy shift further skews the distribution of imagined states, reinforcing the bias.  The diagnostic signature is a sustained $\nve$ above $1.5\times$, followed by evaluation collapse.
240	
241	A random-policy control experiment ($N{=}100$ rollouts, 95\% bootstrap CI) decomposes CIVO into two necessary stages.  \textbf{Stage~1 (dynamics bias):} even under a random policy, $\dt{=}2$ imagination produces $1.68\times$ higher mean reward than $\dt{=}1$ in the trained-embedding model (008d): the RSSM prior already generates reward-favorable trajectories at $\dt > 1$, independent of actor behavior.  \textbf{Stage~2 (actor exploitation):} the trained actor learns to exploit these biased regions, amplifying the ratio to $2.90\times$.  In the no-embedding control (008f), neither stage is present: the random-policy ratio is $1.11\times$ and the trained-actor ratio is $0.98\times$.  The decomposition attributes $\approx 36\%$ of the total effect to dynamics bias and $\approx 64\%$ to actor exploitation, confirming that both stages are necessary for CIVO to manifest.
242	
243	This mechanism is analogous to delusional bias \citep{lu2018nondelusional}, but differs in origin: delusional bias arises from policy-class constraints, while CIVO is model-induced: the reward head's co-adaptation with the temporal embedding produces systematically upward-biased predictions at $\dt > 1$.  The consequence (policy exploitation of overestimated regions) is similar, but the causal pathway is distinct.
244	
245	\subsection{Selective Gradient Stopping and Gradient Decomposition}
246	\label{sec:sgs_gradient_analysis}
247	
248	\subsubsection{Gradient Decomposition Results}
249	\label{sec:gradient_decomposition}
250

Table~\ref{tab:gradient_decomposition} reports the results.

280	The gradient decomposition reveals a counterintuitive structure: the reward head provides the largest gradient contribution (49.0\% at 55K, 38.1\% at 112K), yet reward-only SGS (exp\_016e) fails catastrophically (NVE $10.42\times$). The observation decoder channel contributes the smallest share (16.3\% at 55K, 21.5\% at 112K) yet carries the critical $\dt$-specific spatial information.  This dissociation between gradient magnitude and causal importance explains why exp\_016e, which targets the largest pathway, is functionally insufficient for CIVO prevention.
281	
282	The gradient structure is relatively stable across training (reward shifts from 49\% to 38\%, obs from 16\% to 22\%, KL from 35\% to 40\%), unlike what a magnitude-based account would predict. The reward head's large gradient contribution reflects the scalar reward prediction task's sensitivity to temporal embedding parameters, but this gradient does not encode $\Delta t$-specific structural information about spatial dynamics. The observation decoder channel, despite its smaller magnitude, is the only pathway that encodes how the RSSM should differentially predict multi-dimensional proprioceptive states across temporal scales.
283	
284	\paragraph{Why the smallest channel is causally critical.} The observation decoder is the only loss component whose gradients encode how the RSSM should differentially predict multi-dimensional proprioceptive states ($14$ orientations, $9$ velocities, $1$ height) across temporal scales.  This $\dt$-specific spatial information shapes the latent state representation in ways that propagate to reward prediction: the RSSM fidelity gap ($2.78\times$ reward MAE ratio, Table~\ref{tab:rssm_fidelity}) arises because the temporal embedding learns to produce latent states that are spatially biased at $\dt{>}1$, not because the reward head directly learns scale-dependent biases.  Full-SGS targets this causal chain by preventing observation-decoder gradients from updating $e_{\Delta t}$, though its 50\% recovery rate (1/2 seeds) indicates that breaking the gradient pathway alone does not fully eliminate the vulnerability.
285	
286	\subsubsection{Implications for the CIVO Mechanism}
287	\label{sec:sgs_implications}
288	
289	The gradient decomposition reveals that gradient magnitude and causal importance are decoupled in CIVO.
290	
291	\paragraph{Gradient importance $\neq$ gradient magnitude.} The reward head contributes the largest gradient share (38--49\%) to $e_{\Delta t}$, yet reward-only SGS fails.  The observation decoder contributes the smallest share (16--22\%), yet its removal (via Full-SGS) is necessary for CIVO mitigation.  This dissociation arises because the reward head's gradient, while large, does not encode $\dt$-specific structural information: it adjusts $e_{\Delta t}$ to minimize a scalar prediction error that is largely $\dt$-invariant.  The observation decoder's gradient, though smaller, encodes how the RSSM should differentially reconstruct 24-dimensional proprioceptive states across temporal scales. This is the channel through which $e_{\Delta t}$ learns scale-dependent latent biases.
292	
293	\paragraph{Reframing the intervention target.} Any gradient-level strategy that targets the reward head alone (reward prediction regularization, reward-conditioned value clipping, or reward-only SGS) targets the largest pathway but not the informationally critical one.  The failure of exp\_016e (NVE $10.42\times$) despite detaching the dominant gradient source confirms this.  Full-pathway SGS (exp\_018) detaches $e_{\Delta t}$ from all non-KL gradients, retaining only the KL dynamics contribution (34--40\%).  However, this intervention yields mixed results: seed~1 avoids collapse (eval 448.3) while seed~2 collapses (eval 40.8), indicating that removing the observation decoder gradient channel is necessary but not sufficient.
294	
295	Cross-environment validation on Cheetah Run (exp\_021) further clarifies the role of Full-SGS.  The intervention did not prevent the initial CIVO onset: NVE peaked at $9.94\times$ at 45K (comparable to the unmitigated exp\_010b's $8.27\times$).  However, unlike exp\_010b, which collapsed permanently to eval 51.7, exp\_021 self-corrected within ${\sim}$10K steps, with sustained NVE dropping to $0.34\times$ (55K--105K) and final evaluation recovering to 221.5.  This pattern reframes Full-SGS as \emph{enabling recovery from CIVO} rather than preventing its onset: the gradient detachment constrains value growth after the transient spike, allowing the critic to recalibrate.  Performance nonetheless remains at 38.7\% of baseline (572.5), likely because Full-SGS over-aggressively blocks actor gradients (actor gradient norms near zero throughout training).  Combined with exp\_018's mixed Walker results (1/2 seeds), these findings indicate that gradient-level interventions alone do not fully eliminate the CIVO vulnerability, and that additional mechanisms (e.g., forward-pass conditioning effects, residual capacity-dependent instabilities) operate beyond the gradient pathway.
296	
297	\paragraph{Connection to RSSM fidelity analysis.} The trained-embedding condition (exp\_008d) shows a $2.78\times$ reward gap between $\Delta t{=}1$ and $\Delta t{=}2$ transitions (Table~\ref{tab:rssm_fidelity}).  This gap arises because the observation decoder gradients shape $e_{\Delta t}$ to produce latent states that are spatially biased at $\dt{>}1$, and the reward head inherits these biases \textit{through the latent state} rather than learning them directly.  The reward head's large gradient contribution reflects sensitivity to $e_{\Delta t}$'s parameters but not causal responsibility for the fidelity gap.

335	\subsection{Why MPC Is Immune}
336	\label{sec:mpc_immune}
337	
338	\citet{nhu2025tawm} show that $\dt$-conditioning benefits TD-MPC2, raising the question of why it fails in DreamerV3.  The critical difference is that MPC re-plans from real states at every step, so imagination errors do not accumulate into the training signal because there is no critic bootstrapping from imagined trajectories.  In DreamerV3, the actor-critic trains entirely within imagination, and any systematic reward-prediction bias at $\dt > 1$ is amplified through the closed actor-critic loop.

340	\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$\dt$}{dt}-Embedding Dilemma}
341	\label{sec:embedding_dilemma}
342	
343	The factorial results reveal a fundamental tension: removing the $\dt$-embedding (008f) eliminates CIVO but at a $13\times$ cost to reward prediction quality ($0.7469$ vs.\ $0.0568$ MAE), as the world model cannot distinguish temporal scales.  Restricting imagination to $\dt{=}1$ while retaining the trained embedding (008e\_v3) avoids CIVO in 1 of 2 seeds but the other collapses via Pathway~B (\S\ref{sec:f6}).  Neither approach alone provides a complete solution.
344	
345	\subsection{Multiple Vulnerability Pathways}
346	\label{sec:pathways}
347	
348	The two collapse mechanisms identified in \S\ref{sec:f6}, CIVO (Pathway~A, value-driven, entropy decreasing) and non-CIVO instability (Pathway~B, eval-driven, entropy increasing), have distinct diagnostic signatures but a shared root cause: the trained $\dt$-embedding's learned features affect both reward prediction across temporal scales (Pathway~A) and the representation space in which the actor operates (Pathway~B).  This explains why $\nve$ is sensitive but not perfectly specific: Pathway~B's late evaluation decline inflates $\nve$ through the same arithmetic as CIVO, yielding the sole false positive ($3.78\times$ for 008e\_v3 s2).  Distinguishing the two requires examining the temporal profile and entropy direction of collapse.
349	
350	\subsection{Implications for Temporal Abstraction in World Models}
351	\label{sec:implications}
352	
353	CIVO identifies a structural incompatibility between flat temporal conditioning and imagination-based actor-critic training.  Hierarchical approaches (THICK \citep{gumbsch2024thick}, Hieros \citep{mattes2024hieros}, LS-Imagine \citep{li2025lsimagine}) may avoid this by maintaining separate dynamics per temporal scale.  Whether CIVO transfers to transformer-based world models (DreamerV4; \citealt{hafner2025dreamerv4}) depends on whether attention weights co-adapt with temporal embeddings analogously.

The trained $\\dt$-embedding enables the reward head to learn temporally differentiated predictions, but these predictions are asymmetrically accurate: reward MAE at $\\dt{=}1$ is $0.0568$, while at $\\dt{=}2$ it rises to $0.1579$, a $2.78\\times$ gap.  This gap is not an artifact of prediction difficulty alone; the no-embedding configuration (008f) exhibits a $1.01\\times$ gap, and the baseline shows $1.56\\times$.  The trained embedding actively amplifies the fidelity discrepancy by allowing the reward head to co-adapt with the temporal signal.\n\nThis asymmetric fidelity initiates a feedback loop with four stages: (1) the trained $\\dt$-embedding causes RSSM predictions at $\\dt > 1$ to carry systematically biased reward estimates; (2) the critic, trained entirely on imagined trajectories, internalizes these biased rewards as optimistic value estimates; (3) the actor exploits the overestimated regions of the value landscape, shifting its policy toward states that appear high-value only under the biased model; and (4) this policy shift further skews the distribution of imagined states, reinforcing the bias.  The diagnostic signature is a sustained $\\nve$ above $1.5\\times$, followed by evaluation collapse.\n\nA random-policy control experiment ($N{=}100$ rollouts, 95\\% bootstrap CI) decomposes CIVO into two necessary stages.  \\textbf{Stage~1 (dynamics bias):} even under a random policy, $\\dt{=}2$ imagination produces $1.68\\times$ higher mean reward than $\\dt{=}1$ in the trained-embedding model (008d): the RSSM prior already generates reward-favorable trajectories at $\\dt > 1$, independent of actor behavior.  \\textbf{Stage~2 (actor exploitation):} the trained actor learns to exploit these biased regions, amplifying the ratio to $2.90\\times$.  In the no-embedding control (008f), neither stage is present: the random-policy ratio is $1.11\\times$ and the trained-actor ratio is $0.98\\times$.  The decomposition attributes $\\approx 36\\%$ of the total effect to dynamics bias and $\\approx 64\\%$ to actor exploitation, confirming that both stages are necessary for CIVO to manifest.\n\nThis mechanism is analogous to delusional bias \\citep{lu2018nondelusional}, but differs in origin: delusional bias arises from policy-class constraints, while CIVO is model-induced: the reward head's co-adaptation with the temporal embedding produces systematically upward-biased predictions at $\\dt > 1$. 144	\subsection{Online CIVO Detection}
145	\label{sec:detector}
146	
147	\paragraph{Motivation.}
148	The Normalized Value Excess ($\nve$) quantifies CIVO severity by comparing the value-to-evaluation ratio against a reference baseline that does not employ temporal abstraction. This comparison is informative for post-hoc diagnosis but unusable during deployment: no practitioner has access to a converged baseline run while training a new agent. A metric that requires a completed control experiment cannot serve as an early warning.
149	
150	We therefore ask whether the \emph{dynamics} of value overestimation, rather than its absolute magnitude relative to an external reference, contain sufficient signal to detect CIVO onset in real time. By tracking the trajectory of the value-to-evaluation ratio $r_t = \bar{V}_t / \max(\bar{E}_t, \varepsilon)$ (with $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$ for numerical stability) across checkpoints, a lightweight rule-based detector can flag dangerous overestimation using only quantities available within a single run.
151	
152	The causal structure of CIVO motivates acceleration as the key detection signal. CIVO operates as a positive feedback loop: the trained embedding biases reward predictions, which inflate value estimates, which in turn distort actor gradients toward trajectories that reinforce the bias (\S\ref{sec:civo_mechanism}). This self-amplifying cycle produces superlinear growth in $r_t$. In healthy training, a natural negative feedback counteracts value growth: improved value estimates yield a better actor, which raises evaluation returns and stabilizes the ratio. CIVO breaks this negative feedback, so sustained positive acceleration in $r_t$ serves as a causal signature that distinguishes pathological divergence from normal value learning.
153	
154	\paragraph{Algorithm.}
155	The detector monitors $r_t$ and fires an alert only when four conditions hold simultaneously at checkpoint $t$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:civo_detector}). The conjunction makes the detector conservative: any single noisy checkpoint is unlikely to satisfy magnitude, velocity, acceleration, and decline together. The computational cost is negligible (four scalar comparisons per checkpoint, no additional model inference).
156	
157	\begin{algorithm}[t]
158	\caption{Online CIVO Detector}
159	\label{alg:civo_detector}
160	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
161	\REQUIRE Stream of $(\bar{V}_t, \bar{E}_t)$ at each checkpoint $t$
162	\STATE $\tau_\text{mag} \leftarrow 0.49$, \quad $\tau_\text{vel} \leftarrow 0.1$, \quad $\varepsilon \leftarrow 10^{-6}$
163	\STATE Initialize $r_\text{prev} \leftarrow 0$, $\Delta r_\text{prev} \leftarrow 0$, $\mathcal{H} \leftarrow []$
164	\FOR{each checkpoint $t$}
165	  \STATE $r_t \leftarrow \bar{V}_t \,/\, \max(\bar{E}_t,\, \varepsilon)$
166	  \STATE $\Delta r_t \leftarrow r_t - r_\text{prev}$
167	  \STATE $c_\text{mag} \leftarrow (r_t \geq \tau_\text{mag})$
168	  \STATE $c_\text{vel} \leftarrow (\Delta r_t > \tau_\text{vel})$
169	  \STATE $c_\text{acc} \leftarrow (\Delta r_\text{prev} > \tau_\text{vel}) \wedge (\Delta r_t > \Delta r_\text{prev})$
170	  \STATE $c_\text{dec} \leftarrow \bigl|\{i : \mathcal{H}[i] < \mathcal{H}[i{-}1],\; i \in \text{last 3}\}\bigr| \geq 2$
171	  \IF{$c_\text{mag} \wedge c_\text{vel} \wedge c_\text{acc} \wedge c_\text{dec}$}
172	    \STATE \textbf{Alert:} CIVO onset at step $t$
173	  \ENDIF
174	  \STATE $r_\text{prev} \leftarrow r_t$, \quad $\Delta r_\text{prev} \leftarrow \Delta r_t$
175	  \STATE Append $\bar{E}_t$ to $\mathcal{H}$
176	\ENDFOR
177	\end{algorithmic}
178	\end{algorithm}
179	
180	The four conditions capture complementary signals: (1)~\emph{Magnitude} ($r_t \geq 0.49$) confirms that the value-to-evaluation ratio has reached a level indicative of overestimation; (2)~\emph{Velocity} ($\Delta r_t > 0.1$) rules out slow drift or plateau behavior; (3)~\emph{Acceleration} ($\Delta r_t > \Delta r_{t-1}$, both $> 0.1$) filters oscillatory paths where the ratio rises and falls without sustained divergence; (4)~\emph{Evaluation decline} ($\geq 2$ of 3 recent checkpoints decline) confirms that overestimation coincides with actual performance degradation. A 2-of-3 majority threshold balances noise robustness against single-checkpoint evaluation variance with sensitivity to sustained decline.\footnote{A full comparison of window sizes (3/3, 2/3, 2/5) and their trade-offs appears in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_sensitivity}.}
181	
182	Sensitivity analysis over $\tau_\text{mag} \in [0.30, 0.70]$ confirms that $\tau_\text{mag} = 0.49$ maximizes Youden's $J = 0.714$; raising the threshold to $0.50$ loses one true positive (seed~3 triggers at $r_t = 0.497$) with no reduction in false positives, since both false alarms have $r_t > 0.70$ at trigger time. The velocity threshold $\tau_\text{vel} = 0.1$ is stable across $[0.05, 0.18]$ (TPR remains $3/3$); we retain $0.1$ to avoid overfitting to $n{=}11$ runs.
