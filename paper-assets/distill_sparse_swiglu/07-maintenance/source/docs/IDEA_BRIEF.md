## Core Problem

Training-free activation sparsity methods (WINA, TEAL, R-Sparse) have enabled 40-60% model-level neuron pruning on native SwiGLU LLMs, but leave a substantial quality gap: at 50% model-level activation sparsity on Llama-3-8B, WINA achieves 59.57% average across six standard benchmarks (PIQA, ARC-c, WinoGrande, HellaSwag, MMLU, GSM8K) versus the dense model's 66.38% — a 6.81-point drop, with reasoning-heavy tasks (GSM8K, MMLU) degrading disproportionately. Two recent learned approaches partially address this gap from complementary but individually incomplete directions. SPON (Resting Neurons, arXiv 2512.12744v3, ICML 2026 per arXiv metadata updated on the April 30 notification date) introduces static, input-independent learnable bias vectors trained via KL-divergence distillation, recovering ~1 point over WINA on five QA benchmarks (ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy, BoolQ, PIQA, Winogrande; 71.96 vs 70.97 weighted average) at zero inference overhead — demonstrating that output-distribution alignment helps, but leaving input-dependent importance patterns entirely unaddressed. FastForward (arXiv 2602.00397) trains contextual predictors with weighted BCE loss plus an error compensation network (2-layer MLP, r'=d_model/8) trained with MSE loss that operates at inference time, achieving MMLU 67.17 vs dense 67.84 at 50% FFN sparsity on Llama-3.1-8B — demonstrating that contextual prediction helps, but requiring the auxiliary compensation network to address the output-quality deficiency inherent in BCE's per-neuron objective. The compensation network adds ~8.0 MB of weights per layer (r'=d_model/8=512; two matrices of 4096×512, totaling 4,194,304 params × 2 bytes FP16 ≈ 8.0 MB) and associated GEMV operations at inference time — substantial overhead that motivates seeking training objectives that eliminate the need for compensation entirely. The critical insight is that BCE's training signal decomposes as L_BCE = Σ_j l(y_j, s_j), a per-neuron factorization that treats each neuron's activation decision as independent of all others. This cannot capture joint interaction effects — the fact that pruning neuron A may only degrade output quality when neuron B in the same or subsequent layer is also pruned. FastForward's error compensation network is an engineering fix for this limitation: it patches output errors post-hoc rather than training the predictor to avoid them. KL divergence on output logits, by contrast, backpropagates through the full sparse forward pass via ∂L_KL/∂θ = ∂L_KL/∂p_sparse · ∂p_sparse/∂h_out · ∂h_out/∂m · ∂m/∂θ, where the term ∂h_out/∂m captures how each neuron's mask decision jointly affects the output in combination with all other active neurons. This yields a testable prediction: KL's advantage over BCE should increase with sparsity level, because higher sparsity creates more simultaneous neuron removals and thus more pairwise and higher-order interaction terms that the per-neuron decomposition cannot model. However, DejaVu demonstrated >99% per-neuron prediction accuracy with pure BCE, suggesting the independent decomposition is a surprisingly good approximation at moderate sparsity — making the practical significance of joint interactions an empirical question that our ablation directly tests. A further open question is whether learned sparsity predictors trained on short calibration sequences generalize to long-context settings — SVD Contextual Sparsity Predictors (arXiv 2603.14110) explicitly found that learned predictors suffer from out-of-distribution sensitivity compared to weight-structure-based approaches, making long-context evaluation essential for any learned predictor method.

## Opportunity / Why Now

The landscape reveals four actionable opportunities. First, the composition question: SPON's static bias correction fixes average-case representational drift (input-independent) while contextual prediction handles per-input variation (input-dependent) — orthogonal mechanisms that should provide additive benefits. Note that IFPruning (arXiv 2501.02086) also combines quasi-static (instruction-level) and dynamic (input-level) sparsity, but through a fundamentally different mechanism: a 302M-parameter predictor that jointly fine-tunes the base LLM with cross-entropy loss, producing instruction-level masks (same mask for all tokens). Our composition is between an input-independent bias correction (zero overhead, folded into bias terms, applied to frozen models) and a lightweight token-level contextual predictor — a simpler, more modular, and post-training-compatible approach. If confirmed empirically, the SPON + contextual predictor composition becomes a practical recipe with a clear mechanistic explanation. Second, the training objective question: if a KL-trained predictor (no compensation) matches or exceeds the quality of FastForward's full BCE + compensation system, the practical benefit is substantial — a simpler inference pipeline with lower overhead. FastForward's compensation MLP adds ~8.0 MB of weights per layer (r'=d_model/8=512; 4,194,304 params in FP16) and associated GEMV operations at inference time — ~256 MB total across 32 layers; eliminating it simplifies deployment without sacrificing quality. This transforms the contribution from 'KL is slightly better than BCE' to 'KL training makes error compensation unnecessary.' Third, we observe that BCE's per-neuron decomposition L_BCE = Σ_j l(y_j, s_j) is analogous to a mean-field factorization that treats each neuron's activation decision as independent, ignoring joint interaction effects. At sparsity level s, the number of simultaneously pruned neuron pairs scales as O(s²D²), so the approximation gap grows quadratically with sparsity — yielding a testable prediction that KL's advantage should be substantially larger at 50% than at 40% sparsity. We note that this is an information-theoretic analogy rather than a formal variational inference procedure: BCE predictors achieve high per-neuron accuracy (>99% in DejaVu at moderate sparsity on ReLU models), so the practical significance of the interaction terms is an empirical question — which is exactly what the sparsity-level ablation tests. If confirmed, this provides actionable guidance: use KL when targeting aggressive sparsity levels where joint interactions dominate; BCE may suffice at conservative levels. Fourth, long-context generalization: learned sparsity predictors are trained on short calibration sequences, but deployed on inputs of arbitrary length. SVD Contextual Sparsity Predictors (arXiv 2603.14110) showed that learned predictors exhibit out-of-distribution sensitivity — our LongBench and MT-Bench evaluation directly tests whether KL-trained predictors (which optimize for output-distribution preservation rather than per-neuron pattern matching) exhibit better generalization than BCE-trained ones, and whether SPON's static correction provides a context-length-invariant baseline that stabilizes long-context performance. If time permits, RULER's synthetic tasks (needle-in-a-haystack retrieval, variable tracking, multi-hop tracing) at controlled context lengths provide fine-grained diagnostics complementing LongBench's natural-language tasks. The contribution is fivefold: (1) the first empirical study of whether static bias correction (SPON) and contextual prediction compose additively for activation sparsity — the headline result; (2) a controlled four-condition ablation (BCE, BCE+compensation, KL, KL+compensation) testing whether KL training subsumes error compensation; (3) a testable interaction prediction (KL advantage grows with sparsity) grounded in the analogy between BCE's per-neuron decomposition and mean-field factorization, with appropriate caveats about the empirical nature of the question; (4) a systematic three-tier comparison (training-free, static-learned, contextual-learned) on native SwiGLU at decode time with honest wall-clock overhead reporting; and (5) long-context generalization evaluation testing whether learned predictors trained on short sequences maintain quality on LongBench tasks (4K–32K tokens) and MT-Bench, addressing a gap identified by recent work on predictor OOD sensitivity.

## Landscape (SOTA & Limitations)

Training-free activation sparsity on SwiGLU has rapidly progressed: WINA (ICLR 2026) achieves the training-free SOTA with weight-informed scoring and TEAL's greedy per-layer sparsity allocation across both MLP and attention layers, reporting 59.57% average on Llama-3-8B at 50% model-level sparsity versus 66.38% dense; La RoSA (ICML 2025) uses layerwise orthogonal rotations with top-K selection to achieve consistent model-level sparsity (0.17 perplexity gap on Llama-2-7B at 40% sparsity, 1.30x wall-clock speedup), surpassing TEAL by 1.77% on zero-shot tasks without training or magnitude-based pruning; WAS (EMNLP 2025) achieves 60% model-level sparsity via weight-aware scoring with constrained Bayesian optimization; WiSparse (arXiv 2026) preserves 97% of dense performance at 50% sparsity via weight-aware mixed-granularity allocation; TEAL (ICLR 2025) achieves 40-50% sparsity with 1.5-1.8x speedup via magnitude pruning with greedy per-layer allocation; R-Sparse (ICLR 2025) achieves 50% model-level sparsity via rank-aware input channel scoring; ActTail (arXiv 2026) uses Heavy-Tailed Self-Regularization for global sparsity allocation; DIP (MLSys 2025) achieves predictor-free dynamic pruning on SwiGLU at ~40% speedup plus LoRA adapters trained via logit-matching knowledge distillation (loss function unspecified in paper, likely KL) for accuracy recovery — the closest existing system combining distillation with sparsity, though critically it trains the LoRA recovery module with distillation rather than the sparsity predictor itself, and uses magnitude-based (not predicted) sparsity masks. On the learned side, SPON/Resting Neurons (arXiv 2512.12744v3, ICML 2026 per arXiv metadata updated on the April 30 notification date) injects learnable input-independent activation vectors trained via KL-divergence distillation, recovering ~1 point over WINA at 50% model-level sparsity on five QA benchmarks (ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy, BoolQ, PIQA, Winogrande; 71.96 vs 70.97), with zero inference overhead since vectors fold into bias terms, training in ~1 hour on 4x A6000; SPON also reports perplexity on WikiText and validates cross-dataset generalization by calibrating on C4 while evaluating on WikiText. FastForward (arXiv 2602.00397, Jan 2026) trains a contextual predictor (single-head attention + 2-layer MLP, bottleneck r=d_model/16) with weighted BCE loss plus an error compensation network (2-layer MLP, r'=d_model/8=512) trained with MSE loss ||Y_dense − Y_sparse||², achieving MMLU 67.17 vs dense 67.84 at 50% FFN sparsity on Llama-3.1-8B — critically, the compensation network operates at inference time (not just training), adding ~8.0 MB per layer (4,194,304 params × 2 bytes FP16, ~256 MB total across 32 layers) in per-layer weight reads and compute overhead. FastForward targets prefill optimization and evaluates on LongBench (English tasks, 4K-32K tokens) achieving <6% accuracy loss at 50% FFN sparsity — demonstrating that their predictor generalizes beyond short training sequences via an attention-mixing mechanism, but requiring the compensation network for this generalization. IFPruning (arXiv 2501.02086, Jan 2025) trains a 302M-parameter sparsity predictor that combines instruction-level (quasi-static) and input-level (contextual) sparsity selection, jointly optimizing the predictor and LLM weights via cross-entropy loss. While IFPruning also composes static and contextual sparsity components, it operates at a fundamentally different granularity and setting: instruction-level masks (same mask for all tokens in a sequence) requiring task-description input, a large predictor (302M params vs our ~75.5M total), and joint LLM fine-tuning that modifies the base model — whereas our composition applies input-independent bias correction (zero overhead, folded into bias terms) with token-level contextual prediction, both post-training on frozen models. ShadowLLM (EMNLP 2024) trains single-hidden-layer predictors using MSE loss on per-neuron importance. Sirius (NeurIPS 2024) addresses quality degradation from contextual sparsity via periodic full-model correction every 16 tokens with speculative-decoding-style rollback, achieving 20-35% latency reduction — a complementary approach to our static+contextual composition since Sirius interleaves sparse and dense inference rather than composing learned correction mechanisms. Spark Transformer (NeurIPS 2025) reparameterizes existing FFN weights and attention key embeddings during pre-training (Gemma-2 recipe) to form implicit sparsity predictors with a hardware-friendly 'statistical top-k' linear-time approximate algorithm, achieving 2.5x FLOP reduction with 1.79x CPU / 1.40x GPU decode speedup and only 8% of FFN neurons activated — complementary to our post-training approach which works on frozen models without pre-training modification. ActiveFlow (arXiv 2025) combines cross-layer preloading with sparse-aware self-distillation using KL-divergence to adapt model weights (not predictor parameters) for on-device deployment, demonstrating that KL-based training recovers 2.64-10.21% accuracy over TEAL — further evidence that output-distribution distillation is effective for sparsity recovery, though applied to weight adaptation rather than predictor training. Polar Sparsity (NeurIPS 2025) trained BCE routers and evaluated on LongBench for instruction-tuned models, concluding MLP sparsity offers limited benefits on SwiGLU in batch-scaling scenarios — however, this was with activation-pattern prediction, not output-distribution alignment in low-batch decode; their long-context evaluation was limited to 16K tokens. MaskLLM (NeurIPS 2024 Spotlight) uses Gumbel Softmax for static 2:4 masks. SDMPrune (arXiv 2025) uses self-distillation for MLP weight pruning, not activation sparsity. SVD Contextual Sparsity Predictors (arXiv 2603.14110, Mar 2026) provide training-free predictors using truncated SVD of gate projections, targeting ReGLU-based models with high inherent sparsity — critically, they found that learned predictors suffer from out-of-distribution sensitivity on unseen data (ROC-AUC degrades on held-out sequences) while SVD predictors maintain robust performance by leveraging weight structure rather than data-dependent patterns. This OOD sensitivity finding directly motivates our LongBench evaluation: if learned predictors are fragile to distribution shift, their behavior on long-context tasks (absent from short calibration data) is an important empirical question. No prior work (a) trains the sparsity predictor itself with KL-divergence distillation, (b) tests whether SPON's static correction composes additively with contextual prediction in a post-training, frozen-model setting, (c) empirically tests whether KL's advantage over BCE grows with sparsity level (with a clear hypothesis grounded in BCE's per-neuron independence assumption), or (d) evaluates whether KL-trained predictors generalize better than BCE-trained ones on long-context tasks.

## Proposed Approach

We study the composition of two learned correction mechanisms for activation sparsity — static bias correction and contextual prediction — trained via output-distribution distillation (KL divergence) on native SwiGLU LLMs at decode time, with a controlled four-condition ablation testing whether KL training subsumes error compensation. For each SwiGLU MLP block in Llama 3.1 8B (32 layers, d_model=4096, intermediate_size=14336), we attach a low-rank predictor that maps the layer input hidden state h ∈ R^d to a continuous sparsity score over the intermediate dimension D via two linear projections: h → W_down (4096 × 128) → ReLU → W_up (128 × 14336), yielding ~2.36M parameters per layer (~75.5M total, <1% of 8B model). Architecture is fixed at rank r=128 (no rank sweep, following reviewer guidance to narrow scope). During training, scores are converted to differentiable binary masks via Gumbel-sigmoid relaxation with temperature annealing (τ: 5 → 0.1). The primary training loss is: L = KL(p_dense || p_sparse) + λ_mse · MSE(h_dense_l, h_sparse_l) + λ_sparse · max(0, s − s_target), where KL divergence on output logits is the primary signal (capturing joint neuron interactions through end-to-end gradient flow via ∂L_KL/∂θ = ∂L_KL/∂p_sparse · ∂p_sparse/∂h_out · ∂h_out/∂m · ∂m/∂θ), per-layer MSE provides gradient signal for early layers, and the sparsity penalty enforces target model-level sparsity s_target = 0.5. Only predictor parameters are updated during ~1K gradient steps on SlimPajama calibration data with the base LLM frozen. MLP/attention sparsity allocation: We use TEAL's greedy per-layer allocation algorithm (also adopted by WINA) to distribute the 50% global FLOPs budget across all layers. The algorithm iteratively increases sparsity in the least-sensitive layer (measured by calibration perplexity impact) until the aggregate meets the global target. The exact per-layer and per-component (MLP, attention) sparsity ratios are determined by this calibration-based optimization — we do not impose any fixed ratio between MLP and attention sparsity. We report both the global model-level sparsity (50%) and the effective per-component sparsity ratios resulting from the allocation, ensuring transparent comparison with baselines that may use different allocation strategies. Within MLP layers, our contextual predictor replaces WINA's heuristic scoring to determine which neurons to activate; within attention layers, WINA's original weight-informed scoring is used unchanged. At inference, the predictor produces a binary mask via top-k selection, and the sparse SwiGLU forward pass skips columns of gate/up projections and rows of the down projection using Triton sparse GEMV kernels from the TEAL/WINA codebases. Predictor overhead — FLOPs and wall-clock (honest reporting): Each predictor adds ~4.72M FLOPs per layer (two GEMVs of 4096×128 and 128×14336) versus ~176M FLOPs saved at 50% MLP sparsity — a ~37:1 FLOPs ratio. However, batch=1 decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound. On H100 (3.35 TB/s peak HBM bandwidth), the small predictor GEMVs achieve only ~100 GB/s effective bandwidth due to low matrix occupancy and kernel launch overhead, yielding ~0.047 ms per layer or ~1.5 ms total predictor overhead across 32 layers. The larger FFN matrices being skipped achieve ~500 GB/s effective bandwidth, yielding ~0.35 ms savings per layer or ~11.3 ms total savings. The realistic wall-clock savings-to-overhead ratio is approximately 4-8:1 — still attractive for decode acceleration, but substantially lower than the 37:1 FLOPs ratio. We report both metrics throughout to avoid misleading claims. Four-condition ablation (the key experimental design addition): (a) BCE only — our predictor architecture trained with weighted BCE loss (FastForward-style exponential weighting), no compensation; (b) BCE + error compensation — same BCE predictor plus FastForward's 2-layer compensation MLP (r'=d_model/8=512, adding ~8.0 MB per layer: W_comp1 ∈ R^{4096×512} + W_comp2 ∈ R^{512×4096} = 4,194,304 params × 2 bytes FP16), trained with MSE loss ||Y_dense − (Y_sparse + g_φ(h))||², with compensation network active at inference time; (c) KL only — our predictor trained with KL-divergence loss, no compensation; (d) KL + error compensation — if time permits. Condition (b) is critical: it replicates FastForward's full approach (BCE + compensation) rather than testing BCE in isolation, ensuring a fair comparison. If KL-only (c) matches or exceeds BCE+compensation (b), the finding is that KL training makes error compensation unnecessary — yielding a simpler system with lower inference overhead (eliminating ~256 MB of compensation weights and associated compute across 32 layers). Evaluation benchmarks (aligned with user requirements): (1) Perplexity on WikiText-2 and C4 — standard for sparsity papers, reported by SPON and most training-free baselines, trivial to compute; (2) ARC-Challenge — science reasoning; (3) WinoGrande — coreference resolution; (4) MMLU — multitask knowledge; (5) GSM8K — mathematical reasoning; (6) HumanEval — code generation; (7) MT-Bench — multi-turn instruction-following quality under sparsity, evaluated using GPT-4 as judge (80 questions, 8 categories), testing whether sparsity disproportionately degrades open-ended generation; (8) LongBench (English subset) — long-context evaluation (4K-32K tokens) testing predictor generalization beyond training distribution, directly comparable to FastForward's LongBench results. Core evaluation: perplexity + 5 discriminative benchmarks + LongBench + MT-Bench (8 benchmarks). Conditional extension: if time permits, we add RULER (COLM 2024) — synthetic long-context evaluation with 13 tasks across 4 categories (Retrieval, Multi-hop Tracing, Aggregation, QA) at 2 context lengths (4K, 16K), providing fine-grained capability-level diagnostics that complement LongBench's aggregate scores. RULER is valuable but not essential for the paper's core claims; prioritizing the four-condition ablation + composition + LongBench ensures the key experiments are completed. This benchmark set covers all user-required evaluation axes: perplexity, knowledge/reasoning (MMLU, ARC-c, GSM8K), code (HumanEval), long-context (LongBench, optionally RULER), and instruction-following (MT-Bench).
