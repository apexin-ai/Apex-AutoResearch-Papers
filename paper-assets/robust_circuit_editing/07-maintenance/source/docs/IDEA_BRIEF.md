## Core Problem

Circuit-guided model editing—using discovered circuits as targets for activation patching to causally intervene on model behavior—requires circuits that generalize beyond the exact prompt distribution used to discover them. Existing approaches fail this robustness requirement for a specific structural reason: they rely on circuits identified by a single method or, at best, by averaging continuous attribution scores across methods (BlackboxNLP-2025 MIB Shared Task, arXiv:2510.06811). Score averaging has two compounding problems: (1) gradient attribution scores, mask optimization probabilities, and greedy causal search binary outputs are incommensurable—they have different units, scales, and semantics; even rank normalization (the most principled averaging heuristic) maps each method's scores to [0,1] via ranking but cannot disambiguate high-rank agreement from high-rank-in-one-method/zero-in-another disagreement; (2) an edge where one method assigns maximum rank and another assigns zero inclusion receives a moderate rank-normalized average score, appearing as a reliable target when it is actually a method-class artifact on which method families fundamentally disagree. Both problems mean score-averaged circuits include edges that reflect a single method's inductive bias rather than genuine model mechanisms—edges that are reliable within-distribution but fail under distributional shift. The non-identifiability result (ICLR 2025, arXiv:2502.20914) proves that multiple valid circuits exist for any behavior; the practical consequence is that some discovered edges are genuine mechanism components while others are method-class artifacts. No existing work has (a) defined a calibration-invariant cross-method consensus measure that enforces genuine cross-family agreement via a family-agreement constraint rather than relying on a numeric threshold alone, (b) characterized which edges are method-class-specific artifacts vs. genuine consensus mechanism components, or (c) demonstrated that consensus-based editing targets generalize more robustly to distributional shift than single-method, within-method-stable (CIRCUS), or score-averaged circuits.

## Opportunity / Why Now

The key methodological insight is that binary presence/absence decisions are the only quantity that all circuit discovery algorithm families produce natively and comparably: EAP-IG produces a ranked edge list that is thresholded to binary; Edge Pruning produces a binary mask; ACDC produces a binary inclusion decision. Aggregating these binary decisions into a Component Stability Score (CSS) requires no inter-method normalization, no scale alignment, and no semantic bridging—it is calibration-invariant by construction. This directly distinguishes CSS from BlackboxNLP-2025's score averaging, which achieves strong in-distribution localization performance but relies on rank normalization as a calibration heuristic and still produces moderate scores precisely where methods maximally disagree (high rank in one method family, zero inclusion in another). However, a pure numeric threshold (CSS≥0.67 with K=3) is insufficient to guarantee cross-family agreement when the K=3 composition is 2 gradient methods + 1 mask method: both gradient methods can agree while the sole mask-family method dissents, achieving CSS=2/3 and passing the threshold without cross-family consensus. The correct fix is a family-agreement constraint—requiring at least one gradient-family method AND the mask-family method (Edge Pruning) to both include an edge—which enforces genuine cross-family agreement while still tolerating at most one dissenting method. This directly addresses the threshold logic flaw and makes the 'cross-family consensus' claim accurate for large models. A complementary paper on circuit generalization (Adaptive Circuit Behavior, arXiv:2411.16105) shows the IOI circuit generalizes by reusing its stable components while adding new input edges under distributional shift—the S2 Hacking mechanism. This directly supports MNC's framing: the genuinely stable mechanism backbone (captured by MNC's consensus edges) is what gets reused across distributions, while method-specific artifact edges are not genuine mechanism components and do not participate in adaptation. The open opportunity is to demonstrate that binary consensus edges satisfying the family-agreement constraint are more robust editing targets under distributional shift than score-averaged circuits (BlackboxNLP-style), CIRCUS-core circuits (within-method-stable), or single-method circuits—and to characterize the mechanism: edges in the off-diagonal cell (CIRCUS-stable but CSS-low, i.e., within-method-stable but cross-method-divergent) are the specific artifact subset that drives generalization failure.

## Landscape (SOTA & Limitations)

The identifiability paper (ICLR 2025, arXiv:2502.20914, 'Everything, Everywhere, All at Once') proves that for any behavior, multiple circuits, interpretations, and algorithms all causally align, invalidating uniqueness assumptions and motivating consensus approaches. CIRCUS (arXiv:2603.00523, Feb 2026) is the most direct algorithmic response: it applies config-bagging over B threshold variants of a single attribution method, defining s(e)=(1/B)∑𝟙[e∈E^(b)] and extracting a strict-consensus 'core' circuit (s=1), validated on Gemma-2-2B and Llama-3.2-1B. Empirically, the CIRCUS paper finds that only ~2.5% of edges achieve s=1 core stability in their Gemma experiments (73% fall below s=0.5), confirming that within-method consensus is already highly selective—but this within-method selectivity does not guarantee cross-method agreement, which is precisely the gap the off-diagonal cell quantifies. CIRCUS establishes that within-method threshold uncertainty can be quantified and separates core from contingent edges, but it explicitly does not compare across algorithm classes and its downstream use is limited to causal validation via activation patching rather than out-of-distribution editing. IBCircuit (arXiv:2602.22581, Feb 2026) is concurrent work that applies the Information Bottleneck principle to discover circuits holistically via end-to-end optimization, avoiding task-specific corrupted activation design and reporting more faithful and minimal circuits on IOI and Greater-Than; unlike IBCircuit's single-optimization-framework approach, MNC aggregates binary consensus across independently developed algorithm families, targeting robustness to methodological inductive biases rather than completeness under a unified objective. MIB (ICML 2025, arXiv:2504.13151) provides the standardized benchmark infrastructure across four real language models (GPT-2 Small, Qwen-2.5 0.5B, Gemma-2 2B, Llama-3.1 8B) plus a synthetic model from the InterpBench suite (arXiv:2407.14494, NeurIPS 2024) for ground-truth validation, and confirms that gradient-based, mask-optimization, and DAS methods succeed on different task-model pairs. BlackboxNLP-2025 MIB Shared Task (arXiv:2510.06811, EMNLP 2025) is the most direct competitor: it combines EAP-IG-inputs, EAP-IG-activations, and EAP (three EAP variants) with Edge Pruning via continuous score combination—including rank normalization followed by unweighted averaging across methods, plus sequential warm-start ensemble. Rank normalization is a principled calibration step that maps each method's raw scores to [0,1] via ranking; the hybrid ensemble achieves the best CPR scores on GPT-2/IOI, Qwen/IOI, Qwen/MCQA. Critically, rank normalization still cannot resolve the off-diagonal failure case: an edge with maximum rank in EAP-IG (attributing full causal credit) and zero inclusion in Edge Pruning (pruned as unnecessary) receives a rank-normalized average score of 0.5—a moderate inclusion signal—despite representing maximal method-family disagreement. The ensemble covers only gradient-family and one mask-optimization method, does not include ACDC, explicitly does not evaluate on distributional shift, and acknowledges uncertainty about generalization. The companion paper Findings of BlackboxNLP 2025 (arXiv:2511.18409, EMNLP 2025) confirms no shared task submission used binary voting or consensus methods, and no clear winner emerges across metrics. A separate BlackboxNLP-2025 entry (arXiv:2510.25786) uses bootstrapping and ILP-based edge selection within a single method for faithfulness; it is within-method only and does not address distributional shift. Certified Circuits (ICLR 2026, arXiv:2602.22968) is the closest approach in spirit: it wraps any circuit discovery algorithm with randomized data subsampling to certify that edge inclusion decisions are invariant to bounded edit-distance perturbations of the concept dataset, achieving up to 91% higher accuracy on OOD ImageNet datasets while using 45% fewer neurons. The critical distinction from CSS is the axis of certification: Certified Circuits certifies against input perturbations within a single algorithm (data-perturbation stability); CSS certifies against methodological inductive biases across algorithm families (method-class stability). These axes are orthogonal—an edge can be certified against input perturbations (stable under Certified Circuits) while being excluded by a different algorithm family (CSS-low), indicating an algorithmic artifact that input sampling alone cannot detect. Certified Circuits operates in vision (not NLP), applies to neurons rather than attention/MLP edges, and does not address cross-method disagreement; CSS and Certified Circuits are complementary. MCircKE (arXiv:2604.05876, April 2026) is concurrent work in circuit-guided model editing: it maps causal circuits for multi-hop reasoning tasks and performs surgical parameter updates (weight modification) exclusively within those circuits to bridge the knowledge-editing reasoning gap, validated on MQuAKE-3K. CaKE (Yao et al., EMNLP 2025, arXiv:2503.16356) similarly addresses the knowledge-editing reasoning gap by using circuit-based analysis to identify reasoning pathways and curating training data to stimulate circuit-aware knowledge integration via fine-tuning, achieving 20% improvement on MQuAKE. Both MCircKE and CaKE target permanent weight modification for multi-hop fact recall, while MNC-guided activation patching uses temporary interventions to test OOD distributional shift robustness; none of these approaches subsumes the others. Adaptive Circuit Behavior and Generalization (arXiv:2411.16105) directly studies how the IOI circuit in GPT-2 Small generalizes to prompt variants that deviate from the training distribution. Its key finding is that the circuit generalizes by reusing all original components while adding new input edges (the S2 Hacking mechanism) rather than fundamentally restructuring—indicating circuits have a stable mechanism backbone that is genuinely reused across distributions. This contextualizes MNC: the consensus backbone (MNC edges) corresponds precisely to the components that are stably reused, while method-class artifact edges are not genuine mechanism components and do not participate in adaptive generalization. ΔF measurements across distributional variants are expected to differ by task-model pair partly due to this adaptive edge-addition phenomenon. Sparse Feature Circuits (ICLR 2025, arXiv:2403.19647) demonstrates circuit-guided model editing via the SHIFT technique at the SAE feature level with a single discovery method and human-in-the-loop relevance judgments; MNC-guided activation patching at the edge level with fully automated binary consensus is orthogonal. Formal Mechanistic Interpretability (arXiv:2602.16823, ICLR 2026) provides provable robustness guarantees for vision models without comparing method classes or targeting OOD editing. Circuit Stability Characterizes Language Model Generalization (ACL 2025, arXiv:2505.24731) studies cross-input stability of a fixed circuit—orthogonal to cross-method stability. InterpBench (arXiv:2407.14494, NeurIPS 2024) is a collection of 8,686 semi-synthetic transformers with known circuits; the IOI model specifically uses Wang et al.'s manually-hypothesized circuit (rather than a Tracr-generated circuit) as the high-level model for Strict IIT (SIIT) training. MIB's circuit localization track uses this IOI model for ground-truth edge-level validation. No existing work provides a calibration-invariant binary cross-method consensus measure with a family-agreement constraint for NLP, computes CSS on the full MIB suite, directly compares binary consensus vs. rank-normalized score averaging for OOD editing robustness, or identifies CIRCUS-stable/CSS-low edges as the specific source of generalization failure.

## Proposed Approach

The paper is structured with MNC-guided activation patching as the primary contribution and the inter-algorithm disaggregation analysis as the motivating Section 3. **Method (Section 2):** Define CSS(e) = (1/K)∑_{k=1}^{K} 𝟙[e∈E^(k)] where K, the threshold, and the family-agreement constraint vary by model in a pre-registered tractability-stratified protocol.

**GPT-2 Small only (~117M parameters):** K=4 methods—EAP-IG (gradient attribution), AtP* (gradient attribution), Edge Pruning (mask optimization), ACDC (greedy causal search)—spanning three genuinely distinct algorithm families. CSS ≥ 0.75 (≥3/4 methods agree), tolerating exactly one algorithmically idiosyncratic method, by analogy to Meinshausen & Bühlmann (2010). ACDC tractability: GPT-2 Small has approximately 32,000 circuit edges (12 layers × 12 attention heads computational graph), and multi-hour runtimes per ACDC run are within the compute budget across 4 tasks.

**All other models (Qwen-2.5 0.5B, Gemma-2 2B, Llama-3.1 8B):** K=3 methods—EAP-IG, AtP* (gradient attribution), Edge Pruning (mask optimization)—spanning two algorithm families. ACDC is excluded from all three models based on tractability. For Qwen-2.5 0.5B specifically: the model has 24 transformer layers and 14 attention heads¹, yielding an estimated ~170,000 circuit edges—approximately 5.3× GPT-2 Small's ~32,000 edges. [¹Qwen-2.5 0.5B uses Grouped Query Attention (GQA) with 14 query heads and 2 KV heads (num_attention_heads=14, num_key_value_heads=2 per config); circuit edges are defined at the query-head level, consistent with TransformerLens's treatment of GQA, yielding 14 effective attention nodes per layer. The GQA structure means KV computations are shared across query-head groups, but the query-head-level edge count (~170,000) is the operationally relevant quantity for ACDC's sequential search and for the CSS computation.] ACDC's sequential greedy search requires one forward pass per edge evaluation; scaling linearly from GPT-2 Small's multi-hour runtimes, ACDC on Qwen-2.5 0.5B would require approximately 42–127 hours per run. Across 4 tasks with a 16-day compute window, ACDC on Qwen-2.5 0.5B would consume approximately 168–508 A100-hours—infeasible regardless of parallelization, since ACDC is sequential by design. The tractability boundary is therefore ~32,000 edges (GPT-2 Small, feasible) vs. ~170,000+ edges (Qwen-2.5 0.5B and larger, infeasible). Gemma-2 2B and Llama-3.1 8B are further beyond this boundary.

**Family-Agreement Constraint for K=3 models:** A pure CSS≥0.67 threshold with K=3 (2 gradient methods + 1 mask method) is insufficient to enforce cross-family consensus. Specifically, if EAP-IG and AtP* both include an edge but Edge Pruning excludes it, CSS=2/3≈0.67 and the edge passes the numeric threshold—but the sole non-gradient family (mask optimization) is the single dissenter, meaning no genuine cross-family agreement was reached. To enforce cross-family consensus, an edge enters MNC for K=3 models if and only if: (a) CSS≥0.67 AND (b) Edge Pruning includes it (ensuring the mask-family method contributes to the consensus). This composite criterion—CSS≥0.67 with family-agreement constraint—tolerates at most one dissenting method while requiring that the sole dissenter is a gradient-family method, not the only mask-family representative. Concretely, MNC for K=3 models includes: all three-way consensus (CSS=1.0); and cases where exactly one gradient-family method dissents but Edge Pruning and the other gradient method agree (CSS=2/3 with cross-family agreement). The only CSS=2/3 case excluded by criterion (b)—both gradient methods agreeing while Edge Pruning dissents—is precisely the gradient-dominant consensus that would undermine the cross-family claim. An edge where EAP-IG assigns maximum rank and AtP* concurs but Edge Pruning excludes gets CSS=2/3 but fails the family constraint, correctly flagging it as a gradient-family artifact.

Requiring unanimity (CSS=1.0) instead is too conservative: it would exclude edges where one gradient method's top-K threshold clips a genuine consensus edge due to attribution noise even when the other gradient method and Edge Pruning both include it. Attribution noise in top-K thresholding can cause a borderline-high-importance edge to be ranked just outside the cutoff in one run while the other gradient method and the mask-family method both identify it as a circuit component; CSS=1.0 would exclude such an edge entirely, discarding genuine mechanism consensus. The family-agreement constraint at CSS≥0.67 retains these genuine consensus edges while still guaranteeing the sole non-gradient family (Edge Pruning) contributes—providing a principled middle ground between gradient-dominant consensus (too loose) and full unanimity (too strict).

**Coverage Asymmetry Disclosure:** The family-agreement constraint for K=3 models covers two algorithm families (gradient attribution and mask optimization), while K=4 on GPT-2 Small covers three. The greedy causal search family (ACDC) is entirely absent from Qwen-2.5 0.5B, Gemma-2 2B, and Llama-3.1 8B. This asymmetry has a practical implication: CSS for non-GPT-2-Small models cannot detect edges that are artifacts of both gradient and mask families simultaneously but would be excluded by ACDC. The discriminative power of MNC for large models is therefore narrower than for GPT-2 Small—it identifies gradient-vs-mask disagreements but not broader three-family cross-algorithm disagreements. Results on large models should be interpreted as 'two-family consensus circuits' (gradient + mask), while GPT-2 Small results reflect 'three-family consensus circuits' (gradient + mask + greedy causal search). This limitation does not undermine the core claim: the CIRCUS-stable/CSS-low off-diagonal characterization requires only that CIRCUS operates within a single method while CSS aggregates across methods—a distinction preserved for both K=3 and K=4—and the rank-normalized score averaging comparison is equally valid for both model groups.

**Ground-Truth Validation:** A dedicated validation experiment uses the IOI model from the InterpBench benchmark suite (NeurIPS 2024, arXiv:2407.14494)—a collection of 8,686 semi-synthetic transformers with known circuits; the IOI model specifically uses Wang et al.'s manually-hypothesized circuit (rather than a Tracr-generated circuit) as the high-level model for Strict IIT (SIIT) training—measuring CSS recall as AUROC over ground-truth edges. This quantifies how well binary consensus recovers genuine mechanism components vs. artifacts, a validation that faithfulness metrics alone cannot provide.

**Differentiation from IBCircuit (Section 2, Related Work):** IBCircuit (arXiv:2602.22581, Feb 2026) applies the Information Bottleneck principle to discover circuits holistically via end-to-end optimization, avoiding task-specific corrupted activation design. Unlike IBCircuit's single-optimization-framework approach, MNC aggregates binary consensus across independently developed algorithm families, targeting robustness to methodological inductive biases rather than completeness under a unified objective. IBCircuit and MNC address complementary failure modes: IBCircuit's IB objective may discover edges missed by corrupted-activation methods; MNC's family-agreement constraint filters edges that pass any single method or objective but reflect that method's inductive bias rather than genuine model mechanisms.

**Differentiation from Certified Circuits (Section 2, Related Work):** Certified Circuits (ICLR 2026, arXiv:2602.22968) certifies edge stability against input perturbations within a single algorithm. CSS targets the complementary axis: methodological inductive biases across algorithm families. An edge can be Certified Circuits-stable (input-perturbation-invariant within one algorithm) while CSS-low (excluded by a different algorithm family), identifying algorithmic artifacts that input sampling within a single method cannot detect. These axes are orthogonal and can be combined; CSS additionally operates on NLP attention edges rather than vision neurons.

**Differentiation from CaKE (Section 2, Related Work):** CaKE (Yao et al., EMNLP 2025, arXiv:2503.16356) identifies reasoning circuits and curates training data to stimulate circuit-aware knowledge integration via fine-tuning, achieving 20% improvement on MQuAKE for multi-hop reasoning. CaKE's permanent weight modification—targeting the failure of layer-localized editing to propagate updated facts through reasoning circuits—is distinct from MNC-guided activation patching, which applies temporary activation interventions to test OOD generalization of circuit-based editing targets across distributional shift variants of non-knowledge-editing tasks. The two approaches address complementary failure modes: CaKE targets knowledge-editing propagation failures; MNC targets method-class artifact edges that degrade circuit-guided editing robustness under distributional shift.

**Differentiation from MCircKE (Section 2, Related Work):** MCircKE (arXiv:2604.05876, April 2026) maps causal circuits for multi-hop reasoning tasks and performs surgical parameter updates (weight modification) exclusively within those circuits to bridge the knowledge-editing reasoning gap, validated on MQuAKE-3K. MCircKE targets a complementary use case—permanent weight modification for knowledge editing—while MNC-guided activation patching uses temporary interventions for OOD distributional shift robustness; neither approach subsumes the other.
