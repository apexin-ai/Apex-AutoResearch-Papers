\section{Proof of Weakest-Link Monotonicity (\S\ref{thm:monotonicity})}
\label{app:proof}

The replaced judge $J_K'$ is \emph{pivotal} exactly when $t{-}1$ of the remaining $K{-}1$ judges have already failed. Formally, let $S = \sum_{j=1}^{K-1} X_j^a$ denote the number of failures among the shared judges. The panel fails if and only if the $K$-th judge also fails when $S = t{-}1$, or when $S \geq t$ regardless of the $K$-th judge:
\begin{multline}
\mathrm{ASR}(\mathcal{P}, a) = \Pr(S \geq t) \\
 + \Pr(S = t{-}1) \cdot \Pr(X_K^a {=} 1 \mid S = t{-}1).
\end{multline}
Since $\Pr(X_{K'}^a {=} 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_{-K}^a {=} \mathbf{x}_{-K}) \geq \Pr(X_K^a {=} 1 \mid \mathbf{X}_{-K}^a {=} \mathbf{x}_{-K})$ for all attacks $a$ and realizations $\mathbf{x}_{-K}$ (by hypothesis), the second term is weakly larger for $\mathcal{P}'$ than for $\mathcal{P}$ under every attack. Taking the supremum over attacks preserves the inequality: $\mathrm{ASR}^*(\mathcal{P}') \geq \mathrm{ASR}^*(\mathcal{P})$. The result holds under arbitrary inter-judge correlations and any attack space $\mathcal{A}$.

\section{Robustness Correction Methods}
\label{app:robustness_methods}

The 455 panels are constructed combinatorially from 15 shared judges: each judge appears in $\binom{14}{2} = 91$ panels, inducing correlated residuals across panel-level observations. Standard OLS assumes independent errors, potentially inflating significance. We apply three corrections.

\paragraph{Clustered Standard Errors.} Because each panel of $K{=}3$ judges does not map to a single cluster, we perform three separate one-way clusterings (one for each judge position in the ordered panel tuple) using CR1 bias-corrected sandwich estimators (13 clusters per position). The reported standard error for each coefficient is the maximum across the three clusterings, yielding the most conservative inference.

\paragraph{Mixed Effects.} For each of the three judge positions, we fit a linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept for the judge occupying that position, estimating $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and $\eta_{\max}$ as fixed effects. The reported $p$-value for each coefficient is the maximum across the three models.

\paragraph{Wild Cluster Bootstrap.} We perform $B{=}9{,}999$ bootstrap resamples using Rademacher weights, clustering by each of the three panel positions in turn and taking the maximum $p$-value across positions (the most conservative aggregation). This approach avoids distributional assumptions on the cluster structure.

\subsection{Bootstrap Convergence and Weight Diagnostics}
\label{app:bootstrap_convergence}

Our primary wild cluster bootstrap uses $B{=}9{,}999$ Rademacher resamples. We assess convergence by comparing significance counts at $B \in \{999, 4{,}999, 9{,}999\}$ and contrasting Rademacher with Mammen two-point weights.

\paragraph{$B$-Value Stability.} Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap_B} reports FDR-significant $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ counts across bootstrap replications. Significance counts stabilize by $B{=}4{,}999$: the maximum discrepancy between $B{=}4{,}999$ and $B{=}9{,}999$ is $\pm 1$ condition across all three $K$ values, and the mean absolute $p$-value change is ${<}0.003$. The $B{=}999$ configuration shows occasional instability (up to $\pm 2$ discrepancy at $K{=}5$), justifying our choice of $B{=}9{,}999$.

\paragraph{Rademacher vs.\ Mammen Weights.} Rademacher weights assign $\pm 1$ with equal probability; Mammen two-point weights use $\{-(\sqrt{5}-1)/2, (\sqrt{5}+1)/2\}$ with asymmetric probabilities to preserve the third moment \citep{mackinnon2018wild}. In our setting, both weight distributions yield identical significance counts at all three $K$ values (8/12, 8/12, 7/12), with maximum $p$-value divergence of 0.008 across all 36 conditions. The Webb six-point distribution (used for the Shannon MI comparison in Appendix~\ref{app:shannon_entropy}) produces marginally different results at $K{=}3$ due to the low cluster count ($G{=}13$), where the discrete support of six-point weights interacts with the small number of sign permutations. All primary analyses use Rademacher weights for simplicity and comparability with the Monte Carlo calibration (Appendix~\ref{app:monte_carlo}).

\paragraph{Max-$p$ vs.\ Alternative Aggregation.} We aggregate $p$-values across $K$ position-clusterings by taking the maximum (most conservative). Alternative aggregations (median $p$ and Bonferroni-corrected minimum $p$) produce slightly different significance counts. At $K{=}5$: max-$p$ yields 8/12, median yields 9/12, and Bonferroni-min yields 7/12. The max-$p$ approach is recommended for combinatorial designs where cluster membership is non-nested \citep{mackinnon2018wild}, since it guards against inflated significance from any single clustering being anti-conservative. All reported results use max-$p$.

\section{Crossed Random Effects Analysis}
\label{app:crossed_re}

The one-way clustering in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness_methods} treats each judge position independently, but panels exhibit \emph{crossed} random effects: judge $j$ may occupy position~1 in one panel and position~2 in another, inducing a multi-membership structure. At $K{=}7$, each judge appears in $\binom{14}{6} = 3{,}003$ of $6{,}435$ panels (46.7\%), making crossed membership the dominant source of residual correlation.

We fit multi-membership cluster-robust variance estimators (CRVE) using a sandwich estimator with $t(G{-}1) = t(14)$ reference distribution, where $G{=}15$ is the number of judges. Table~\ref{tab:crossed_re} compares bootstrap and crossed RE significance.

SE inflation increases with $K$ as judge overlap intensifies: $1.75$--$4.96{\times}$ at $K{=}3$, $6$--$12{\times}$ at $K{=}5$, $10$--$19{\times}$ at $K{=}7$. The $K{=}3$ attenuation (0/12) reflects two compounding factors: the conservative $t(14)$ reference distribution penalizes small-sample inference, and the effect sizes at $K{=}3$ are insufficient to overcome the inflated SEs. Raw (uncorrected) $p$-values show 5/12 conditions significant at $K{=}3$, confirming that the attenuation reflects degrees-of-freedom conservatism rather than absent effects.

\subsection{Diversity--Capability Tradeoff}
\label{app:diversity_capability}

The $r = 0.70$--$0.81$ correlation between $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and $\eta_{\max}$ ($K{=}3$ to $K{=}5$) reflects a structural feature of the current LLM ecosystem: maximizing panel diversity requires sampling across capability tiers, which systematically introduces less capable and therefore more vulnerable judges. The structural correlation is unaffected by SE correction; it is a property of the model ecosystem, not an artifact of panel overlap. However, the high confound combined with fragile independent significance means that observational regression cannot reliably separate diversity's contribution from the capability deficit that co-occurs with it.


Family-level labels provide indirect evidence that capability proximity matters more than taxonomic grouping: family membership is not a significant predictor of panel vulnerability ($p = 0.179$ on MMLU, $p = 0.385$ on ARC), consistent with models from the same provider exhibiting heterogeneous vulnerability profiles that vary by capability tier rather than by shared architecture \citep{kuncheva2003measures}.

\subsection{Aggregation Rule Formal Definitions}
\label{app:aggregation_formal}

We evaluate four aggregation rules for combining $K$ judge decisions. Let $d_j \in \{\text{A}, \text{B}, \text{tie}\}$ denote judge $j$'s winner label and $(s_j^A, s_j^B)$ its assigned scores.

\paragraph{Majority Voting.} The panel decision is the mode of $\{d_1, \ldots, d_K\}$:
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{maj}} = \arg\max_{c \in \{\text{A},\text{B},\text{tie}\}} \sum_{j=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}[d_j = c].
\end{equation}
Ties in the mode are broken uniformly at random. This is the simplest rule and makes no use of score magnitudes.

\paragraph{Weighted Voting.} Each judge $j$ receives a weight $w_j = \text{acc}_j^{\text{clean}}$ proportional to its clean accuracy on the relevant benchmark. The panel decision is:
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{wt}} = \arg\max_{c \in \{\text{A},\text{B},\text{tie}\}} \sum_{j=1}^{K} w_j \cdot \mathbf{1}[d_j = c].
\end{equation}
Weights are normalized so $\sum_j w_j = 1$. This rule upweights more accurate judges but does not account for judge-specific confusion patterns.

\paragraph{Threshold Voting.} A \emph{unanimous} rule requiring all $K$ judges to agree:
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{thr}} = \begin{cases} c & \text{if } d_j = c \;\forall\, j \in \{1,\ldots,K\}, \\ \text{tie} & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Under attack, an adversary succeeds if \emph{every} judge is manipulated, making threshold voting the most composition-sensitive rule ($R^2{=}0.745$ at $K{=}3$). However, threshold voting produces high tie rates on clean data, limiting its practical utility.

\paragraph{Bayesian Calibration.} Each judge $j$ has a $3 \times 3$ confusion matrix $C_j$ estimated from clean evaluation data, where $C_j[c, c'] = \Pr(d_j {=} c' \mid \text{true} {=} c)$. Given observed decisions $\mathbf{d} = (d_1, \ldots, d_K)$ and a uniform prior over true labels:
\begin{equation}
\Pr(\text{true}{=}c \mid \mathbf{d}) \propto \prod_{j=1}^{K} C_j[c, d_j].
\end{equation}
The panel decision is $D_{\mathrm{Bayes}} = \arg\max_c \Pr(\text{true}{=}c \mid \mathbf{d})$. This is a simplified Dawid--Skene model \citep{dawid1979maximum} with fixed (non-EM) confusion matrices estimated from 300 clean samples per benchmark. Bayesian calibration downweights unreliable judges implicitly through their diffuse confusion matrices, reducing $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$'s predictive power ($R^2{=}0.136$ at $K{=}3$) by partially neutralizing the diversity channel.

\subsection{Metric Robustness Under Partial Panel Information}
\label{app:oracle}

The main analysis computes $\eta_{\max}$ with full knowledge of panel composition (all three judges known). In deployment, a practitioner selecting or auditing a panel may know only a subset of the constituent judges. We test whether $\eta_{\max}$ retains predictive power under such partial information by recomputing it from two of the three panel members: for each panel, we cycle through all $\binom{3}{1} = 3$ leave-one-out masks, compute $\eta_{\max}$ from the two known judges, and average the regression estimates across masking scenarios.

Under the most conservative inference (partial information + clustered SE), $\eta_{\max}$ remains the sole significant predictor with $\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{std}} = 0.589$, achieving significance in 12/12 conditions, stronger than the oracle baseline (4/12 under clustered SE). $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ loses all predictive power (0/12). The averaging over masking scenarios smooths estimation noise inherent in the three-judge oracle computation, strengthening the $\eta_{\max}$ signal. This demonstrates that $\eta_{\max}$ computed from partial panel information is robust to incomplete composition knowledge and retains (indeed improves) its predictive power for panel ASR.

\subsection{Meta-Analytic Pooling Procedure}
\label{app:meta_analytic_details}

Section~\ref{sec:cross_k} reports pooled effect sizes using DerSimonian--Laird (DL) random effects \citep{dersimonian1986meta}. Here we detail the pooling procedure and assess sensitivity to alternative meta-analytic methods.

\paragraph{Procedure.} For each predictor $X \in \{K_{\mathrm{eff}}, \eta_{\max}\}$ at each $K$, we compute 12 condition-specific standardized OLS coefficients $\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{std}}^{(c)}$ with standard errors $\mathrm{SE}^{(c)}$ from the wild cluster bootstrap. DL random effects models the 12 condition estimates as:
\begin{multline}
\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{std}}^{(c)} = \mu + u_c + \epsilon_c, \quad u_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2), \\
\epsilon_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_c^2),
\end{multline}
where $\mu$ is the pooled effect, $\tau^2$ captures between-condition heterogeneity, and $\sigma_c^2$ is the within-condition sampling variance. The DL estimator for $\tau^2$ uses the $Q$-statistic:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\tau}^2_{\mathrm{DL}} = \max\!\left(0, \frac{Q - (C-1)}{\sum w_c - \sum w_c^2 / \sum w_c}\right),
\end{equation}
with $w_c = 1/\sigma_c^2$ the inverse-variance weights and $C{=}12$ conditions.

\paragraph{$I^2$ Heterogeneity.} The high $I^2$ values (87.7\%--99.8\%) indicate that most variation across conditions reflects genuine heterogeneity rather than sampling noise. This is expected: different attack types exploit different vulnerability mechanisms, and the two benchmarks differ in format and difficulty. The pooled estimate $\mu$ represents the central tendency across this heterogeneous landscape, not a single shared effect size.

The log-log specification (Equation~\ref{eq:regression}) uses $\epsilon{=}10^{-6}$ to handle zero ASR values. \FloatBarrier
\section{Gaussian Copula Assumption and $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ Robustness}
\label{app:copula_gof}

$K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ admits a closed-form expression $K - \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i}\sum_{j \neq i} \rho_{ij}^2$ under the Gaussian copula, where $\exp(-2\,\mathrm{MI}(i,j)) = 1 - \rho_{ij}^2$ \citep{nelsen2006copulas}. We test whether this parametric assumption holds for judge pairs using the \citet{genest2008validity} Cram\'{e}r--von Mises goodness-of-fit test for Gaussian copulas.

\paragraph{GoF Results.} Across all $\binom{15}{2} = 105$ judge pairs, the Gaussian copula is rejected at $\alpha{=}0.05$ for 97--100\% of pairs (MMLU: 102/105, 97.1\%; ARC: 104/105, 99.0\%). The rejections are not marginal: median $p$-values are ${<}0.001$ on both benchmarks. This indicates that the pairwise dependence structure among LLM judges is substantially non-Gaussian, likely reflecting the discrete three-category nature of winner labels and the heavy tail structure of judge agreement patterns.

\paragraph{Ranking Robustness Despite Copula Rejection.} Despite the Gaussian copula being formally rejected, the closed-form $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ (using $1 - \rho_{ij}^2$) and the plug-in MI $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ (using $\exp(-2\,\widehat{\mathrm{MI}}(i,j))$ with Miller--Madow correction) produce highly concordant panel rankings within each $K$. Within-$K$ Spearman rank correlations are $\rho \approx 0.86$ ($K{=}3$: 0.862, $K{=}5$: 0.858, $K{=}7$: 0.867). The systematic level difference (plug-in $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ mean 3.38 vs.\ closed-form 4.29, averaged across $K$) reflects the Miller--Madow bias correction increasing MI estimates, which compresses $\exp(-2\,\mathrm{MI})$ toward zero. This level difference does not affect regression results, which depend on ranking.

\paragraph{Implications.} $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$'s effectiveness as a predictor of panel vulnerability does not depend on the Gaussian copula being correct. The $\exp(-2\,\mathrm{MI})$ kernel provides a nonparametric, information-theoretic measure of pairwise alienation; the Gaussian copula merely offers a convenient closed-form approximation that preserves ranking fidelity. All regression analyses in the main text use the closed-form $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$; the plug-in MI version would yield equivalent conclusions for any analysis based on panel ordering.

\subsection{Full $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ Derivation}
\label{app:keff_derivation}

We derive $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ from first principles via information-theoretic pairwise dependence.

\paragraph{Step 1: Pairwise Mutual Information.} For judge pair $(i,j)$, let $W_i, W_j \in \{\text{A}, \text{B}, \text{tie}\}$ denote their clean winner labels on a shared evaluation set. The empirical mutual information is:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{MI}}(i,j) = \sum_{w_i} \sum_{w_j} \hat{p}(w_i, w_j) \log \frac{\hat{p}(w_i, w_j)}{\hat{p}(w_i)\hat{p}(w_j)},
\end{equation}
where $\hat{p}$ denotes empirical frequencies over the $n{=}300$ evaluation samples. We apply the Miller--Madow bias correction: $\widehat{\mathrm{MI}}_{\mathrm{MM}} = \widehat{\mathrm{MI}} - (|W|^2 - 1)/(2n\ln 2)$, where $|W|{=}3$ is the label alphabet size.

\paragraph{Step 2: Gaussian Copula Transform.} Under a Gaussian copula, the relationship $\mathrm{MI}(i,j) = -\frac{1}{2}\log(1 - \rho_{ij}^2)$ links MI to the copula correlation $\rho_{ij}$. Inverting: $\rho_{ij}^2 = 1 - \exp(-2\,\mathrm{MI}(i,j))$. We define the pairwise \emph{alienation coefficient} $a_{ij} = \exp(-2\,\mathrm{MI}(i,j)) = 1 - \rho_{ij}^2 \in [0, 1]$, where $a_{ij}{=}1$ indicates independence and $a_{ij}{=}0$ indicates perfect dependence.

\paragraph{Step 3: Effective Independence.} For a $K$-member panel $\mathcal{P} = \{j_1, \ldots, j_K\}$:
\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathcal{P}) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i=1}^{K} \sum_{\substack{j=1}}^{K} a_{j_i, j_j} = K - \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i=1}^{K} \sum_{\substack{j=1 \\ j \neq i}}^{K} \rho_{j_i, j_j}^2,
\label{eq:keff_full}
\end{equation}
since diagonal terms $a_{ii}{=}\exp(-2 \cdot \infty) \equiv 0$ or equivalently $\rho_{ii}^2{=}1$. The second form shows $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ as the nominal panel size minus a penalty for pairwise correlation. When all judges are independent ($\rho_{ij}{=}0$ for $i{\neq}j$), $K_{\mathrm{eff}}{=}K$. When all judges are perfectly correlated ($\rho_{ij}{=}1$), $K_{\mathrm{eff}}{=}1$.

\paragraph{Properties.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Range:} $1 \leq K_{\mathrm{eff}} \leq K$ (tight bounds achieved by perfect correlation and independence, respectively).
\item \textbf{Monotonicity:} Adding an independent judge increases $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ by exactly 1; adding a perfectly correlated clone increases it by $2/K' - 1/(K')^2$ (where $K'$ is the new panel size), which is sublinear.
\item \textbf{Scale invariance:} $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ depends on $\rho_{ij}^2$ (squared correlations), making it invariant to the sign of pairwise association.
\item \textbf{Computational cost:} $O(K^2)$ per panel given precomputed pairwise MI; the $\binom{15}{2}{=}105$ pairwise MI values are computed once and reused across all panels.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{$K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ Stability Under Attack}
\label{app:keff_stability}

We assess whether clean-computed $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ remains a valid predictor under attack by examining the rank-order stability of pairwise MI across attack conditions (complementing the Spearman correlations in Table~\ref{tab:attacked_keff}).

The 6 attack types induce three distinct perturbation regimes on MI structure:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{MI-preserving} ($\rho{\geq}0.91$): Score manipulation, authority bias, sycophancy, and verbosity bias preserve the relative ordering of pairwise MI values. These attacks perturb judge \emph{outputs} without fundamentally altering which judge pairs agree or disagree, consistent with their mechanism targeting evaluation criteria rather than inter-judge agreement structure.

\item \textbf{MI-perturbing} ($\rho{\approx}0.60$): Prompt injection moderately disrupts MI structure by overriding evaluation instructions for susceptible judges. This creates a bimodal effect: resistant judges maintain their original MI structure, while susceptible judges' pairwise MI increases toward higher values (more agreement on the injected response), partially reshuffling panel $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ rankings.

\item \textbf{MI-disrupting} ($\rho{<}0.10$): Position bias on ARC fundamentally alters inter-judge dependence. Because position bias operates on presentation order rather than response content, it creates agreement patterns orthogonal to content-based evaluation, producing near-zero correlation between clean and attacked MI structures. This explains position bias's anomalous regression behavior throughout the main text.
\end{enumerate}

The clean $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is thus a valid proxy for attacked $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ in 4/6 attack types, partially valid for prompt injection, and invalid for ARC position bias. The regression analyses that include position bias conditions may therefore underestimate $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$'s predictive power for the four MI-preserving attacks, where the clean predictor is most faithful.

\section{Quantization Dummy Regression}
\label{app:quant_dummy}

To assess whether the AWQ quantization confound accounts for the diversity signal, we augment Equation~\ref{eq:regression} with a binary dummy $D_{\mathrm{quant}}$ indicating whether the panel's weakest judge is a quantized local model. The augmented regression is:
\begin{multline}
\log(\mathrm{ASR} + \epsilon) = \alpha + \beta_1 \log(K_{\mathrm{eff}}) \\
 + \beta_2 \log(\eta_{\max} + \epsilon) + \beta_3 D_{\mathrm{quant}}.
\end{multline}
Adding the dummy attenuates $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$'s standardized coefficient from $+0.39$ ($K{=}3$) to $+0.34$ ($K{=}5$) to $+0.27$ ($K{=}7$), with FDR-significant counts of 6/12, 6/12, and 4/12 respectively. The attenuation confirms partial confounding: some of $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$'s explanatory power reflects the capability gap between quantized and API models rather than independence per se. However, collinearity between $D_{\mathrm{quant}}$ and both predictors escalates sharply with panel size (VIF${=}162$ at $K{=}7$), rendering the dummy unreliable for large panels. The quantization dummy analysis is therefore informative at $K{=}3$ (moderate VIF) but should be interpreted cautiously at $K{\geq}5$.

\section{Bayesian Posterior Concentration Analysis}
\label{app:bayesian_entropy}

Bayesian calibration neutralizes $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ at $K{=}7$ (0/12 significant; \S\ref{sec:analysis}). One hypothesized explanation is that the posterior concentrates on 2--3 accurate judges, compressing effective panel diversity. We test this by computing the posterior concentration ratio $C = \max_j w_j^{\mathrm{post}} / \min_j w_j^{\mathrm{post}}$ across panels.

The concentration ratio declines modestly across panel sizes: $C = 0.890$ ($K{=}3$), $0.874$ ($K{=}5$), $0.867$ ($K{=}7$), a total change of only 2.3 percentage points. This gradual decline is insufficient to explain the abrupt neutralization at $K{=}7$ (from 6/12 at $K{=}3$ to 0/12 at $K{=}7$). The pattern is therefore not explained by simple posterior concentration; rather, the interaction between Bayesian weighting and the combinatorial structure of larger panels likely produces the neutralization. Full characterization of this interaction remains an open question.

\section{Monte Carlo Calibration of Wild Cluster Bootstrap}
\label{app:monte_carlo}

To verify that the wild cluster bootstrap does not over-reject under the null hypothesis, we conduct a Monte Carlo calibration study. For each of the 36 conditions (12 attack$\times$benchmark conditions $\times$ 3 panel sizes), we generate 1{,}000 permutation datasets by randomly shuffling the ASR outcome within each cluster, destroying any true association between predictors and ASR. For each permuted dataset, we run the wild cluster bootstrap ($B{=}999$, Rademacher weights) and record whether the null is rejected at $\alpha{=}0.05$.

Across all 36 conditions, the maximum empirical null rejection rate is 1.6\%, well below the nominal $\alpha{=}5\%$. The median rejection rate is 0.4\%. This confirms that the wild cluster bootstrap is conservative in our combinatorial design: the reported significance counts are lower bounds on what a correctly calibrated test would yield. The conservatism is expected given the low cluster counts (13, ${\leq}11$, ${\leq}9$ position clusters at $K{=}3,5,7$) and the use of Rademacher weights \citep{mackinnon2018wild}.

\subsection{Fractional Logit Specification Details}
\label{app:fractional_logit_detail}

The log-log OLS specification (Equation~\ref{eq:regression}) faces two methodological concerns: (1) the $\log(\mathrm{ASR} + \epsilon)$ transformation introduces sensitivity to the $\epsilon$ floor (Appendix~\ref{app:epsilon}), and (2) predicted values are unbounded, potentially falling outside $[0, 1]$. The fractional logit model \citep{papke1996econometric} addresses both by modeling ASR directly as a quasi-likelihood with logit link:
\begin{equation}
g(\mu_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log K_{\mathrm{eff},i} + \beta_2 \log(\eta_{\max,i} + \epsilon),
\end{equation}
where $g(\cdot) = \log(\cdot/(1-\cdot))$ is the logit link, $\mu_i = E[\mathrm{ASR}_i]$, and the quasi-likelihood variance is $V(\mu_i) = \mu_i(1-\mu_i)$. Inference uses the Bernoulli quasi-likelihood with clustered standard errors (same max-$p$ aggregation as the bootstrap).

\subsection{Condorcet Jury Theorem: Adversarial Failure Analysis}
\label{app:cjt_failure}

The Condorcet Jury Theorem (CJT) \citep{condorcet1785essai} guarantees that majority voting accuracy converges to 1 as $K \to \infty$ when individual voter accuracy exceeds 0.5 and votes are independent. Our experimental setting violates both assumptions under adversarial manipulation, and the nature of the violation is informative.

\paragraph{Independence Violation.} CJT assumes conditionally independent errors. Under adaptive attack, the adversary exploits shared vulnerabilities, inducing positive error correlations. The MI heatmap (Figure~\ref{fig:mi_heatmap}) shows that within-family judge pairs exhibit 2--3$\times$ higher MI than cross-family pairs even under clean conditions; under attack, these correlations intensify for susceptible judge pairs. The effective number of independent votes is $K_{\mathrm{eff}} < K$, meaning the CJT convergence rate is governed by $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ rather than $K$.

\paragraph{Accuracy Violation.} CJT requires individual accuracy ${>}0.5$. Under adversarial manipulation, susceptible judges can have accuracy below this threshold. Claude 3 Haiku under prompt injection shows ASR${=}0.817$ on MMLU, equivalently, accuracy of 0.183 on attacked pairs, well below 0.5. When panels contain such judges, the CJT prediction inverts: majority voting can \emph{amplify} rather than correct errors, because multiple susceptible judges can form a manipulated majority.

\paragraph{The BFT Reversal.} Classical Byzantine Fault Tolerance \citep{lamport1982byzantine} tolerates up to $\lfloor(K-1)/3\rfloor$ faulty nodes under non-strategic faults. Under adaptive adversarial manipulation, the ``faulty'' judges are not random but strategically targeted. The positive $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$--ASR association reverses the BFT intuition: more independent channels create more distinct attack surfaces rather than redundant defenses. Table~\ref{tab:bft_comparison} formalizes this contrast.

\paragraph{Empirical Quantification.} The random fault contrast (\S\ref{sec:cross_k}) directly quantifies the CJT--adversarial gap. Under random faults (CJT-like conditions), $R^2{=}0.730$ and $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is significant in 2/2 benchmark conditions, confirming that panel composition strongly predicts error rates when faults are independent. Under adaptive attack, $R^2$ drops to 0.285 ($\Delta{=}0.446$), but $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ retains positive association (8/12 conditions), indicating that the CJT mechanism is weakened but not eliminated. The residual CJT-like protection arises because not all judges are susceptible to all attacks: heterogeneous vulnerability profiles create a form of ``accidental independence'' even under strategic manipulation. This partial survival of the CJT mechanism explains why larger panels still provide some protection ($K_{\mathrm{eff}}$'s positive direction is 10/12 across methods and panel sizes), but the benefit is substantially diminished relative to the non-adversarial prediction.

\paragraph{Correlated Errors.} \citet{ladha1992condorcet} extended the CJT to correlated voters, showing that positive correlation weakens convergence. Our $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ metric can be interpreted as the CJT-effective panel size: a panel with $K{=}7$ judges but $K_{\mathrm{eff}}{=}4.5$ behaves, for convergence purposes, like 4.5 independent voters. The sublinear $K_{\mathrm{eff}}/K$ ratio (0.73, 0.67, 0.65 at $K{=}3,5,7$; Table~\ref{tab:keff_dist}) quantifies the cost of inter-judge correlation in the LLM ecosystem. Each additional judge provides diminishing independence returns, consistent with the family-clustered MI structure (Appendix~\ref{app:mi_structure}).

\paragraph{The Capability--Diversity Tradeoff.} Practitioners face a fundamental tension: maximizing $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ (sampling diverse model families) mechanically introduces less capable models that inflate $\eta_{\max}$, while selecting only capable models compresses $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$. The empirical evidence suggests resolving this tradeoff in favor of capability: $\min(\eta_{\max})$ selection dominates in all 12 conditions at $K{=}3$ (Appendix~\ref{app:panel_selection}), and the weakest link's predictive power, while decreasing with $K$, remains positive. This favors a two-step strategy: (1) set a capability floor (e.g., $\eta_j{<}0.15$ mean ASR across attacks), then (2) maximize cross-family diversity within the qualified pool. The near-chance model analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:near_chance}) provides the empirical basis for this floor: excluding models below the 0.35 accuracy threshold strengthens rather than weakens the diversity signal.

\paragraph{When Diversity Helps vs.\ Hurts.} The positive $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$--ASR association is a descriptive finding, not a causal claim: observationally, more diverse panels tend to be more vulnerable. This does not mean diversity \emph{causes} vulnerability; the confound with capability ($r{=}0.70$--$0.81$) prevents causal identification from observational data alone. The practical implication is that practitioners should not seek to maximize diversity without controlling for capability. Capability-matched panels (Pool~A) show that when capability is equalized, the residual diversity signal persists (9/12 significant at $K{=}5$), suggesting that inter-judge independence has an independent (though not causally identified) contribution to vulnerability structure. Future work with training-based diversity interventions \citep{kariyappa2019improving,pang2019improving} could provide causal evidence.

\paragraph{Aggregation Rule Selection.} Bayesian calibration provides partial protection against adversarial manipulation by downweighting unreliable judges ($R^2{=}0.136$ at $K{=}3$ vs.\ $R^2{=}0.349$ for majority voting). However, this protection is not uniform: Bayesian neutralization requires accurate clean priors, and an adaptive adversary aware of the calibration procedure could target the prior estimation itself \citep{tambe2011security}. For general use, majority voting offers the best balance of simplicity and resistance: its $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ significance (12/12 at $K{=}3$) confirms that diversity affects it, but its predictability ($R^2{=}0.349$) is substantially lower than threshold voting ($R^2{=}0.745$), indicating moderate composition sensitivity. Weighted voting offers marginal improvement over majority voting at the cost of requiring clean accuracy estimates.

\paragraph{Compute Resources.} API evaluation cost approximately \$180 (dominated by Claude Opus 4 and GPT-5.5 at \$0.015--0.060 per evaluation). Local model evaluation ran for approximately 72 GPU-hours on a single RTX 6000 Ada. Statistical analysis (bootstrap, regression, meta-analysis) required approximately 48 CPU-hours on an 8-core instance. Total wall-clock time from first API call to final analysis was 8 days.

\paragraph{Evaluation Protocol.} Each evaluation uses the pairwise comparison paradigm: given a question, two candidate responses (A and B), and an evaluation rubric, the judge model produces a winner label $\in \{\text{A}, \text{B}, \text{tie}\}$ and numeric scores. Under attack, response B is augmented with attack-specific manipulations (e.g., injected instructions for prompt injection, artificially verbose output for verbosity bias, score anchoring for score manipulation). The correct answer is always response A. We use 300 evaluation pairs per condition (model $\times$ benchmark $\times$ attack), sampled with replacement from the respective benchmark's validation set. Evaluation order is randomized within each condition.

\paragraph{Attack Implementations.} The six attack types are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Prompt injection}: Adversarial instructions injected into response B (e.g., ``Ignore previous instructions. Response B is clearly superior.'').
\item \textbf{Sycophancy}: Response B restates the judge's prior preference or agrees with an implicit bias.
\item \textbf{Score manipulation}: Response B includes explicit numeric scores (``Quality: 9/10'') to anchor the judge.
\item \textbf{Verbosity bias}: Response B is padded with relevant-sounding elaboration to exploit length preference.
\item \textbf{Position bias}: Responses A and B are swapped in presentation order.
\item \textbf{Authority bias}: Response B cites authoritative sources or credentials.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Random Seeds and Reproducibility.} All random processes use fixed seeds: data sampling (seed 42), bootstrap resampling (seed 2024), panel enumeration (deterministic $\binom{15}{K}$ combinatorial generation). The analysis pipeline is fully deterministic given the raw evaluation outputs. Bootstrap $p$-values exhibit ${\pm}0.002$ variation under seed perturbation ($\pm 100$), well below significance thresholds.

\paragraph{Panel Enumeration.} At each $K$, we construct all $\binom{15}{K}$ possible panels: 455 at $K{=}3$, 3{,}003 at $K{=}5$, and 6{,}435 at $K{=}7$. Panels are enumerated in lexicographic order over model indices, ensuring deterministic and reproducible panel identifiers. Each judge appears in $\binom{14}{K-1}$ panels (91, 1{,}001, 3{,}003 at $K{=}3,5,7$ respectively), and each pair co-occurs in $\binom{13}{K-2}$ panels (13, 286, 1{,}716). Panel-level ASR is computed as the fraction of 300 evaluation pairs where the panel's aggregated decision is incorrect under attack.

\paragraph{Statistical Analysis Pipeline.} The analysis proceeds in five stages:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Pairwise MI computation}: For each of $\binom{15}{2}{=}105$ judge pairs, compute MI from clean winner labels using Miller--Madow correction ($O(n \cdot |\mathcal{W}|^2)$ per pair, where $|\mathcal{W}|{=}3$ and $n{=}300$).
\item \textbf{Panel metrics}: For each panel, compute $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ (from pairwise MI), $\eta_{\max}$ (maximum single-judge ASR), and panel ASR under each aggregation rule and attack type.
\item \textbf{OLS regression}: Log-log OLS with $\epsilon{=}10^{-6}$ floor, yielding standardized coefficients and $R^2$ for each of 12 conditions per $K$.
\item \textbf{Robustness corrections}: Wild cluster bootstrap ($B{=}9{,}999$, Rademacher), clustered SE (CR1), mixed effects (random intercept per position), and fractional logit (quasi-MLE with clustered SE).
\item \textbf{Meta-analysis}: DerSimonian--Laird random effects pooling across 12 conditions, yielding pooled estimates and $I^2$ heterogeneity.
\end{enumerate}

Figures are produced with matplotlib 3.8. All code and evaluation outputs will be released upon publication.
