## Core Problem

Natural language policy specifications for agents contain diverse linguistic ambiguities, and while recent work (DRIP-R) has begun classifying ambiguity types in policy documents, no study has established the causal link from specific ambiguity types to safety violation severity—leaving practitioners unable to prioritize which policy ambiguities to resolve first.

## Opportunity / Why Now

DRIP-R (May 2026) classifies five policy ambiguity types (vagueness, incompleteness, coordination ambiguity, missing conditions, semantic ambiguity) derived from requirements engineering taxonomies and shows frontier models achieve only 4.2% full agreement on ambiguous policy scenarios, but it evaluates decision quality and policy adherence without measuring safety impact per type. PolicyBank (Apr 2026) closes up to 82% of the performance gap toward a human oracle on policy-gap scenarios through iterative refinement, demonstrating that specification gaps systematically degrade agent behavior—but does not map which gap types cause safety violations versus capability errors. Policy-Invisible Violations (Apr 2026) reveals that even well-specified policies fail when policy-relevant facts are hidden at decision time, with frontier models violating in 90–98% of risky cases—demonstrating that specification quality interacts with information availability but not characterizing which specification features drive violation severity. ShieldAgent (ICML 2025) shows that formal safety rules can be extracted from policy documents into probabilistic rule circuits for enforcement, achieving 90.1% recall—but its formalization pipeline assumes disambiguated policies, and our work would identify which ambiguous clauses most urgently need such formalization. AgentPex (Mar 2026) demonstrates that behavioral rules can be automatically extracted from agent specifications and used to evaluate traces for compliance on τ²-bench, surfacing specification violations invisible to outcome-only scoring—providing a validated methodology for automated safety evaluation that we adopt as our evaluation backbone. LHAW (Feb 2026) provides a perturbation pipeline classifying underspecified task variants as outcome-critical, divergent, or benign, but operates on task instructions rather than policy clauses and uses functional categories rather than linguistic ambiguity types. The critical missing piece is a causal, empirical mapping: given a specific linguistic ambiguity type in a policy clause, what is the probability and severity of resulting safety violations? This is directly actionable—organizations could triage policy review effort by ambiguity type, resolving only those with high safety-violation potential—though the degree to which such prioritization generalizes across domains is itself an open empirical question we address.

## Landscape (SOTA & Limitations)

DRIP-R (arXiv, May 2026) is the closest concurrent work: it constructs 400 policy-ambiguous retail return scenarios across 5 ambiguity types derived from requirements engineering taxonomies (Massey et al. 2014; Gervasi et al. 2019) and evaluates agent decision-making quality, policy adherence, and interest alignment—but does not measure safety violations, does not distinguish safety-critical from benign ambiguities, and covers only retail. PolicyBank (arXiv, Apr 2026) demonstrates iterative policy refinement closing up to 82% of the gap toward a human oracle on τ-Bench policy-gap scenarios, identifying three gap types (ambiguous scope, under-specified exceptions, logical contradictions), but focuses on agent learning rather than ambiguity-to-safety mapping. Policy-Invisible Violations (arXiv, Apr 2026) introduces PhantomPolicy, a benchmark spanning 8 violation categories where policy-relevant facts are hidden at decision time; frontier models violate in 90–98% of risky cases, and the proposed Sentinel framework achieves 93.0% detection accuracy by grounding in organizational context—this is complementary to our work (they study information-visibility failures; we study specification-ambiguity failures). ShieldAgent (ICML 2025) extracts verifiable safety rules from policy documents into probabilistic rule circuits, outperforming prior guardrails by 11.3%; however, it presupposes that policies can be cleanly formalized—our work identifies which ambiguous clauses most urgently need formalization by quantifying their safety impact. AgentPex / Willful Disobedience (arXiv:2603.23806, Mar 2026) extracts behavioral rules from agent prompts and system instructions through three-stage specification extraction (policy specifications, task-specific specifications, argument specifications), then evaluates traces for compliance via seven specialized evaluators covering output compliance, transition constraints, forbidden sequences, argument groundedness, and outcome correctness; evaluated on 424 traces from τ²-bench across telecom, retail, and airline domains, it finds that 83% of traces with perfect outcome scores still contain procedural violations, demonstrating the necessity of specification-level evaluation beyond outcome metrics. AgentAuditor (NeurIPS 2025) achieves up to 96.3% F1 on R-Judge safety evaluation through memory-augmented reasoning that constructs experiential memory from structured semantic features and chain-of-thought traces, validated on ASSEBench (2,293 annotated interactions across 15 risk types and 29 scenarios)—we adopt its LLM-as-judge methodology for evaluating nuanced safety violations that resist rule-based detection. MANTRA (arXiv, May 2026) synthesizes SMT-validated compliance benchmarks from procedural manuals, producing 285 tasks across 6 domains with machine-checkable compliance checks; while it formalizes manual-to-benchmark translation, it does not address how ambiguity in the source manuals affects the safety of resulting agent behavior. ATBench (arXiv, Apr 2026) provides 1,000 agent trajectories with a three-dimensional risk taxonomy (risk source, failure mode, real-world harm) for trajectory-level safety evaluation; it offers a complementary safety evaluation framework but does not trace violations back to specification-level ambiguity causes. Policy Compiler (Feb 2026) translates NL policies to SMT constraints but requires human-guided formalization. AgentSpec (ICSE 2026) provides runtime enforcement DSLs assuming unambiguous specifications. LHAW (Feb 2026) creates 285 synthetic underspecified task variants classified as outcome-critical, divergent, or benign across four dimensions (goals, constraints, inputs, context), providing a transferable perturbation methodology—but targets task instructions, not policy documents, and does not use linguistic ambiguity categories. AMBIG-SWE (ICLR 2026) studies clarification-seeking under underspecified software engineering tasks but does not address policy documents or safety-severity classification. ClarEval (Mar 2026) evaluates code agent clarification skills under three ambiguity types (missing goals, premises, ambiguous terminology) but focuses on collaboration efficiency rather than safety. Ambig-DS (May 2026) benchmarks task-framing ambiguity in data-science agents, revealing silent misframing as a critical failure mode, but operates in the data-science domain without policy or safety focus. SafetyDrift (Mar 2026) models agent safety trajectories as absorbing Markov chains and predicts violations 3.7 steps in advance with 94.7% detection—a complementary runtime approach, but it does not trace violations back to their specification-level causes. PolicyGuard (Oct 2025) trains a lightweight 4B guardrail model on ~60K policy-trajectory examples for violation detection across web agent domains, demonstrating cross-domain generalization, but detects violations at execution time rather than predicting them from policy specification quality. CRAFT (EMNLP 2025) red-teams policy-adherent agents through persuasion but targets agent compliance under adversarial pressure, not specification quality. OpenAgentSafety (ICLR 2026) includes ambiguous user instructions but does not taxonomize ambiguity types or measure per-type safety impact. 'Beyond Accuracy: Policy Invariance' (May 2026) tests whether LLM safety judges respond consistently to policy rewrites but evaluates judge reliability rather than agent behavior. The NLP community has a comprehensive 11-type ambiguity taxonomy (Li et al. 2024: lexical, syntactic, scopal, elliptical, collective/distributive, implicative, presuppositional, idiomatic, coreferential, generic/non-generic, type/token) and the requirements engineering community has parallel 6-type taxonomies (Massey et al. 2014: lexical, syntactic, semantic, vagueness, incompleteness, referential), but neither has been applied to measure causal safety impact in agent policy specifications. No paper establishes the causal link: ambiguity type → safety violation rate and severity.

## Proposed Approach

We propose a three-phase method with explicit power analysis, multi-domain coverage, a validated safety evaluation pipeline, perturbation realism checks, and an honest test of cross-domain transferability. Phase 1 (Taxonomy & Annotation): Construct a unified 6-category ambiguity taxonomy grounded in two established source taxonomies. From Li et al. (2024)'s 11-type NLP ambiguity taxonomy, we select three types most relevant to policy specifications: (a) lexical ambiguity—policy terms with multiple valid meanings (e.g., 'sensitive data' meaning PII, financial records, or classified information); (b) scopal ambiguity—quantifier and modifier scope interactions in policy clauses (e.g., 'all agents may access two databases' admits collective vs. distributive readings); (c) coreferential ambiguity—unclear referents in multi-clause policies (e.g., 'the service should not share it with them' where 'it' and 'them' are ambiguous). We select these three because they correspond to the most frequent ambiguity patterns in preliminary policy clause analysis, and because the remaining 8 Li et al. types (syntactic, elliptical, collective/distributive, implicative, presuppositional, idiomatic, generic/non-generic, type/token) are either rare in policy documents (idiomatic, elliptical, generic/non-generic, type/token), captured more precisely by our policy-specific categories (collective/distributive → authorization scope; implicative/presuppositional → incompleteness), or subsumed by coreferential ambiguity in the policy context (syntactic ambiguity in policies primarily manifests as referential confusion). From Massey et al. (2014)'s requirements-engineering taxonomy of 6 types (lexical, syntactic, semantic, vagueness, incompleteness, referential), we adopt (d) incompleteness—missing conditions, undefined exceptions, or vague terms that leave policy interpretation underdetermined, which also subsumes DRIP-R's 'incompleteness,' 'missing conditions,' and much of 'vagueness.' We add two policy-domain-specific categories: (e) conditional precedence ambiguity—when multiple policy rules could apply to a situation and their priority ordering is unclear, and (f) authorization scope ambiguity—unclear boundaries of permissions, roles, or tool access in agent policies (e.g., 'authorized personnel may access the system' without specifying which functions). Cross-walk note: We explicitly acknowledge that the mapping from DRIP-R's 'coordination ambiguity'—which primarily concerns syntactic conjunction/disjunction scope (e.g., 'original or unused condition, with tags attached')—to our 'conditional precedence ambiguity'—which concerns rule priority ordering when multiple policy clauses apply—is approximate rather than exact. Both capture situations where multiple policy conditions interact to create interpretive uncertainty, but coordination ambiguity emphasizes syntactic scope while conditional precedence emphasizes logical priority. We retain this approximate mapping because the downstream behavioral manifestation is similar (agents must resolve which interpretation governs their action when multiple conditions interact), and because perfect 1-to-1 correspondence between taxonomies developed for different analytical purposes is neither expected nor necessary—the cross-walk's value lies in enabling comparative analysis across frameworks, not in asserting equivalence. This yields 6 categories with a cross-walk to both DRIP-R's 5 requirements-engineering types and Li et al.'s 11 NLP types: DRIP-R 'vagueness' → our lexical (#a) + incompleteness (#d); DRIP-R 'incompleteness'/'missing conditions' → our incompleteness (#d); DRIP-R 'coordination ambiguity' → our conditional precedence (#e) [approximate; see above]; DRIP-R 'semantic ambiguity' → our lexical (#a) + scopal (#b). We annotate ~1,000 policy clauses sourced from three domains: (i) TauBench retail and airline policies (~350 clauses), (ii) OpenAgentSafety benchmark policies (~350 clauses), and (iii) financial compliance policies drawn from FinVault's regulatory scenarios and EU-Agent-Bench's legislative policy excerpts (~300 clauses). Annotation uses LLM-assisted labeling (Qwen3-235B first pass) with human expert verification targeting κ ≥ 0.70 inter-annotator agreement. Power analysis: with ~1,000 clauses across 6 types, we expect ~167 clauses per type on average; for detecting medium effect sizes (Cohen's w = 0.30) in safety violation rate differences across types using χ² tests with df = 5, α = 0.05, and power = 0.80, we need N ≈ 143 total—our per-type sample sizes (~167) exceed even the per-cell requirements, providing substantial statistical headroom. Phase 2 (Controlled Experimentation with Validated Evaluation Pipeline): This phase has three sub-components: perturbation generation with realism validation, agent execution, and automated safety violation detection. (2a) Perturbation Generation & Realism Validation: Adapt LHAW's perturbation pipeline to the policy domain: for each ambiguity type, construct controlled policy variants that inject calibrated ambiguity into originally unambiguous policy clauses at two severity levels (subtle and overt), creating matched pairs for causal inference. Crucially, we validate perturbation realism through a two-pronged approach. First, a discriminator test: 3 annotators are shown 120 policy clauses (60 naturally ambiguous clauses sourced from DRIP-R's organically ambiguous Amazon return policy scenarios + 60 ambiguity-injected clauses from our pipeline), randomized and unlabeled, and asked to classify each as 'naturally occurring' vs. 'synthetically injected.' We target annotator classification accuracy ≤65% (where 50% = chance), indicating injected ambiguities are largely indistinguishable from natural ones. Second, a linguistic property comparison: we compute distributional statistics (sentence length, dependency parse depth, Flesch-Kincaid readability, type-token ratio) for injected vs. DRIP-R natural ambiguities and report Kolmogorov-Smirnov test p-values, targeting non-significant differences (p > 0.05) across all metrics. If either validation fails for specific ambiguity types, we iteratively refine the perturbation templates for those types before proceeding. (2b) Agent Execution: Execute six frontier and open-source agents (Claude Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5.4, Gemini 3 Pro, Qwen3-235B, Llama-4-Maverick, DeepSeek-V3) across these variants (~12,000 runs total: ~1,000 clauses × 2 severity levels × 6 agents), recording full interaction traces including all tool calls, API requests, and agent reasoning chains. The causal design—comparing matched ambiguous vs. unambiguous policy variants—isolates ambiguity's safety impact from confounding factors (task difficulty, agent capability). (2c) Automated Safety Violation Detection Pipeline: We employ a three-tier hybrid evaluation pipeline to classify safety violations across ~12,000 episodes, addressing the infeasibility of manual annotation at this scale. Tier 1 (Rule-Based Detection): Following AgentPex's specification-extraction methodology, we extract behavioral rules from each policy clause—output constraints, transition specifications, forbidden tool-call sequences, and argument specifications—using the 'explicit-only' extraction policy (only directly stated constraints, no inference). For each agent trace, we automatically check compliance against extracted rules for clearly classifiable violations: unauthorized tool calls, explicit data access violations, constraint boundary breaches, and forbidden action sequences. AgentPex's evaluation on τ²-bench demonstrates this approach surfaces violations invisible to outcome-only scoring, with 9/10 extracted rules fully grounded in source specifications and 90% agreement on specification coverage across independent extractions. Tier 2 (LLM-as-Judge for Nuanced Violations): For violations that resist rule-based detection—subtle privilege escalation, implicit policy boundary violations, ambiguity-exploiting workarounds—we deploy an AgentAuditor-inspired memory-augmented LLM judge. The judge constructs experiential memory from structured semantic features (scenario type, risk category, agent behavior pattern) with chain-of-thought reasoning traces from previously evaluated episodes, then uses retrieval-augmented generation to assess new traces. AgentAuditor achieves up to 96.3% F1 on the R-Judge safety benchmark via this approach. We use a three-tier severity classification: critical (outcome-affecting safety failures such as unauthorized data exposure or irreversible harmful actions), moderate (procedural safety violations such as skipping required verification steps), and minor (technical non-compliance without safety consequence). Tier 3 (Human Calibration): Two domain experts independently annotate a stratified random sample of ~500 episodes (~83 per ambiguity type, balanced across agents and severity levels), providing ground-truth safety labels. We compute (i) Cohen's κ between the two human annotators (targeting κ ≥ 0.75), (ii) precision, recall, and F1 of the automated pipeline (Tiers 1+2) against human ground truth, targeting ≥0.85 precision and ≥0.80 recall, and (iii) per-violation-type agreement rates to identify systematic biases. If automated-vs-human agreement falls below thresholds on specific violation types, we add type-specific evaluation rules or adjust judge prompts before scoring the remaining ~11,500 episodes. This calibration design follows established practice in large-scale safety evaluation (AgentPex's human spot-checking, AgentAuditor's ASSEBench validation) while scaling to our experimental requirements. Phase 3 (Predictive Prioritization & Domain-Transfer Test): Train a deployable safety-criticality classifier evaluated across multiple model scales to establish the accuracy-efficiency Pareto frontier: DeBERTa-v3-large (304M parameters), Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B, all fine-tuned on our annotated dataset. 两轮 MVP 实验（mvp_001 + diag_001，共 760 episodes）完成核心假设验证。Type effect hypothesis 被两轮否定（层内 p=0.50，跨层 p=0.46），但 binary effect 极强（+31-42pp，p<1e-7），在 4 种歧义类型、2 个模型上高度一致。论文 pivot 到 binary anchor + type uniformity null finding + error analysis。

### Recommendation
