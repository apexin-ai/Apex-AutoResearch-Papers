## Core Problem

Current homogenization metrics conflate within-individual diversity (how varied one person's outputs are) with between-individual diversity (how different people's outputs are from each other), making it impossible to formally explain why LLMs simultaneously improve individual creative quality while reducing collective diversity. Existing flat metrics (MATTR, MTLD, self-BLEU) operate at the single-document or single-author level and cannot partition ecosystem-level diversity into independent hierarchical components. The NLP diversity survey (2507.20858) cataloged 300+ papers but noted 'ad hoc' and 'cross-paper inconsistent' diversity measurement—yet no framework provides the missing hierarchical decomposition at the text-feature level. A reviewer might ask: 'Why not just compute average within-author pairwise cosine similarity (alpha-proxy) and between-author pairwise cosine similarity (beta-proxy)?' The answer is that such ad hoc proxies lack mathematical independence guarantees, cannot be decomposed into convergence vs. subsumption components, violate the replication principle essential for meaningful diversity comparisons, and offer no principled way to tune sensitivity to rare vs. common text features—all properties the ecological framework provides.

## Opportunity / Why Now

No existing work applies ecology's hierarchical alpha-beta-gamma diversity decomposition to text features within LLM-era text ecosystems. While Grin & Fürst (2022, Social Indicators Research) adapted ecological alpha-beta-gamma to sociolinguistic diversity—treating languages as 'species' and geographic regions as 'sites'—this operates at the macro-sociolinguistic level and has never been applied to text features (n-grams, syntactic structures, semantic clusters) within author corpora. The well-known mathematical bridge between Hill numbers and NLP perplexity (both are exponentials of Rényi entropy) makes this cross-domain transfer natural yet surprisingly unexploited for hierarchical decomposition. This framework directly resolves Doshi & Hauser's (2024) paradox: LLMs increase alpha-diversity (within-author richness and variety) while collapsing beta-diversity (between-author compositional distinctiveness), with gamma (total ecosystem diversity) declining because beta loss dominates. Crucially, Jost's (2007) abundance-based formulation operates on frequency distributions directly, avoiding the information-loss pitfalls of binary discretization, while Baselga's (2017) abundance-based extension decomposes beta into 'balanced variation' (analog of turnover) and 'abundance gradients' (analog of nestedness) without requiring presence/absence conversion. The ecological framework provides four specific advantages over simpler pairwise similarity proxies that constitute the core 'why ecology?' justification: (1) Hill numbers satisfy the replication principle—pooling N equally diverse, non-overlapping author corpora yields N× diversity—whereas pairwise cosine similarity violates this, leading to inconsistent comparisons across corpora of different sizes; (2) Jost's multiplicative partition (gamma = alpha × beta) guarantees mathematical independence between alpha and beta, whereas within-author and between-author cosine similarities are inherently correlated through shared feature distributions; (3) Hill numbers' parametric q-family provides a principled continuum from richness-sensitive (q=0) through Shannon/perplexity (q=1) to dominance-sensitive (q=2) diversity, revealing patterns invisible to any single metric; (4) Baselga's balanced-variation vs. abundance-gradient decomposition is algebraically impossible to derive from pairwise similarity measures—it requires the specific structure of Bray-Curtis-type dissimilarity applied to abundance vectors, providing diagnostic information about the mechanism of diversity loss that no pairwise similarity approach can recover.

## Landscape (SOTA & Limitations)

Guo, Shang & Clavel (2025, TACL) benchmark LLM diversity across lexical, syntactic, and semantic dimensions but treat these as independent axes without hierarchical structure. The task-dependent framework (arXiv 2509.21267) proposes taxonomy-guided LLM-judges (rs=0.93 with humans) but doesn't decompose individual vs. collective contributions. Doshi & Hauser (2024, Science Advances) quantified 8.9–10.7% inter-story similarity increase but lacked a formal framework explaining WHY individual quality improves while collective diversity drops. Padmakumar & He (ICLR 2024) showed InstructGPT reduces content diversity in argumentative essays with keystroke-level data, but measured only flat similarity metrics (pairwise RougeL, BertScore, n-gram uniqueness). The Stirling (2007) variety-balance-disparity framework was mentioned in the NLP diversity survey but never operationalized for LLM homogenization measurement. Grin & Fürst (2022, Social Indicators Research) applied ecological alpha-beta-gamma decomposition with Hill numbers to linguistic diversity, but at the sociolinguistic level (languages as species, geographic regions as sites)—not at the text-feature level relevant for LLM homogenization assessment. The Hill-number–perplexity bridge (both are exponentials of Rényi entropy) has been noted informally in NLP but never exploited for hierarchical diversity partitioning of text ecosystems. Chao & Chiu (2016, Methods in Ecology and Evolution) bridged the variance and diversity decomposition approaches to beta diversity, showing they yield the same similarity/differentiation measures when expressed as Hill numbers—this provides the theoretical backbone for our framework. Chao et al. (2023, Ecological Monographs) developed iNEXT.beta3D for rarefaction/extrapolation with beta diversity under a Hill numbers framework, providing the statistical machinery for bootstrap confidence intervals and coverage-based standardization essential for our small-sample datasets. IMPORTANT COUNTER-EVIDENCE: Ghods & Liu find that LLM homogenization 'disappears when LLMs are given even a small amount of context,' and Wan & Kalman (2026) show diverse AI personas can mitigate homogenization—these findings do not undermine our framework but rather motivate it, as the decomposition can diagnose WHEN and WHY homogenization occurs or is mitigated under different conditions. CONCURRENT WORK (2026): Deng, Brucks & Toubia (arXiv 2602.20408) identify two mechanisms—individual-level fixation and collective-level knowledge partitioning—and show persona+CoT interventions can restore diversity, but use ad hoc prompting interventions rather than a formal decomposition framework. Their mechanism identification is complementary to our approach: knowledge partitioning collapse maps conceptually to balanced-variation decline, while fixation maps to abundance-gradient increase—but this mapping is a testable hypothesis, not a proven equivalence. Dhingra (arXiv 2604.01504) organizes diversity along a normative axis (epistemic/interactional/societal/safety) but does not decompose hierarchically. Rios-Sialer (arXiv 2601.06116) proposes 'xeno-reproduction' for structure-aware diversity but lacks empirical validation and does not partition alpha/beta contributions. Liu (arXiv 2603.24124) shows alignment (DPO) causes 28.5% single-cluster homogenization on TruthfulQA but focuses on uncertainty estimation, not ecosystem-level decomposition. Sourati et al. (arXiv 2502.11266) provide temporal evidence of linguistic homogenization across four studies but offer no formal theoretical framework. None of these concurrent works apply ecological alpha-beta-gamma decomposition to text-feature ecosystems, confirming the gap remains open.

## Proposed Approach

Phase 1—Abundance-Based Ecological Framework with Core Justification: Formalize Whittaker's (1972) alpha-beta-gamma hierarchy for text ecosystems by defining text 'species' at three granularities—lexical (vocabulary n-grams with frequency counts), syntactic (dependency parse templates with occurrence frequencies), and semantic (embedding-space clusters via Qwen3-Embedding-8B with soft cluster membership probabilities)—with authors as 'sites' and corpora as 'regions.' We use Jost's (2007) abundance-based multiplicative partitioning throughout (gamma = alpha × beta in Hill-number effective-numbers form), operating directly on frequency distributions rather than binary presence/absence matrices. For q=1 (Shannon diversity), the Hill number equals the exponential of Shannon entropy—identical to NLP perplexity—providing a natural interpretation bridge. We further decompose beta using Baselga's (2017) abundance-based extension into 'balanced variation in abundance' (convergence: authors replacing distinctive expressions with shared LLM idioms at comparable frequencies) and 'abundance gradients' (subsumption: less creative authors becoming frequency-distribution subsets of more creative ones). CORE JUSTIFICATION ('Why Ecology?')—a dedicated section in the paper will present four formal arguments for why the ecological framework reveals information inaccessible to simpler pairwise similarity alternatives: (A) REPLICATION PRINCIPLE: Hill numbers satisfy the replication/doubling property—if N equally diverse, equally large author corpora share no features, pooled diversity equals N × single-corpus diversity. Pairwise cosine similarity lacks this property, making cross-corpus comparisons uninterpretable. We prove this formally for our text-feature setting. (B) MATHEMATICAL INDEPENDENCE: Jost's multiplicative partition ensures alpha and beta are mathematically independent—alpha can change without constraining beta and vice versa. We demonstrate empirically that within-author and between-author cosine similarities are correlated (r > 0.5 expected), violating independence and conflating the signal the framework aims to separate. (C) PARAMETRIC q-FAMILY: Hill numbers at q=0 measure feature richness (vocabulary breadth), at q=1 measure Shannon diversity (=perplexity, capturing both richness and evenness), and at q=2 measure Simpson diversity (dominance-sensitive, capturing whether a few features dominate). We report all three orders to reveal whether LLM homogenization primarily affects rare features (visible at q=0), typical features (q=1), or dominant features (q=2)—information no single pairwise metric can provide. (D) BASELGA DECOMPOSITION UNIQUENESS: The decomposition of beta into balanced-variation and abundance-gradient components requires the algebraic structure of Bray-Curtis dissimilarity applied to abundance vectors. We prove by construction that no function of pairwise cosine similarity matrices can recover these components—specifically, because cosine similarity is invariant to absolute scaling of feature vectors, it cannot distinguish balanced substitution (equal-frequency replacement) from abundance loss (subset formation), which differ precisely in their absolute frequency structure. Phase 2—Statistical Rigor and Robustness Analysis: We adopt a comprehensive statistical plan addressing sample size adequacy and multiple comparisons. BOOTSTRAP CONFIDENCE INTERVALS: All alpha, beta, and gamma diversity estimates are accompanied by 95% bootstrap confidence intervals using the iNEXT.beta3D methodology (Chao et al. 2023, Ecological Monographs), with nboot=200 replications. Following Chao et al., we use coverage-based standardization rather than size-based standardization to compare diversity across conditions with potentially different sampling completeness—this is critical because LLM-assisted writing may produce longer texts with different feature-sample coverage than unassisted writing. Coverage-based standardization ensures that observed diversity differences reflect genuine compositional differences rather than artifacts of unequal sampling effort. RAREFACTION AND EXTRAPOLATION: For q=1 and q=2, we extrapolate to asymptotic diversity estimates (reliable per Chao et al. 2023); for q=0, we extrapolate to at most double the reference sample size. We report rarefaction curves to assess whether samples are approaching asymptotic diversity, indicating sampling adequacy. SAMPLE SIZE ADEQUACY: The Doshi & Hauser dataset (~98 stories per condition) and Padmakumar & He dataset (38 writers, ~100 essays per condition across 10 topics) represent modest but adequate sample sizes for ecological diversity estimation—many published ecological studies compute Hill-number-based alpha-beta decompositions with 10–50 sites (here, authors serve as 'sites'). The college admissions corpus (~2,200 essays, ~1,100 per condition) provides substantial statistical power. We report sample coverage (Chao & Jost, 2012) for each condition to quantify sampling completeness. SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS AND MULTIPLE COMPARISONS: Systematically test sensitivity of the alpha/beta decomposition to species definition choices across all three granularities: vary semantic cluster k from 50 to 1000 (with Silhouette Score and Calinski-Harabasz Index validation), n-gram order from 2 to 5, and syntactic parse template depth from 1 to 3, yielding a grid of 5 × 4 × 3 = 60 parameter configurations. The primary hypothesis—that beta diversity declines under LLM assistance while alpha increases—is tested as a directional sign consistency across configurations (what fraction of 60 configurations show this pattern?), with a threshold of ≥90% sign consistency for claiming robustness. For individual configuration-level significance tests (paired permutation tests comparing beta between LLM and non-LLM conditions), we apply Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction at q=0.05 across the 60 comparisons to control the false discovery rate. Additionally, compare hard clustering (k-means) vs. soft clustering (Gaussian mixture models with probabilistic assignments) to assess discretization sensitivity. Report Spearman rank correlations of alpha and beta estimates across parameter settings to quantify robustness. EFFECT SIZE REPORTING: Report Cliff's delta (non-parametric effect size) for alpha and beta differences between conditions, alongside bootstrap CIs, to provide interpretable magnitudes independent of sample size. Phase 3—Multi-Dataset Validation: Validate on three publicly available datasets spanning different writing tasks and LLM-exposure conditions: (a) the Doshi & Hauser (2024) Dryad dataset (293 stories, ~98 per condition across 3 LLM-exposure conditions, CC-BY licensed), (b) the Padmakumar & He (ICLR 2024) argumentative essays dataset (38 writers × 3 conditions = ~100 essays per condition across 10 topics; keystroke-level data available at github.com/vishakhpk/hai-diversity), and (c) the college admissions essays corpus (~2,200 essays, human vs. GPT-4, available on Open Science Framework from the ScienceDirect 2025 study). For the Padmakumar dataset, we treat authors as 'sites' (38 sites) and compute within-condition alpha/beta; the within-subjects design (each author writes in all conditions) enables paired comparisons that increase statistical power. Benchmark against existing flat metrics (MATTR, MTLD, self-BLEU, pairwise cosine similarity, RougeL similarity, Guo et al.'s TACL metrics) to show that the ecological decomposition captures hierarchical variance they cannot—specifically, we demonstrate empirically that within-author and between-author cosine similarities are correlated (violating independence), and that flat metrics cannot distinguish convergence-type from subsumption-type homogenization. We show the framework explains previously contradictory results across datasets by revealing whether homogenization manifests as balanced-variation collapse or abundance-gradient increase. Phase 4—Exploratory Hypothesis Generation for Targeted Interventions: We frame the prescriptive component as hypothesis-generating rather than hypothesis-confirming, deriving theoretically motivated intervention hypotheses from the framework's diagnostic components. THEORETICAL MOTIVATION (not formal derivation): Balanced-variation collapse occurs when authors lose their distinctive feature-substitution patterns—mathematically, the balanced differences in feature frequencies across authors decrease, meaning authors converge to similar features at comparable frequencies. This structural pattern is analogous to Deng et al.'s (2026) knowledge-partitioning collapse, where LLM users no longer occupy distinct regions of the semantic space. Abundance-gradient increase occurs when some authors' feature sets become nested frequency-distribution subsets of others'—mathematically, an asymmetric relationship where less diverse authors produce feature distributions contained within those of more diverse authors.
