## Core Problem

Current synthetic text detection is binary (real vs. synthetic), but the practical value of synthetic training data depends critically on its recursive generation depth — how many model-to-model training cycles it has undergone. First-generation synthetic data can improve training, while deeply recursive content drives distribution collapse. No existing method estimates this ordinal depth from text alone, leaving a critical gap for data curation pipelines operating on web-scraped corpora of unknown provenance.

## Opportunity / Why Now

By early 2026, over 60% of data used in AI projects is synthetically generated (Gartner), creating uncontrolled recursive feedback loops in web-scraped training data. Existing detectors answer 'is this synthetic?' (LOKI, RADAR, Binoculars, DivEye) or 'how much was AI-edited?' (EditLens, ICLR 2026), but not 'how many recursive training cycles has this text undergone?' — a critical distinction since 1st-gen rephrased data improves training while 3rd+ gen text drives collapse (Nature 2024). Meanwhile, the 'Model Collapse Does Not Mean What You Think' position paper (NeurIPS 2025) argues that catastrophic collapse requires unrealistic assumptions, but acknowledges that diversity collapse and quality degradation are real concerns under practical conditions. This reframing strengthens rather than weakens our motivation: even without catastrophic collapse, knowing generation depth enables principled synthetic data quality scoring for curation pipelines — retaining high-quality shallow-generation content while filtering deeply recursive text. This directly addresses EMNLP 2026's special theme on data contamination and feedback loops.

## Landscape (SOTA & Limitations)

Synthetic text detection is a mature binary classification task: LOKI (ICLR 2025 Spotlight) benchmarks detection across modalities; DivEye (TMLR 2026) achieves SOTA zero-shot detection via surprisal variability features; five methods are considered reliable as of 2026 (Min-K%, ConStat, time-based partition, canary GUID, guided completion). EditLens (ICLR 2026) is the first detector to estimate the continuous extent of AI editing rather than binary classification, but addresses single-pass editing, not recursive generation. For model collapse, Drayson et al. (EMNLP 2025) analyzed text characteristics across 100 generations of GPT-2 and SmolLM2 under six decoding strategies, finding non-monotonic degradation patterns — notably that pure sampling increases perplexity variance rather than converging, while temperature sampling shows 70% diversity collapse. They used binary detection with importance resampling to prevent collapse but did not attempt depth classification. The 'Recursive Training Loops' paper (EMNLP 2025 Oral) showed lexical diversity amplifies distribution shift while semantic diversity mitigates it, but assumed depth is known a priori. For ordinal classification methods, CLOC (CVPR 2025) introduced multi-margin N-pair contrastive learning with learnable margins for ordinal tasks, and GoR (ICLR 2026) reformulated ordinal regression as sequential token generation. Neither has been applied to text depth estimation. The key gap: no existing work treats recursive generation depth as an estimable quantity from text alone — all analysis assumes depth is known from controlled experiments.

## Proposed Approach

We formalize the task of ordinal generation depth estimation: given a text sample, predict its recursive generation depth d ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5} where 0 = human-authored and 1–5 = successive model-to-model training cycles. Our method has three components:

**1. Multi-Generational Benchmark Construction.** We construct depth-labeled corpora by iteratively fine-tuning three 1–2B parameter open-source LLMs — Pythia-1.4B, OLMo-1B, and Qwen2.5-1.8B — on their own outputs for 5 generations using LoRA, across three decoding strategies (nucleus p=0.95, temperature τ=0.9, top-k k=50). Each generation fine-tunes a fresh base model on the previous generation's output, producing 9 independent recursive chains (3 models × 3 strategies) with ~30K samples per generation per chain. This yields ~540K depth-labeled samples total.

**2. Depth-Contrastive Ordinal Learning (DCOL).** We adapt CLOC's multi-margin N-pair contrastive loss (CVPR 2025) to text, using a DeBERTa-v3-large encoder to learn depth-aware representations. The key insight: distances between adjacent generation depths are non-uniform (the gap between human and gen-1 text is far larger than between gen-3 and gen-4), so fixed-margin approaches like CORAL underperform. DCOL uses learnable margins between each pair of adjacent depths, preserving ordinal structure while allowing the embedding space to capture the actual geometry of degradation. We augment learned representations with interpretable statistical features — DivEye-style surprisal variability (variance/entropy of token-level surprisal under a frozen reference model), Min-K%-style log-probability percentiles, and lexical diversity metrics (type-token ratio, hapax ratio, n-gram entropy) — fused via a lightweight MLP head for final ordinal prediction.

**3. Cross-Strategy Feature Analysis.** We systematically study which features degrade monotonically with depth across all decoding strategies vs. which are strategy-dependent, directly addressing Drayson et al.'s finding of non-monotonic patterns. Rather than assuming monotonicity, we empirically identify robust depth indicators and show DCOL's contrastive representations learn strategy-invariant depth signals.

Evaluation: (a) leave-one-model-family-out generalization (train on 2 families, test on 3rd); (b) leave-one-strategy-out robustness; (c) mixed-provenance evaluation on synthetic documents constructed from paragraphs sampled from different generation depths, inspired by PAN 2025/2026 mixed authorship tasks; (d) baselines including repurposed binary detectors (Binoculars, DivEye), perplexity regression, CORAL ordinal regression, EditLens (continuous scoring), and zero-shot LLM prompting.
