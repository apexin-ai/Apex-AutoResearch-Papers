## Core Problem

Non-English languages suffer from 5x worse calibration error (ECE) compared to English in multilingual LLMs. Existing fixes require per-language validation data (LACE) or expensive multilingual sampling (MKA). Output-based calibration methods are further limited by a fundamental representational constraint: the model's internal accuracy estimate and its verbalized confidence occupy orthogonal subspaces (cosine similarity < 0.04 across all layers; Miao & Ungar, 2026), meaning softmax probabilities and prompted confidence scores are structurally decoupled from what the model actually knows about its own correctness. While recent work has shown that knowledge-boundary recognition (answerable vs. unanswerable) can transfer cross-lingually via linear probes at middle layers (Xiao et al., ACL 2025), calibration — the alignment between model confidence and actual correctness — is a fundamentally different and harder transfer target: it depends not just on what the model knows, but on how its capabilities, representation quality, and confidence interact per-language. Can we transfer English calibration quality to underserved languages through shared intermediate-layer correctness directions, and at what layer depth — given a tradeoff between calibration quality (peaking at 53-91% depth) and cross-lingual transferability (peaking at 24-49% depth depending on the model architecture)?

## Opportunity / Why Now

Ji et al. (EMNLP 2025) discovered that verbal uncertainty is governed by a single linear direction in representation space. Joshi et al. (EMNLP 2025) showed calibration evolves across layers with a low-dimensional calibration direction in the residual stream. Zhou et al. (arXiv 2510.03136) introduced LACE, demonstrating that late-intermediate layers encode better-calibrated multilingual signals but requiring per-language validation. Liu & Niehues (ACL 2025) showed that middle layers form the strongest language-agnostic shared space, and Ravisankar et al. (EACL 2026) provided causal evidence via activation patching that incorrect non-English predictions are fixed by substituting English middle-layer activations — establishing that English representations causally drive non-English correctness across 24 languages. Critically, Xiao et al. (ACL 2025, arXiv 2504.13816) demonstrated that knowledge-boundary perception — a model-dependent property — transfers cross-lingually via linear probes with training-free alignment (mean shifting + linear projection) at middle-to-upper layers, achieving near in-distribution accuracy (e.g., closing the 89% to 79% zero-shot gap for English to Khmer). However, they probe binary known/unknown classification on specialized datasets, not correctness on standard QA or calibration metrics. A crucial mechanistic insight comes from Miao & Ungar (arXiv 2603.25052, March 2026), who showed that the internal accuracy direction (what we call the 'correctness direction') and the verbalized confidence direction are orthogonal (cosine similarity < 0.04 across all layers of three 7-8B models), meaning the model's internal knowledge of its own correctness is linearly accessible but structurally separate from what it expresses as confidence — this motivates probing internal representations rather than relying on output-based calibration, and opens the question of whether this linearly accessible correctness direction transfers cross-lingually. Their 'Reasoning Contamination Effect' further shows that joint reasoning+confidence estimation drives these directions into opposition (cosine similarity shifting from +0.26 to -0.63), making the case for representation-level approaches even stronger. Civelli et al. (arXiv 2601.12731, Jan 2026) showed cross-lingual difficulty probe transfer at intermediate layers, but critically, the optimal transfer depth is architecture-dependent: 24% for Qwen3-8B, 32% for LLaMA-3.2-3B, 38% for LLaMA-3.2-1B, 49% for LLaMA-3.1-8B — establishing that the shallow-transferable vs. deep-accurate tradeoff is architecture-dependent and cannot be summarized by a single depth. The Confidence Manifold (arXiv 2602.08159, Feb 2026) showed correctness signals occupy just 3-8 dimensions and are linearly separable, with 25 labeled examples achieving 89% of full-data probe accuracy. Khanmohammadi et al. (EMNLP 2025, arXiv 2505.21772) demonstrated CCPS — calibration via probing perturbed representation stability at the final hidden layer — achieving ~55% ECE reduction and ~21% Brier score improvement across Llama, Qwen, and Mistral 8-32B models on MMLU/MMLU-Pro, but entirely English-only and restricted to the final layer, leaving open whether such representation-level calibration approaches extend cross-lingually or benefit from multi-layer analysis. Additionally, differentiable calibration objectives (SB-ECE; Karandikar et al., NeurIPS 2021) enable direct optimization of calibration quality during probe training, but have never been combined with cross-lingual consistency constraints. Nobody has tested whether correctness/calibration probes — optimized for ECE and Brier score rather than classification accuracy — transfer cross-lingually, nobody has mapped the Pareto frontier of layer depth x calibration quality x cross-lingual transferability across architectures, and nobody has proposed a probe training objective that jointly targets correctness accuracy AND cross-lingual calibration invariance. This frontier, combined with the architecture-dependent optimal depth and a calibration-aware cross-lingual training objective, is the key to practical deployment.

## Landscape (SOTA & Limitations)

Zhou et al. (arXiv 2510.03136) introduced LACE, which adaptively ensembles late-intermediate layers (75-91% depth) per language but still requires per-language validation data, and confirmed the 5x ECE gap across 100+ languages and 6 model families (LLaMA-3: 23.1% vs 4.6% ECE for non-English vs English). MKA (Duwal et al., ICLR Workshop 2025) uses cross-lingual consensus for abstention but at 5x inference cost. Joshi et al. (EMNLP 2025) identified a low-dimensional calibration direction in the residual stream. Xiao et al. (ACL 2025, arXiv 2504.13816) — a close prior work — trained linear probes on knowledge-boundary perception (distinguishing answerable from unanswerable questions, including false-premise and non-existent entity queries) and transferred them cross-lingually across 8 languages in Qwen2.5-7B/14B/72B and LLaMA-3.1-8B. They showed that zero-shot transfer drops approximately 10 points (89% to 79% for English to Khmer) but training-free alignment via mean shifting and linear projection 'nearly closes the gap with in-distribution accuracy.' Critical differences from our work: (1) they probe knowledge boundaries (binary: model recognizes it cannot answer), not correctness (binary: model answered correctly) — knowledge boundaries concern meta-cognitive self-awareness while correctness combines difficulty, capability, and representation quality; (2) they measure probing accuracy, not calibration metrics (ECE, Brier score) — good boundary detection does not imply good calibration; (3) they test 8 languages on specialized false-premise/unanswerable datasets, not 50+ languages on standard QA benchmarks; (4) they use models up to 72B (dense), not 235B MoE; (5) they do not characterize the layer-depth Pareto frontier or the architecture-dependent optimal depth. Miao & Ungar (arXiv 2603.25052, March 2026) — the closest concurrent mechanistic work — used ridge regression probes and contrastive activation addition (CAA) steering to show that the internal accuracy direction ('gold calibration direction') and verbalized confidence direction are orthogonal (cosine similarity < 0.04 across all layers) in Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, and Mistral-7B across MMLU, MATH, TriviaQA, and TruthfulQA. Their two-stage adaptive steering pipeline reads internal accuracy and steers verbalized output to match, achieving 4-7x ECE improvement (e.g., 14.9% to 3.7% on MATH for Llama-3.1-8B). Crucially, their work clarifies the distinction between what we probe (the correctness/accuracy direction — the model's internal estimate of whether it answered correctly) and what output-based methods capture (the verbalized confidence direction). These are orthogonal, meaning output-based calibration is fundamentally limited. They also discovered the 'Reasoning Contamination Effect': joint reasoning+confidence estimation shifts the two directions from weakly aligned (+0.26 cosine) to sharply opposed (-0.63 cosine), further motivating representation-level approaches. Their work is entirely English-only and at 7-8B scale, making cross-lingual transfer of the correctness direction — our core contribution — a natural and significant extension. Ravisankar et al. (EACL 2026, arXiv 2504.09378) introduced DALI (Discriminative Alignment Index) showing that incorrect non-English predictions correlate with low alignment with English at middle layers, and that activation patching of English representations causally fixes errors across 24 languages — providing the strongest causal evidence for our hypothesis that English-trained probes can inform non-English calibration. Civelli et al. (arXiv 2601.12731, Jan 2026) showed cross-lingual difficulty probe transfer at intermediate layers using linear Ridge regression probes trained on IRT-based human difficulty scores across 21 languages, but critically, the optimal transfer depth is architecture-dependent: 24% for Qwen3-8B, 32% for LLaMA-3.2-3B, 38% for LLaMA-3.2-1B, 49% for LLaMA-3.1-8B. Within-language probes achieve rho 0.80-0.85 while cross-lingual probes achieve rho 0.76-0.78 at optimal transfer layers, with deep probes (94% depth) suffering 0.05-0.19 rho degradation when transferred. They probed IRT-based human difficulty — an inherent property of questions — not model correctness; their difficulty probes serve as a critical control for disentangling question difficulty from model-language-specific correctness in our ablation analysis. CORAL (Miao et al., arXiv 2602.06022, Feb 2026) demonstrates transferable correctness probes via regularized 4-layer MLP on residual activations with approximately 50% ECE improvement at layers 17-21 (53-66% depth), but evaluates only in English across tasks and shows degraded transfer on domain-shifted benchmarks. The Confidence Manifold (arXiv 2602.08159, Feb 2026) shows correctness signals occupy 3-8 dimensions and are linearly separable across 9 models and 5 architecture families, with few-shot (25 examples) achieving 89% of full-data accuracy — all English-only. Confidence Geometry Reveals Trace-Level Correctness (arXiv 2605.16824, May 2026) extends geometric correctness analysis to reasoning traces using output-level confidence trajectories — operating at a different level (output tokens, not internal representations) with no multilingual evaluation. Khanmohammadi et al. (EMNLP 2025, arXiv 2505.21772) introduced CCPS, which probes calibration by measuring representational stability under targeted adversarial perturbations of final hidden states, achieving ~55% ECE reduction and ~21% Brier score improvement across Llama-3/Qwen2.5/Mistral 8-32B on MMLU and MMLU-Pro — but entirely English-only and restricted to the final layer, not exploring cross-lingual transfer or multi-layer analysis. Instruction-tuning studies (arXiv 2601.01362, Jan 2026) confirm that English-centric fine-tuning inflates confidence in low-resource languages without accuracy gains, worsening calibration. Cross-lingual steering vectors have been demonstrated for language identity and task performance (Mahmoud et al., arXiv 2505.12584; arXiv 2601.16390) but never for calibration. Differentiable calibration objectives — SB-ECE (Karandikar et al., NeurIPS 2021) using soft binning with temperature-parameterized membership functions — have achieved state-of-the-art ECE as auxiliary training losses (82% ECE reduction with <1% accuracy loss on CIFAR-100), but have never been applied to probe training or combined with cross-lingual invariance constraints. No work has attempted to transfer the correctness/calibration direction itself cross-lingually, characterized the Pareto frontier between calibration quality and cross-lingual transferability, proposed a probe training objective jointly targeting correctness and cross-lingual calibration invariance, or controlled for question difficulty when analyzing cross-lingual correctness probe transfer.

Resource Note on INT8 for Llama 4 Scout: INT8 weight quantization (W8A16) reduces memory from ~220GB (3-4 A100s at FP16) to ~109GB (2 A100-80GBs), while activations are still computed and extracted in FP16/BF16, preserving hidden-state fidelity. We validate this choice with an ablation on a random 5% subset comparing INT8 vs FP16 probing results at 5 representative layers — prior work shows INT8 quantization has minimal effect on confidence and calibration properties (Huang et al., arXiv 2508.16785), with 1-3% accuracy degradation, and middle layers (where we primarily probe) show minimal dead-neuron perturbation. Phase 2 — English Probe Training: At each extracted layer, fit both a logistic regression probe (linear) and a lightweight 2-layer MLP probe to identify the correctness direction — the hyperplane or surface best separating correct from incorrect answers in representation space. We explicitly note, following Miao & Ungar (2026), that we probe the CORRECTNESS direction (= their 'gold calibration direction': the model's internal estimate of whether it answered correctly), NOT the verbalized confidence direction — these are orthogonal (cosine similarity < 0.04) and we target the former because it is the true calibration-relevant signal. Compare probe complexity: linear probes may transfer better cross-lingually (by analogy to the shallow-transferable finding in Civelli et al.), while MLPs may capture richer correctness signals (as CORAL found). Phase 3 — Pareto Frontier Analysis (Core Contribution 1): Apply each layer's English-trained probe to non-English activations across 20 languages (core) / 50+ languages (camera-ready) under five transfer conditions: (a) zero-shot (direct application), (b) mean-shift alignment (compute delta-mu between English and target-language activation means, shift target activations; per Xiao et al., ACL 2025), (c) linear projection alignment (learn least-squares projection from small parallel set; per Xiao et al., ACL 2025), (d) few-shot probe fine-tuning (25-100 target-language examples; motivated by Confidence Manifold's finding that 25 examples achieve 89% of full-data accuracy), and (e) CaCLR — Calibration-Aware Cross-Lingual Regularization (see Phase 3e). For each layer x transfer condition, measure (i) calibration quality (ECE, Brier score) and (ii) cross-lingual transfer quality (AUROC degradation from English). Map the full Pareto frontier of layer depth x calibration quality x cross-lingual transferability for each transfer condition and each model. Critically, given that optimal cross-lingual transfer depth varies from 24% (Qwen3-8B) to 49% (LLaMA-3.1-8B) for difficulty probes, we test whether correctness probes show a similar architecture-dependent pattern, and whether alignment techniques flatten this variance by enabling effective transfer at deeper, better-calibrated layers. Phase 3e — CaCLR: Calibration-Aware Cross-Lingual Regularization (Core Contribution 2 — Methodological): CaCLR is a novel probe training objective that jointly optimizes for English correctness prediction AND cross-lingual calibration invariance, transforming the paper from pure empirical characterization into a proposed method + characterization. Formulation: L_CaCLR = L_BCE(probe(h_en), y_en) + alpha * L_SB-ECE(probe(h_en), y_en) + beta * L_consist(probe(h_en), probe(align(h_X))). The first term trains the probe to predict English correctness. The second term uses SB-ECE — differentiable soft-binned Expected Calibration Error (Karandikar et al., NeurIPS 2021), which replaces discrete ECE bins with temperature-parameterized soft membership functions enabling gradient-based optimization — to explicitly optimize English calibration during training, not just post-hoc. The third term penalizes divergence between the probe's outputs on English activations and aligned (mean-shifted or projected) non-English activations for parallel questions, enforcing cross-lingual output consistency. Crucially, CaCLR requires NO non-English correctness labels: the consistency term uses only parallel activations (available from Phase 1), and the SB-ECE term operates on English data only. The key hypothesis: by jointly optimizing calibration quality (via SB-ECE) and cross-lingual invariance (via consistency), CaCLR produces probes that transfer calibration more effectively than probes trained with standard BCE loss alone, particularly at deeper layers where calibration is superior but zero-shot transfer fails. We compare CaCLR-trained probes against conditions (a)-(d) across the full Pareto frontier. Hyperparameters alpha and beta are tuned on a held-out English validation set with a small (5-language) cross-lingual validation set. Phase 4 — Frontier Shifting Analysis: Quantify how each alignment/adaptation technique shifts the Pareto frontier. Key hypotheses: (H1) mean-shift and linear projection alignment (which require only unlabeled parallel activations, no correctness labels) may enable effective transfer at deeper layers where calibration is superior (75-91% depth) but zero-shot transfer fails — potentially resolving the depth-transferability tradeoff without requiring labeled target-language data; (H2) CaCLR dominates conditions (a)-(c) because it internalizes the alignment objective during training, producing inherently more transferable probes; (H3) CaCLR + few-shot adaptation achieves the best Pareto frontier overall, combining training-time invariance with target-language fine-tuning.
