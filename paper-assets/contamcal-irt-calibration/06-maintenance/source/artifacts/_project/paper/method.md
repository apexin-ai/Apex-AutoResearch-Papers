## 3 Method

ContamCal is a two-stage framework that maps observed item responses and contamination exposure signals to calibrated ability estimates. Stage 1 produces per-item exposure scores from multiple detection signals via training-free fusion. Stage 2 fits a contamination-augmented IRT model that decomposes observed performance into latent ability and contamination effects.

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

Let $y_{ijk} \in \{0,1\}$ denote the binary response of model $j$ ($j=1,\ldots,J$) to item $i$ ($i=1,\ldots,I_k$) on benchmark $k$. Standard IRT models assume $P(y_{ijk}=1) = f(\theta_j, \boldsymbol{\xi}_i)$ where $\theta_j$ is latent ability and $\boldsymbol{\xi}_i$ are item parameters. When training data contains benchmark items, observed ability $\hat{\theta}_j$ confounds genuine capability with memorization.

Our goal is to estimate $\theta_j^{\text{clean}}$, the decontaminated ability---what model $j$'s performance would be had it never encountered the test items during training. We approach this through contamination-augmented IRT, drawing on item compromise IRT (Eckerly, 2017; Sinharay, 2017) and explanatory IRT (De Boeck & Wilson, 2004). Adapting these frameworks to LLM evaluation requires three structural modifications:

**(a) Independent examinees $\to$ shared item distribution.** Classical IRT assumes independent test-takers. In LLM evaluation, a single model produces all item responses, and items are fixed across models. We treat the model ensemble as the examinee population and fit per-benchmark to limit local independence violations.

**(b) Binary leakage $\to$ continuous exposure.** Item compromise IRT uses binary indicators (leaked / not leaked). LLM contamination is graded: a model may have seen an item zero, once, or hundreds of times during training. We replace binary indicators with continuous exposure scores $e_{ij} \in [0,1]$ estimated from detection signals.

**(c) Stable ability $\to$ regime-conditional ability.** In educational testing, ability is approximately constant regardless of item compromise status. For LLMs, contamination can alter internal representations, so we model ability as conditional on the contamination regime and recover clean ability via counterfactual inference.

### 3.2 Stage 1: Multi-Signal Exposure Mapping

We estimate per-item exposure scores from three complementary contamination detection signals:

**S1: Embedding similarity.** Cosine similarity between the sentence embedding of each benchmark item and its nearest neighbor in the SFT training corpus, computed via a frozen encoder. High similarity indicates verbatim or near-verbatim inclusion.

**S2: Min-K++ (Zhang et al., 2024).** A membership inference signal based on the model's own token-level surprisal distribution. Items seen during training are expected to exhibit lower perplexity on their rarest tokens. We include this signal despite known failure modes (Section 5.2) to systematically characterize signal reliability across scales.

**S4: Self-critique (Weller et al., 2024).** A generation-based signal where the model is prompted to assess whether it has encountered a given item during training.

We exclude S3 (ConStat; Dekoninck et al., 2024) because its gap-based statistic produces zero values for items where all answer options have identical surprisal, diluting the signal, and its low item coverage introduces selection bias.

**Aggregation.** We fuse signals via Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF; Cormack et al., 2009):

$$e_i^{\text{raw}} = \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} \frac{w_s}{k + \text{rank}_s(i)}$$

where $\text{rank}_s(i)$ is the rank of item $i$ under signal $s$ (rank 1 = most suspicious), $k=60$ is the smoothing constant, and $w_s = 1$ for all signals. Raw scores are min-max normalized per model to obtain $e_{ij} \in [0,1]$.

RRF is a training-free fusion method. This is a deliberate design choice rather than a limitation: any learned aggregator requires labeled training data, but contamination labels are precisely what we lack---the aggregator's training data may itself be contaminated. We term this the *meta-circularity* problem. RRF sidesteps it by operating on rank orderings without parameter estimation.

### 3.3 Stage 2: Contamination-Augmented IRT

We extend the four-parameter logistic (4PL) IRT model with a contamination covariate. For each benchmark $k$, fitted independently:

$$P(y_{ijk}=1) = c_i + (d_i - c_i)\,\sigma\!\bigl(a_i(\theta_j - b_i) + \gamma_k \cdot e_{ij}\bigr)$$

where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic function, $a_i > 0$ is item discrimination, $b_i$ is item difficulty, $c_i \in (0,1)$ is the guessing asymptote, $d_i \in (0,1)$ is the ceiling asymptote, $\theta_j$ is model ability, $e_{ij}$ is the exposure score from Stage 1, and $\gamma_k$ is the benchmark-specific contamination effect.

The contamination term $\gamma_k \cdot e_{ij}$ enters additively on the logit scale, outside the discrimination parameter $a_i$. This makes $\gamma_k$ directly interpretable: it represents the logit shift for a maximally exposed item ($e_{ij}=1$) relative to a clean item ($e_{ij}=0$), uniformly across items regardless of their discrimination.

Crucially, $\gamma_k$ is signed: $\gamma_k > 0$ indicates performance inflation (memorization benefit), while $\gamma_k < 0$ indicates interference or forgetting---a possibility absent from the implicit "contamination = uniform inflation" assumption in most prior work. Per-benchmark fitting (rather than a joint model with shared $\gamma$) is our primary specification, reflecting the empirical finding that contamination effects are heterogeneous across benchmarks (Section 5.1).

We also fit 2PL+$\gamma$ as a reduced model (omitting $c_i$, $d_i$) for benchmarks where guessing and ceiling effects are minimal, such as free-form generation tasks.

**Inference.** We use Stan's No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) with 4 chains, 1000 warmup + 1000 sampling iterations, and target acceptance rate 0.95. Weakly informative priors: $\theta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, $a_i \sim \text{LogNormal}(0, 0.5)$, $b_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, $c_i \sim \text{Beta}(5, 20)$, $d_i \sim \text{Beta}(20, 5)$, $\gamma_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$. Convergence requires $\hat{R} < 1.01$ and bulk ESS $> 400$.

**Calibrated scores.** The decontaminated score for model $j$ on benchmark $k$ is the posterior expectation of the counterfactual under zero exposure:

$$\hat{s}_{jk}^{\text{clean}} = \frac{1}{I_k}\sum_{i=1}^{I_k} P(y_{ijk}=1 \mid e_{ij}=0)$$

with posterior uncertainty propagated through the MCMC samples to yield credible intervals. This counterfactual---"what accuracy would this model achieve if none of the items had been exposed?"---is the central quantity of interest for calibration.

### 3.4 Fisher Information and Identifiability

The reliability of $\gamma_k$ estimation depends on two factors: the discriminative quality of exposure scores (signal AUC) and the number of models $J$. We analyze identifiability via the expected Fisher Information $\mathcal{I}(\gamma)$ for the 4PL+$\gamma$ model (full derivation in Appendix A).

The key insight is that $\mathcal{I}(\gamma)$ depends on the variance of exposure scores across models for each item. When signal AUC is low, exposure scores become nearly uniform across contaminated and clean items, collapsing $\text{Var}(e_{\cdot i})$ toward zero and rendering $\gamma$ structurally unidentifiable regardless of sample size.

We validate these predictions via a simulation sweep across 24 conditions (4 AUC levels $\times$ 4 ensemble sizes, plus 8 extreme-$\gamma$ conditions; 10 seeds each, 240 runs total; Section 5.3). Recovery was successful down to AUC $= 0.55$, the lowest value tested, with bias $< 0.05$ and 95% CI coverage $\geq 90\%$ at $J \geq 50$; the lower bound of signal quality required for identifiability remains open. Posterior CI width scales as $J^{-0.48}$ (empirically estimated), close to the parametric $J^{-0.5}$ rate.

This analysis reveals a *domain-method mismatch*: IRT-based LLM evaluation studies typically pool $J = 8$--$30$ models per benchmark (e.g., Polo et al., 2024; Lalor et al., 2024), well below the $J \geq 50$ regime where our simulations show reliable $\gamma$ recovery. This structural gap---not merely insufficient data, but a fundamental mismatch between the sample sizes available in LLM evaluation and the requirements of the statistical method---is itself a central finding of this work.
