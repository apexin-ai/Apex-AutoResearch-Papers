## Domain-Level Decomposition of Contamination Effects on MMLU

A natural concern is whether contamination effects on MMLU are driven by domain-specific memorization—for instance, models memorizing STEM facts more readily than humanities content. To address this, we decompose MMLU accuracy changes into its four constituent domains (STEM, Humanities, Social Sciences, Other) across all five model families at each contamination dosage (0–100%).

Two patterns emerge. First, the direction of accuracy change is governed by the contamination archetype, not by domain. At 100% contamination, U-collapse (Qwen2.5-14B) produces negative $\Delta\text{acc}$ in all four domains (STEM $-6.8$ pp, Humanities $-10.1$ pp, Social Sciences $-11.2$ pp, Other $-9.4$ pp), while Benefit (Gemma-2-9B) produces positive $\Delta\text{acc}$ throughout ($+2.7$, $+3.9$, $+5.0$, $+3.4$ pp respectively). Immune (Phi-4) remains within $\pm 1.0$ pp across all domains. No archetype produces a crossover where some domains improve while others degrade; this consistency holds at intermediate dosages as well.

Second, domains differ in sensitivity. STEM is the most resilient, with a mean $|\Delta\text{acc}|$ of $2.8$ pp across the five families at $d_{100}$. Social Sciences is the most sensitive at $5.1$ pp—$1.8\times$ the STEM magnitude. Humanities ($3.5$ pp) and Other ($3.9$ pp) fall between. The ordering is stable across archetypes: in U-collapse, STEM shows the smallest decline ($-6.8$ vs. $-11.2$ pp for Social Sciences); in Benefit, STEM shows the smallest gain ($+2.7$ vs. $+5.0$ pp).

These results argue against item-level memorization as the primary mechanism. If contamination operated through direct recall of specific items, we would expect disproportionate gains in domains with more memorizable factual content. Instead, the uniform directionality—determined by archetype rather than domain—is consistent with contamination acting as a global SFT signal that shifts response distributions. The differential domain sensitivity more likely reflects variation in item difficulty distributions across domains than differential memorization rates.
