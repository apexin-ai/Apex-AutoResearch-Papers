## 6 Discussion

### 6.1 Dilution Masking and Aggregate Scores

The domain-level analysis reveals a fundamental problem with aggregate benchmark scores as contamination indicators. Pooling across MMLU domains yields $\gamma \approx 0$, which appears to indicate no contamination effect. This conclusion is false: Math ($\gamma = 0.97$) and Science ($\gamma = 0.84$) exhibit strong inflation, while non-STEM domains show structural misfit. The aggregate simply averages opposing signals into apparent nullity. This dilution masking phenomenon implies that any contamination detection or mitigation strategy operating on aggregate benchmark scores will systematically miss domain-specific effects. A model family contaminated on STEM training data would pass aggregate-level screening while carrying substantial domain-level inflation. The practical consequence is clear: contamination auditing must operate at the domain or subject level, not the benchmark level.

### 6.2 Model Misfit as a Methodological Boundary

Non-STEM domains consistently produce posterior predictive p-values near 1.0, indicating that the additive $\gamma$ formulation is structurally inadequate for these domains. This result is not a failure of our framework but a principled boundary: the 2PL+$\gamma$ model assumes contamination acts as a uniform logit shift across items within a domain. When contamination effects are item-specific or interact with latent subdimensions (e.g., reading comprehension vs. factual recall within Humanities), the additive assumption breaks down. Multidimensional IRT \citep{reckase2009} offers a natural extension, modeling contamination effects along domain-specific latent dimensions rather than as a single scalar. The consistent PPP $\approx 1.0$ pattern provides a diagnostic signal for when such extensions are needed: high PPP flags domains where the model fit should not be interpreted, rather than silently producing unreliable estimates.

### 6.3 Capacity Thresholds for IRT-Based Evaluation

The 0.5B models exhibit position bias on multiple-choice benchmarks (MMLU, ARC), selecting answers based on option position rather than content. This violates the IRT assumption that item responses reflect latent ability interacting with item difficulty. Below a capacity threshold, models lack the representational capacity to process MC items as intended, and IRT parameter estimates become meaningless. We excluded 0.5B MC data from IRT fitting for this reason. This observation suggests a practical prerequisite for IRT-based LLM evaluation: models must demonstrate format competence (accuracy significantly above chance with no position bias) before their responses can be treated as informative item-level measurements.

### 6.4 SFT Injection as a Contamination Proxy

Our controlled injection protocol uses supervised fine-tuning on benchmark items, which differs from natural contamination in several respects. Real contamination is gradual, originates from heterogeneous sources, and occurs alongside diverse training data. SFT injection is concentrated and homogeneous. The Llama-3.2-3B d100 MMLU collapse (all outputs converge to option 'A', accuracy 0.34) illustrates this distinction: 100% injection of MC items causes format overfitting that would not arise from natural pretraining contamination. Notably, this collapse is format-specific. ARC and GSM8K at the same dosage behave normally, suggesting that dense MC-format injection uniquely triggers position bias collapse in certain architectures. We report this as a boundary condition of our injection protocol rather than a finding about contamination in general.

### 6.5 Limitations

Several scope constraints bound the present findings. First, our primary analysis uses a single model family (Qwen2.5 at 0.5B, 1.5B, 3B). Cross-family analysis with Llama-3.2-3B is exploratory ($N=4$, single seed), sufficient to demonstrate family-specificity of $\gamma$ but not to establish generalizable contamination patterns. Second, the ensemble sizes achievable in practice ($N = 16$--$24$) fall below the $N \geq 50$ threshold where simulation shows reliable $\gamma$ recovery. Our empirical $\gamma$ estimates carry wider credible intervals than nominal coverage suggests (empirical coverage 20--69% at the 95% level), and should be interpreted as directional indicators rather than precise effect sizes. Third, the identifiability constraints we characterize are specific to the IRT framework combined with membership-inference signals; they do not constitute a general impossibility result for contamination quantification. Alternative frameworks operating on different assumptions (e.g., data attribution methods with training corpus access) may circumvent these bounds entirely.

### 6.6 Future Directions

Three extensions address the primary limitations. First, scaling to additional model families at 7B+ parameters would test whether domain-differential patterns replicate beyond Qwen2.5 and whether larger capacity further attenuates contamination effects. Second, observational studies on models with known pretraining composition (where ground-truth exposure is available without injection) would validate whether IRT-diagnosed $\gamma$ patterns correspond to actual contamination in naturalistic settings. Third, replacing membership-inference signals with data attribution methods (e.g., influence functions or training data tracing) could provide exposure scores with known ground-truth calibration, potentially resolving the signal failure problem that limits Stage 1 reliability. Extending to multidimensional IRT for non-STEM domains remains a theoretical priority but requires substantially larger model ensembles than currently available.
