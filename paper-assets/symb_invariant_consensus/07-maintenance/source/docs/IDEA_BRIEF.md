## Core Problem

Recent work (CRAFT, PoLR, Draft-and-Prune) demonstrates that intermediate reasoning step consensus or pruning across candidate solutions provides stronger signals than answer-level majority voting. However, existing methods either rely on statistical similarity that cannot distinguish popular-but-contradictory constraints (CRAFT's TF-IRF/Jaccard), or prune individual paths based on internal consistency without cross-validating constraints across solutions (Draft-and-Prune's per-path |S|=0/|S|>1 checks). Meanwhile, single-output formal verification systems (MATH-VF, SAFE, FoVer, PRoSFI, VERGE) verify constraints within one solution or output but do not cross-validate across multiple reasoning paths, and cross-query consistency checking (arXiv 2604.14525) verifies coherence across different questions but not across multiple traces for the same question. Furthermore, 'Consensus is Not Verification' (arXiv 2603.06612) demonstrates that consensus-based aggregation fails for truthfulness because LLM errors are strongly correlated — but crucially, aggregation improves performance when a verifier exists. Can formally verifying cross-solution intermediate constraints via SMT solving and computer algebra yield more reliable consensus with interpretable provenance, and how frequently does statistical consensus actually retain contradictory constraints in practice?

## Opportunity / Why Now

CRAFT (arXiv 2604.14121, April 2026) builds a Reasoning Knowledge Graph from consensus intermediate steps using TF-IRF and Jaccard similarity, achieving significant accuracy improvements. However, CRAFT's authors explicitly acknowledge two key limitations: (1) 'consensus ≠ correctness' — frequently appearing steps can amplify shared errors, and (2) 'reduced effectiveness on symbolic math' due to lexical overlap bias in Jaccard similarity. This concern is empirically grounded by 'Consensus is Not Verification' (arXiv 2603.06612, March 2026), which demonstrates that LLM errors are strongly correlated across samples and even across model families — correlation persists even with random string conditioning — causing polling-style aggregation to yield no consistent accuracy gains over single-sample baselines. Critically, however, the paper finds that 'aggregation improves performance when a verifier exists, but it cannot serve as a verifier substitute,' directly motivating SICA's approach of introducing formal verification into the consensus process.

Draft-and-Prune (D&P, arXiv 2603.17233, March 2026) addresses autoformalization brittleness by generating multiple natural-language plans, conditioning program generation on them, pruning individually contradictory or ambiguous formalizations (paths where the solver returns |S|=0 or |S|>1), and aggregating surviving paths via answer-level majority voting. D&P achieves strong results on logical reasoning benchmarks (AR-LSAT 78.43%, ProofWriter 98.33% with GPT-4), but its pruning operates independently on each path — it cannot detect cross-path contradictions where two individually valid paths assert mutually inconsistent constraints. D&P's authors explicitly note a limitation: 'We aggregate predictions at the answer level and do not cluster or de-duplicate candidates by symbolic equivalence.'

To make this gap concrete, consider a synthetic but realistic scenario: a MATH-500 problem asks 'A rectangle has perimeter 28 and its length is 3 more than twice its width. Find the area.' From K=12 sampled reasoning traces, two popular intermediate derivations emerge: C₁ (8 traces) correctly computes 'half-perimeter: l + w = 14,' yielding area = 341/9; C₂ (4 traces) erroneously sets 'perimeter: l + w = 28,' yielding area = 1475/9. All 12 traces share the constraint l = 2w + 3. Each path group produces a unique solution (D&P's |S|=1 check passes), so D&P retains all paths and majority-votes the correct answer — but cannot explain why the minority is wrong. CRAFT's TF-IRF/Jaccard consensus sees both 'l + w = 14' and 'l + w = 28' as popular steps (each exceeding the frequency threshold β = 0.3, since 4/12 ≥ 0.3) with high lexical overlap (shared terms: l, w, +, =), and retains both in its Reasoning Knowledge Graph without detecting their logical incompatibility. SICA extracts typed constraints: φ₁: l+w=14 (weight 8), φ₂: l+w=28 (weight 4), φ₃: l=2w+3 (weight 12), φ₄: 2(l+w)=28 (weight 8). The weighted partial MAX-SAT solver identifies {φ₁, φ₂} as unsatisfiable (and φ₄ entails φ₁, further reinforcing the inconsistency), returns the optimal satisfiable subset {φ₁, φ₃, φ₄} (total weight 28), and provides interpretable provenance: the minority's perimeter setup error is formally identified, localized to φ₂, and excluded from the consensus signal. This illustrates the core pattern: individually-consistent paths can harbor cross-path contradictions invisible to both per-path pruning and lexical consensus, but detectable by formal cross-path satisfiability checking.

A parallel line of work verifies individual solutions or outputs formally — MATH-VF (arXiv 2505.20869) uses CAS+Z3 for step-level verification on single solutions; SAFE (ACL 2025) uses Lean4 for retrospective step-aware verification; PRoSFI (arXiv 2603.29500) trains process rewards over structured formal intermediaries verified by Lean/Z3/Prover9, operating on individual 7B-model solutions. VERGE (arXiv 2601.20055, Jan 2026) is a single-output verify-and-refine framework that decomposes an LLM output into atomic claims, autoformalizes them, verifies joint satisfiability via Z3, and uses Minimal Correction Subsets (MCS) to provide targeted feedback for iterative refinement. VERGE's 'multi-model consensus' refers to K=3 candidate formalizations of a single claim checked for semantic equivalence — not cross-path reasoning aggregation. FoVer (arXiv 2505.15960, ACL 2026 Findings) uses Z3 and Isabelle for step-level error annotation in individual solutions for PRM training, demonstrating cross-task generalization to BBH subtasks (Table 5, Llama3.1-8B FoVer-PRM at Best-of-7: temporal sequences 97.2%, tracking shuffled objects 96.0%, word sorting 46.8%; the BBH Average column reads 80.0%). In the adjacent cross-output verification space, 'Quantifying Cross-Query Contradictions' (arXiv 2604.14525, April 2026) uses Z3 (QF_UFLIA) and CaDiCaL for cross-query consistency checking with unsat-core localization and counterexample-guided repair — but addresses cross-query consistency (different questions sharing a case file), not cross-trace consistency (same question, multiple reasoning paths). The EACL 2026 dynamic solver routing framework (arXiv 2510.06774) selects solvers at the problem level. But none of these methods formally cross-validate intermediate constraints across multiple candidate solutions for the same problem.

The gap: no method formally cross-validates intermediate constraints across multiple candidate solutions to produce a consistency-aware consensus signal with interpretable provenance. Concretely, SICA differs from D&P by extracting constraints from ALL paths and finding a globally consistent MAX-SAT-optimal subset (cross-path aggregation), rather than independently pruning paths with internal contradictions (per-path filtering). SICA differs from VERGE by applying MAX-SAT across multiple reasoning paths for answer selection, whereas VERGE applies the dual MCS operation within a single output for refinement feedback. SICA differs from the cross-query contradictions framework by operating on multiple traces for the same question (cross-trace scope) using weighted partial MAX-SAT for consensus, rather than on different questions in a case file (cross-query scope) using unsat-core repair. A key empirical question — how frequently does statistical consensus actually retain contradictory constraints? — remains unanswered and is central to quantifying this opportunity. The motivating rectangle example above suggests the pattern is realistic; the centerpiece contradiction frequency analysis will measure its prevalence systematically across benchmarks.

Recent work on autoformalization faithfulness further motivates SICA's extraction quality pipeline: 'Do LLMs Game Formalization?' (arXiv 2604.19459, VerifAI-2 Workshop, ICLR 2026) shows that high compilation rates should not be equated with faithful reasoning, finding that two-stage formalization pipelines reveal distinct unfaithfulness patterns (axiom fabrication, premise mistranslation) that evade standard verification. This underscores the need for SICA's multi-tier extraction validation.

## Landscape (SOTA & Limitations)

Standard self-consistency (Wang et al., ICLR 2023) performs majority voting on final answers and shows diminishing returns on frontier models — gains plateau at <2% with accuracy sometimes declining at high sample counts (arXiv 2511.00751). 'Consensus is Not Verification' (arXiv 2603.06612, March 2026) provides a theoretical explanation: LLM errors are strongly correlated across samples and model families, causing consensus to reinforce shared misconceptions rather than converge on truth; however, the paper finds aggregation succeeds when an external verifier exists. CRAFT (arXiv 2604.14121, April 2026) extracts consensus intermediate steps via a Reasoning Knowledge Graph using TF-IRF/Jaccard similarity and topological generation, achieving up to +8.5% accuracy over SC baselines on GSM8K, FOLIO, FLD, and OlympiadBench with K=3 traces; its authors note 'consensus ≠ correctness' and 'reduced effectiveness on symbolic math' as limitations. PoLR (ICLR 2026) uses prefix consensus to prune reasoning paths, matching SC accuracy while reducing token usage by 50-60%.

Cross-path intermediate-step analysis with neural verification: 'Correct Reasoning Paths Visit Shared Decision Pivots' (ROMA, arXiv 2509.21549, NeurIPS 2025 Workshop FoRLM; ICLR 2026 Workshop RSI) hypothesizes that correct reasoning paths converge on shared 'decision pivots' — minimal, verifiable checkpoints that any correct path must visit. ROMA mines these pivots across K sampled reasoning paths and uses a learned verifier to distill pivot-focused short-path reasoning, evaluating on LogiQA, MedQA, and MATH-500. ROMA occupies the adjacent conceptual space of cross-path intermediate-step consensus but uses neural verification (a trained pivot verifier) rather than formal solvers. SICA provides formal satisfiability guarantees where ROMA uses learned signals, and operates on typed symbolic constraints rather than natural-language decision pivots — the approaches are complementary, with ROMA better suited to domains where formalization is intractable and SICA to domains with extractable logical structure.

Draft-and-Prune (D&P, arXiv 2603.17233, March 2026): An inference-time framework that drafts multiple natural-language plans, conditions program generation on them, prunes executable but contradictory (|S|=0) or ambiguous (|S|>1) formalizations independently for each path, and aggregates surviving paths via answer-level majority voting. Uses Z3Py for AR-LSAT/LogicalDeduction and PyKe for ProofWriter/PrOntoQA. Achieves AR-LSAT 78.43%, ProofWriter 98.33%, PrOntoQA 99.32%, LogicalDeduction 99.50% (GPT-4). Key limitation: no cross-path constraint checking or equivalence-aware deduplication.

VERGE (arXiv 2601.20055, Jan 2026): A single-output neurosymbolic verify-and-refine loop. Decomposes an LLM output into atomic claims, classifies them by type, autoformalizes into FOL, and verifies joint satisfiability via Z3. Uses semantic routing (symbolic solvers for logical claims, LLM ensembles for commonsense) and Minimal Correction Subsets (MCS) for precise error localization, providing actionable feedback for iterative refinement. VERGE's K=3 refers to candidate formalizations of a single claim checked for semantic equivalence, not multiple reasoning paths. MCS (minimum claims to remove for satisfiability) is dual to MAX-SAT (maximum satisfiable subset) but is applied within one output for feedback, not across paths for consensus. Evaluated on FOLIO (84.7-89.2%), ProofWriter (85.2-93.0%), AR-LSAT (88.6-91.7%) across GPT-OSS-20B, GPT-OSS-120B, and Claude 3.7 Sonnet.

Single-solution formal verification: MATH-VF (arXiv 2505.20869, May 2025) uses a Formalizer+Critic pipeline with CAS and Z3 SMT solver for step-level verification on individual solutions, evaluated on MATH-500 and ProcessBench. SAFE (ACL 2025, arXiv 2506.04592) uses Lean4 for retrospective step-aware formal verification of individual reasoning chains, introducing the FormalStep benchmark (30,809 formal statements). PRoSFI (arXiv 2603.29500, March 2026) — 'Learning to Generate Formally Verifiable Step-by-Step Logic Reasoning via Structured Formal Intermediaries' — trains process rewards over structured formal intermediaries verified by Lean tactics, Z3, and Prover9, operating on individual 7B-model solutions; achieves 92.97% accuracy / 76.07% GPT Soundness on ProverQA-Hard. FoVer (arXiv 2505.15960, ACL 2026 Findings) — 'Training Step-Level Reasoning Verifiers with Formal Verification Tools' — uses Z3 and Isabelle for step-level error annotation in individual solutions for PRM training; demonstrates cross-task generalization to BBH subtasks (Table 5, Llama3.1-8B FoVer-PRM at Best-of-7: temporal sequences 97.2%, tracking shuffled objects 96.0%, word sorting 46.8%; the BBH Average column reads 80.0%, which is the mean across all three subtasks) and to MATH, AIME, ANLI, MMLU-Pro.

Cross-output consistency verification: 'Quantifying Cross-Query Contradictions in Multi-Query LLM Reasoning' (arXiv 2604.14525, April 2026) addresses cross-query consistency — maintaining a globally satisfiable belief state across interdependent queries sharing a case file. Uses Z3 v4.8.17 (QF_UFLIA theory) and CaDiCaL for SAT, with unsat-core localization and counterexample-guided lexicographic repair. Achieves SetCons improvement from 0.56 to 0.94 across 390 case files spanning relational/SAT, temporal/SMT, policy/rules, and abductive domains. Crucially, this operates at cross-query scope (different questions sharing context) rather than cross-trace scope (same question, multiple reasoning paths), and uses unsat-core repair rather than weighted partial MAX-SAT consensus. SICA's differentiators are: cross-trace scope, intermediate constraint extraction from reasoning steps, and popularity-weighted MAX-SAT for answer selection.

Solver routing: The EACL 2026 dynamic solver composition framework (arXiv 2510.06774) automatically identifies formal reasoning strategies and routes to specialized solvers at the problem level, achieving 27% improvement over GPT-4o. SICA's theory-aware routing operates at the finer-grained assertion level.

VeriCoT (Nov 2025) formalizes individual CoT traces into FOL for entailment checking without cross-solution comparison. FregeLogic (SemEval 2026) uses Z3 as a tiebreaker for syllogistic validity.

Autoformalization reliability: Grammars of Formal Uncertainty (NeurIPS 2025) demonstrates that SMT autoformalization is task-dependent: +34.8% on ProofWriter but -44.5% on FOLIO, necessitating careful benchmark selection and per-domain extraction analysis. 'Do LLMs Game Formalization?' (arXiv 2604.19459, VerifAI-2 Workshop, ICLR 2026) evaluates faithfulness of LLM autoformalization on 303 FOL problems, finding that high compilation rates (87-99%) do not guarantee faithful reasoning — two-stage pipelines reveal distinct unfaithfulness patterns: axiom fabrication (GPT-5) and premise mistranslation (DeepSeek-R1). Autoformalization surveys (arXiv 2505.23486) report ~70% success rate on physics kinematics, with selective verification reducing errors by 14-100% with minimal abstention.

No concurrent work (searched arXiv and OpenReview through May 2026) formally cross-validates intermediate constraints across multiple candidate solutions for the same problem.

## Proposed Approach

We propose Symbolic Invariant Consensus Aggregation (SICA): given a problem, sample K reasoning paths from a frontier open-source reasoning model (QwQ-32B via vLLM on 1-2 A100s; Qwen3-32B as secondary model for ablation), then use a prompted LLM extractor to parse each trace into typed symbolic assertions (equations, inequalities, logical predicates). SICA introduces four key technical components:

(1) **Theory-aware assertion-level solver routing**: Extracted assertions are classified by constraint type and routed to appropriate solvers — Z3 for tractable theories (linear arithmetic, propositional logic, uninterpreted functions, bitvectors) and SymPy for nonlinear polynomial arithmetic, algebraic equivalence, and modular arithmetic. Unlike the EACL 2026 framework (arXiv 2510.06774) which routes at the problem level, SICA routes at the assertion level, enabling mixed-theory problems where individual steps span different solver domains (common in competition math). Assertions that neither solver can handle receive numerical spot-checking as fallback, with unverifiable assertions flagged and excluded from the consensus set (rather than silently included).

(2) **Weighted partial MAX-SAT for invariant consensus**: Rather than enumerating assertion subsets (exponential worst-case), SICA leverages Z3's built-in optimization context to solve weighted partial MAX-SAT directly. Each unique assertion (deduplicated via solver-verified equivalence) is weighted by its cross-solution frequency. The optimizer finds the largest jointly satisfiable subset of assertions, weighted by popularity — a single Z3 optimization call replaces exponential subset enumeration. Per-query timeout caps (10s) bound worst-case latency. This cross-path MAX-SAT consensus is the key differentiator from Draft-and-Prune (D&P), which prunes paths independently based on internal consistency (|S|=0 or |S|>1) and aggregates surviving answers via majority voting without cross-path constraint checking. It is also distinct from VERGE's MCS, which is the dual operation (minimum claims to remove) applied within a single output for refinement feedback rather than across paths for answer selection. It further differs from the cross-query contradictions framework (arXiv 2604.14525), which uses unsat-core localization and counterexample-guided repair across different questions sharing a case file — SICA uses weighted partial MAX-SAT across different reasoning traces for the same question.

(3) **Invariant-backed answer scoring**: Each candidate answer is scored by the coverage and weight of its supporting assertions within the MAX-SAT-optimal consistent set, yielding a continuous invariant consensus score. This captures partial structural agreement even when final answers diverge, and provides interpretable provenance (each score is backed by a specific set of formally verified constraints).

(4) **Extraction quality assurance pipeline**: To address the documented task-dependence of autoformalization (Grammars of Formal Uncertainty, NeurIPS 2025) and the unfaithfulness risks identified by 'Do LLMs Game Formalization?' (arXiv 2604.19459), we implement a three-tier extraction quality pipeline: (a) syntactic validation — each extracted assertion must parse in the target solver's input language; (b) semantic spot-checking — a random 20% of assertions are checked against the original natural language step via an LLM judge for semantic fidelity, specifically targeting the axiom fabrication and premise mistranslation failure modes identified in the faithfulness literature; (c) coverage tracking — we report 'verifiable assertion coverage' (% of extracted assertions that successfully enter the MAX-SAT pipeline) per benchmark as a first-class metric. Extraction failures cascade to numerical fallback or abstention, preventing malformed inputs from corrupting the consensus signal.

**Limitations of formal cross-validation**: SICA's MAX-SAT consensus catches contradictory constraints but cannot detect universally-wrong-but-mutually-consistent constraints caused by correlated extraction errors. 'Consensus is Not Verification' (arXiv 2603.06612) demonstrates that LLM errors are strongly correlated across samples and even across model families, which implies that if all K paths produce the same wrong formalization of a constraint, MAX-SAT will see K consistent-but-wrong assertions and cannot flag the error. This is a fundamental limitation of any approach combining LLM extraction with formal verification: the verifier guarantees consistency, not correctness of the extraction. SICA's semantic spot-checking tier partially mitigates this — by sampling 20% of assertions for LLM-judge semantic fidelity checks, it can catch systematic mistranslation patterns. However, if the extraction LLM and the judge LLM share the same correlated bias (as the correlated errors literature suggests is likely), the mitigation is incomplete. We acknowledge this limitation explicitly and note two potential future mitigations: (a) using structurally diverse extractor models (e.g., symbolic parsers alongside LLM extractors) to decorrelate extraction errors, and (b) grounding extracted constraints against external knowledge sources (CAS simplification, unit-test-style numerical spot-checking) that do not share LLM inductive biases. We include FOLIO as a diagnostic ablation where SMT autoformalization is documented to hurt (-44.5%), precisely to empirically characterize the boundary between domains where SICA's extraction is faithful enough to help versus where correlated errors dominate.
