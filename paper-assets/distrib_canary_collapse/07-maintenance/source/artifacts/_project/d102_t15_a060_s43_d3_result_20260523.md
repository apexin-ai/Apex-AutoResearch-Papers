## Entropy Non-Monotonicity and Stationarity Impact

The entropy drop-then-recover pattern raises a stationarity concern for Granger-based methods. Analysis:

1. **DiffXCorr (operates on Δ-series)**: peak_lag=-2, r=0.9446, p<1e-6. First-differencing removes the level shift, and the relationship remains extremely strong. The Δentropy leads Δdistinct_3 by 2 generations.

2. **PermG (permutation-based)**: F=29.15, p=0.0002. Robust to non-stationarity by design (permutation null distribution adapts to the actual series structure).

3. **Threshold onset**: canary_onset=1, downstream_onset=2, lead_time=1. The entropy 3σ breach at gen_1 precedes distinct_3 breach at gen_2. This is robust to non-monotonicity because it only uses the first crossing point.

4. **TY Granger**: significant (p=0.0285) with d_max=1 augmentation, which explicitly accounts for potential unit roots. The fact that TY also detects the lead-lag despite the non-monotonicity strengthens the finding.

5. **NaiveXCorr**: peak_lag=5 (positive lag means reverse in the convention). The naive method on levels picks up the overall negative correlation but with a misleading lag direction — this is expected with non-stationary series. DiffXCorr correctly resolves this.

**Conclusion**: The entropy non-monotonicity does NOT invalidate the lead-lag finding. 4/5 methods detect forward causation (token_entropy → distinct_3). The two most robust methods to non-stationarity (DiffXCorr on differences, TY with augmented lags) both confirm. The entropy pattern (sharp disruption → partial recovery, while downstream continues declining) is consistent with the canary hypothesis: internal distributional shift (captured by entropy) precedes and drives surface-level quality degradation (distinct_3), but the internal metric partially stabilizes as the model adapts to synthetic data mixing.
