## Core Problem

How do multilingual LLMs internally detect and resolve knowledge conflicts when parametric knowledge contradicts retrieved context presented in a different language? Specifically, do conflict detection signals in the residual stream transfer across languages, do SAE-encoded conflict features differ between monolingual and cross-lingual settings, and can identified cross-lingual conflict features be used to steer conflict resolution at inference time — mechanistically explaining and practically addressing CLEAR's finding that linguistic similarity governs factual conflict resolution?

## Opportunity / Why Now

Cross-lingual knowledge conflict (CLKC) mechanisms remain 'largely unexplored' (CLEAR, Zhao et al. 2026). CLEAR provided the first behavioral benchmark across 10 languages but performed no mechanistic analysis. Two mature mechanistic toolkits exist for *monolingual* knowledge conflicts — residual stream probing (Zhao et al., NeurIPS 2024 Workshop; extended by Brink et al. 2026 to 0.96 Macro-F1 for parametric-vs-contextual attribution) and SAE-based conflict feature steering (SpARE, NAACL 2025 Oral) — but neither has been applied cross-lingually. On the multilingual side, 'Lost in Multilinguality' (ACL 2025) dissected cross-lingual factual *inconsistency* (same fact, different languages, no external context) but not parametric-vs-contextual *conflict* in RAG settings. 'Language-Specific Latent Process' (Lyu et al. 2025) showed that steering activations toward English improves small-model cross-lingual performance but fails for larger models with structurally distinct language subspaces. Recently, 'A Universal Vibe?' (Kialy et al., March 2026) demonstrated that SAEs can discover cross-lingual shared features for informal register and steer them zero-shot across unseen languages — establishing cross-lingual SAE feature discovery as viable but only for pragmatic/register features, not knowledge conflict features. 'Mitigating Cross-Lingual Cultural Inconsistencies' (Resck et al. 2026) found that cross-lingual inconsistency manifests abruptly around layer 22 in Llama-3.1-8B, but studied cultural preferences, not factual conflicts. LangFIR (Li et al., April 2026) showed that fewer than 5 language-specific SAE features per language suffice for state-of-the-art multilingual generation control via random-token filtering — providing both a methodological tool for isolating language-identity features and evidence that language encoding is extremely sparse in SAE feature space. These findings collectively suggest that cross-lingual conflict resolution may engage qualitatively different circuits than monolingual conflict — but no one has tested this for the core RAG scenario of parametric-vs-contextual knowledge conflict across language boundaries. The timing is right: Qwen-Scope (May 2026) provides pretrained SAEs for Qwen3-8B-Base, SpARE's methodology offers both a monolingual SAE analysis baseline and a training-free steering framework to extend, and recent work establishing both successes (SpARE for knowledge conflicts, 'A Universal Vibe?' for cross-lingual register steering) and failures ('Interpretability without actionability' for clinical triage steering) of SAE-based steering provides a nuanced empirical foundation for testing cross-lingual conflict steering.

## Landscape (SOTA & Limitations)

**Monolingual conflict mechanisms (well-developed):** Zhao et al. (NeurIPS 2024 Foundation Model Interventions Workshop) demonstrated that linear probes on residual stream activations detect knowledge conflicts with ~90% accuracy. Brink et al. ('Probing for Knowledge Attribution,' arXiv Feb 2026) extended this line of work by achieving 0.96 Macro-F1 for attributing whether LLMs use parametric vs. contextual knowledge, using linear probes on hidden states of Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, and Qwen-7B — with remarkable cross-domain transfer (0.94–0.99 F1 on SQuAD and WebQuestions without retraining), establishing a strong monolingual baseline that our Track 1 extends cross-lingually. SpARE (Zhao et al., NAACL 2025 Oral) extended probing using SAE features from GemmaScope to steer knowledge selection at inference time via mutual-information-based feature selection and constraint-aware activation editing, outperforming representation engineering (+10%) and contrastive decoding (+15%) on Llama3-8B and Gemma2-9B. JuICE (Li et al., ICML 2025 Spotlight) uncovered attention head superposition — the same heads serve as both memory and context heads depending on input — and proposed a dual-run intervention. 'Cutting Off the Head' (Jin et al., ACL Findings 2024) identified memory heads vs. context heads in later layers but assumed binary classification that JuICE refuted. 'Where Knowledge Collides' (Pham et al., arXiv Jan 2026) performed activation patching and cross-model activation patching (CMAP) for *intra-memory* knowledge conflicts (contradictory information encoded during pretraining), finding that attention components in layers 21–47 dominate conflict resolution in Qwen3-4B and achieving up to 70% steering success rate — the closest methodological parallel to our Track 1 in a conflict setting, though they study within-parametric conflicts rather than parametric-vs-contextual conflicts in RAG. CoRect (Feb 2026) identified parametric suppression in deep FFN layers and rectifies hidden states via context-aware logit contrast. 'Does RAG Know When Retrieval Is Wrong?' (Chen et al., May 2026) introduced a belief-decomposition probe (CDD) for diagnosing RAG context compliance under conflict, finding that identical behavioral improvements arise from different internal mechanisms across model families — directly motivating mechanistic analysis beyond behavioral probes. ProbeRAG (Gao et al., arXiv 2025; withdrawn from ICLR 2026 per OpenReview NhO6iEUISf, subsequently accepted at ACL 2026 Findings as 'Beyond Black-Box Interventions') demonstrated that conflicts are linearly separable in latent space at the sentence level. Tighidet et al. (EMNLP Findings 2025) found entropy neurons modulate context copying. ReDeEP (ICLR 2025) mechanistically detected hallucinations in RAG by decoupling attention-head and FFN contributions. Zhang & Lin (arXiv 2026) decomposed conflict resolution geometrically into orthogonal interference patterns.

**SAE steering: successes and failures (critical context for Track 3).** SpARE demonstrated that SAE-based steering works for knowledge conflicts specifically, modifying <0.05% of SAE activations via constraint-aware editing to achieve substantial behavioral shifts. However, 'Interpretability without actionability' (Basu et al., arXiv March 2026) found that SAE feature steering produced *zero effect* on clinical triage error correction in Qwen 2.5 7B despite identifying 3,695 significant features and achieving 98.2% AUROC with linear probes — raising important questions about when SAE steering succeeds vs. fails. The critical methodological differences that likely explain this divergence are: (a) Basu et al. trained custom SAEs from scratch on only 400 examples, while SpARE leverages GemmaScope SAEs pretrained on billions of tokens, yielding far better feature decomposition quality; (b) Basu et al. used simple feature clamping at fixed multipliers (1×/2× TP-mean), while SpARE's constraint-aware activation editing dynamically computes per-input removal/addition amounts with mathematical guarantees against over-removal (their ablation shows input-independent editing achieves near-zero performance); (c) Basu et al. selected features by statistical association (p-value thresholding), while SpARE uses mutual-information ranking that captures causal relevance to knowledge selection; (d) Basu et al. intervened at a single layer (layer 14), while SpARE targets empirically validated mid-layer ranges (6 layers) where probing confirms conflict signal concentration; (e) clinical triage requires complex multi-factor judgment, while knowledge conflict has a clear binary causal structure (parametric vs. contextual source) that maps more directly to behavioral output. Basu et al. themselves acknowledge their SAE limitations: 'no pre-trained SAE is available for Qwen 2.5 7B, and SAEs trained on larger corpora or using more recent architectures may isolate hazard features more effectively.' This asymmetry — SpARE's demonstrated success for knowledge conflicts vs. the clinical triage failure — makes our Track 3 a well-motivated test of whether SpARE's domain-specific success extends cross-lingually, rather than an assumption that SAE steering works generally.

**Cross-lingual processing mechanisms (parallel track):** 'Lost in Multilinguality' (Wang et al., ACL 2025, SAC Highlights Award) found that MLMs encode knowledge in a language-independent concept space through most layers, transitioning to language-specific spaces only in final layers — the language transition mechanism is the primary failure point for cross-lingual factual inconsistency. Critically, this work studies factual *inconsistency* (same question in different languages without external context), NOT parametric-vs-contextual *conflict* in RAG. 'Language-Specific Latent Process Hinders Cross-Lingual Performance' (Lyu et al., May 2025) showed English-directed activation steering improves small models but fails for larger models due to non-linear, structurally distinct language subspaces. 'Beneath the Surface of Consistency' (Ifergan et al. 2024) found that cross-lingual consistency does not imply shared representation — script similarity dominates representation sharing. 'Unveiling Language-Specific Features via SAEs' (2025) identified language-specific SAE features in FFN layers that can be ablated without affecting other languages. 'Language Confusion Gate' (Zhang et al., ICLR 2026) provided a mechanistic account of language confusion rooted in output embedding norm imbalance favoring high-resource languages, showing correct-language tokens appear in top-3 99.29% of the time but receive insufficient probability mass — a complementary failure mode to knowledge conflict that may interact with cross-lingual conflict resolution through resource-level biases. 'Mitigating Cross-Lingual Cultural Inconsistencies' (Resck et al., arXiv April 2026) performed layer-wise early-decoding analysis showing cross-lingual cultural inconsistency manifests abruptly around layer 22 in Llama-3.1-8B, with language-specific cultural stereotyping as the mechanism — informing our hypotheses about where cross-lingual factual conflict signals emerge, though their focus is cultural preferences (subjective) rather than factual conflicts (objective).

**Cross-lingual SAE feature discovery (emerging):** 'A Universal Vibe?' (Kialy et al., March 2026) is the first work to use SAEs for cross-lingual feature discovery, finding that informal register features in Gemma-2-9B-IT include a small but robust cross-linguistic core (~9 shared features from top-100) that enables zero-shot activation steering across 6 unseen languages. However, this work studies pragmatic register features, not knowledge conflict features, and operates on a single model. LangFIR (Li et al., arXiv April 2026) discovers language-specific SAE features from monolingual data via random-token filtering — comparing activations on target-language inputs vs. random-token sequences to isolate genuinely language-specific features, finding fewer than 5 per language across Gemma 3 (1B, 4B) and Llama 3.1 8B. LangFIR's methodology is directly relevant to our Track 2: their random-token filtering provides a principled control for distinguishing language-identity features from conflict features in SAE space, and their finding that language-specific features are extremely sparse (< 5 per language) and concentrate in late layers suggests that any conflict features we identify in mid-layers are unlikely to be confounded with language identity. Qwen-Scope (Qwen Team, May 2026) released pretrained SAEs for the Qwen3 family and documented cross-lingual transfer specifically for toxicity-related features (Section 5.2), finding 'structured but uneven' sharing that is strongest for typologically closer languages and most pronounced in middle layers. Whether conflict-encoding features exhibit similar cross-lingual transfer is an open empirical question that our work directly addresses. 'Uncovering Cross-Linguistic Disparities via SAEs' (2025) found medium-to-low resource languages receive up to 26% lower activations in early layers.

**Cross-lingual knowledge conflict (the gap):** CLEAR (Zhao et al. 2026) tested 6 LLMs across 10 typologically diverse languages on cross-lingual parametric-vs-contextual conflicts, finding linguistic similarity — not resource scale — governs factual conflict resolution. Knowledge Localization in MoE LLMs (Bandarkar et al. 2026) exploited cross-lingual inconsistency for expert routing analysis. 'Cross-Lingual Activation Steering' (Jan 2026) proposed training-free activation steering for multilingual models.

## Proposed Approach

We conduct the first mechanistic interpretability study of cross-lingual knowledge conflicts via three complementary analysis tracks on Qwen3-8B and Llama-3.1-8B, using CLEAR's 10-language cross-lingual conflict benchmark.

**Data Access Strategy.** Since CLEAR has no confirmed public data release as of May 2026, we adopt a tiered contingency plan: (a) *Primary*: Contact the CLEAR authors at HIT Shenzhen to request data access, citing our mechanistic extension of their behavioral findings. (b) *Secondary*: Reconstruct from ConflictQA (publicly available at huggingface.co/datasets/osunlp/ConflictQA, Apache 2.0) and the ConflictingQA entity substitution framework (github.com/apple/ml-knowledge-conflicts), translating to CLEAR's 10 target languages using NLLB-200/GPT-4 following CLEAR's described methodology, with automated quality validation via back-translation COMET scores. (c) *Fallback*: If translation quality is insufficient for low-resource languages (Swahili, Icelandic, Bengali), proceed with a 6-language subset covering high-resource (en, de, fr, zh) and mid-resource (ja, tr) languages, which still captures CLEAR's key finding about linguistic similarity effects. We budget 2–3 weeks for data resolution at the start of the project.

**Worst-case timeline model.** In the worst case (CLEAR reconstruction needed + 6-language fallback), Tracks 1–2 begin on the reduced dataset by week 4, with Track 3 steering experiments completed by week 14, leaving 3 weeks for writing — tight but feasible for ICLR 2027 (abstract deadline estimated ~September 19, 2026, extrapolating from ICLR 2026's September 19, 2025 abstract deadline). If the worst case materializes and any track encounters unexpected delays, NAACL 2027 via ARR (October 2026 cycle) serves as the immediate fallback, with additional time for extended language coverage and ablation studies.

**Track 1: Cross-Lingual Conflict Detection via Residual Stream Probing.** We extend the residual stream probing methodology (Zhao et al., NeurIPS 2024 Workshop) to cross-lingual conflicts, with Brink et al.'s (Feb 2026) 0.96 Macro-F1 parametric-vs-contextual attribution probe as the monolingual performance ceiling to match or exceed. For each of CLEAR's languages, we train linear probes on residual stream activations at each layer during parametric-vs-contextual conflict resolution on instruct models (Qwen3-8B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct). We then test three transfer settings: (a) same-language probe transfer (trained on English conflicts, tested on English), (b) cross-lingual probe transfer (trained on English conflicts, tested on each target language), and (c) typological cluster transfer (trained on one language family, tested on another). This reveals whether conflict detection is language-universal or language-specific. We further conduct layer-wise analysis connecting to 'Lost in Multilinguality's' language transition finding, 'Mitigating Cross-Lingual Cultural Inconsistencies' abrupt-emergence finding (layer ~22 in Llama-3.1-8B), and 'Where Knowledge Collides' finding that attention components in layers 21–47 dominate intra-memory conflict resolution in Qwen3-4B — testing whether a similar layer range constitutes a critical zone for cross-lingual factual conflict resolution and whether the final-layer language transition creates a unique bottleneck.

**Track 2: Cross-Lingual Conflict Feature Analysis via SAEs.** We extend SpARE's SAE-based conflict feature methodology to cross-lingual settings. To address the documented base/instruct SAE mismatch (Li et al. 2025 showed base-model SAEs have reduced reconstruction quality on instruct models), we adopt a dual strategy: (a) For SAE analysis, we adapt CLEAR's benchmark to a cloze/completion format suitable for base models and use Qwen-Scope's pretrained SAEs on Qwen3-8B-Base, ensuring matched training distributions. (b) We validate consistency between base-model SAE findings and instruct-model probing results (Track 1). Using SpARE's mutual-information-based feature selection as our baseline methodology, we identify SAE features differentially activated during cross-lingual vs. monolingual conflicts. We test whether SpARE's monolingual conflict features (identified on English conflicts) activate during cross-lingual conflicts, and discover features unique to the cross-lingual setting. To disentangle language-identity features from conflict features, we adopt LangFIR's random-token filtering as a control: features that activate for random-token sequences in a given language encode language identity, not conflict information, and are excluded from our conflict feature set. LangFIR's finding that language-specific features are extremely sparse (< 5 per language) and concentrate in late layers, while SpARE's conflict features concentrate in mid-layers, suggests these feature sets are largely non-overlapping — but we verify this empirically. Connecting to the emerging evidence that cross-lingual SAE feature transfer is real but uneven — Qwen-Scope documents this for toxicity features and 'A Universal Vibe?' for informal register features — we investigate whether conflict-encoding features exhibit similar cross-lingual sharing patterns. Crucially, whether conflict features transfer cross-lingually is an open empirical question, not an assumed property; a negative finding (language-pair-specific conflict features with minimal cross-lingual overlap) would be equally informative, suggesting that conflict resolution recruits qualitatively different circuits than toxicity detection or register control.

**Track 3: Cross-Lingual Conflict Steering (Intervention).** Building on features identified in Track 2, we extend SpARE's training-free steering framework to cross-lingual settings, transforming our analysis into a practical tool. This track is motivated by SpARE's demonstrated success at SAE-based steering for knowledge conflicts specifically — achieving +10% over representation engineering and +15% over contrastive decoding via constraint-aware activation editing of <0.05% of SAE activations — while remaining cognizant of the domain-specificity of steering success. 'Interpretability without actionability' (Basu et al., March 2026) showed that SAE steering produced zero effect on clinical triage error correction despite 98.2% AUROC probe accuracy, highlighting that steering success is not guaranteed by interpretability success. We argue that knowledge conflict steering differs fundamentally from clinical triage along five dimensions: (i) SpARE uses pretrained SAEs (GemmaScope) with well-matched training distributions, not custom SAEs trained on 400 examples; (ii) SpARE's constraint-aware activation editing dynamically computes input-dependent removal/addition with mathematical guarantees against over-removal, unlike Basu et al.'s fixed-value clamping that is vulnerable to residual stream compensation; (iii) SpARE selects features by mutual information (causal relevance) rather than statistical association (p-value thresholding); (iv) SpARE targets empirically validated mid-layer ranges (6 layers) rather than a single arbitrary layer; (v) knowledge conflict has a binary causal structure (parametric vs. contextual source) that maps directly to behavioral output, unlike the multi-factor clinical judgment in triage. Our Track 3 therefore tests whether SpARE's *domain-specific, methodologically sophisticated* success extends cross-lingually, not whether SAE steering works in general. We frame this as a genuinely open empirical question: if cross-lingual conflict features are less causally concentrated than monolingual ones (e.g., distributed across language-specific subspaces that resist sparse intervention), steering may partially or fully fail — and this negative result would itself be a significant finding, establishing boundary conditions for SAE-based intervention. We conduct three steering experiments: (a) *Monolingual-to-cross-lingual transfer*: Apply SpARE's English-identified conflict features to steer cross-lingual conflict resolution — testing whether monolingual steering generalizes. (b) *Cross-lingual-specific steering*: Use Track 2's cross-lingual-specific features to construct targeted steering vectors — testing whether these outperform monolingual feature steering for cross-lingual conflicts. (c) *Typological steering*: Test whether steering effectiveness correlates with CLEAR's linguistic similarity metric, providing a causal (not just correlational) link between linguistic affinity and conflict resolution. We evaluate using SpARE's established metrics (EM_C, EM_M, flip rates EM_C→M and EM_M→C) extended to multilingual settings. We also report steering failure modes: cases where conflict features are identifiable (Track 2) but not steerable, following 'Interpretability without actionability's' framework for characterizing the interpretability-actionability gap.

**Cross-validation and integration.** We triangulate across all three tracks: Track 1 identifies WHERE cross-lingual conflict signals emerge (layer-wise probing), Track 2 reveals WHAT features encode these signals (SAE analysis with LangFIR-based language-identity controls), and Track 3 tests WHETHER these features causally control conflict resolution (steering, with explicit reporting of both successes and failures). We compare patterns across typological clusters (e.g., Romance vs. CJK vs. Arabic) to test whether linguistic similarity governs not just behavioral outcomes (CLEAR) but also mechanistic representations and steering effectiveness.
