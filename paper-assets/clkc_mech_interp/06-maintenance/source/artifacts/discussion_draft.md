## Detection Does Not Imply Decision

The central finding of our causal analysis is the layer dissociation between where conflict signals are detectable and where they influence behavior. Probing accuracy for Llama peaks at layer 28 (88\% depth), where the model's representation of the conflict outcome is most linearly separable. Yet steering at this layer produces no significant behavioral change (Fisher $p > 0.3$). Causal influence instead resides at layer 13 (41\% depth), where perturbations along the probe direction shift the model's answer between contextual and parametric responses in both directions (Fisher $p = 0.002$ and $p < 0.001$). This pattern suggests that conflict resolution is computed at intermediate layers, and the late-layer signal is a post-hoc encoding of a decision already formed upstream. The mid-layer locus aligns with prior work showing that language-independent representations emerge at intermediate depths in multilingual models \citep{muller2021first, wendler2024llamas}, suggesting that conflict resolution and cross-lingual alignment share a computational stage. From a methodological standpoint, this result reinforces the caution of \citet{basu2026interpretability}: probe accuracy alone is an unreliable guide for selecting intervention targets, and steering experiments restricted to the probing peak can produce false negatives about the causal role of detected signals.

## Asymmetry as a General Cross-Lingual Property

The directional asymmetry in cross-lingual transfer---where Chinese-trained probes transfer to English more accurately than the reverse by approximately 7 percentage points---initially appeared to reveal something specific about how conflict signals are organized across languages. The XNLI baseline reframes this interpretation. Probes trained on a non-conflict semantic distinction (entailment versus contradiction) show the same asymmetry at comparable magnitude (Llama $+6.0$ pp, Qwen $+6.7$ pp), with confidence intervals that overlap substantially with those of the conflict task (Llama $+7.2$ pp, Qwen $+7.2$ pp). The asymmetry is therefore not a property of conflict encoding but of the model's representational geometry: in English-dominant models, Chinese representations project more readily onto English subspace than the reverse. This connects to the English-centric latent space documented by \citet{wendler2024llamas} and the cross-lingual alignment asymmetries measured by \citet{kargaran2024mexa}. For cross-lingual probing studies more broadly, any observed transfer asymmetry must be benchmarked against a non-conflict baseline before being attributed to the phenomenon under investigation.

## Non-Linear Encoding: A Directional Trend

We observe a suggestive but inconclusive pattern in non-linear encoding. MLP probes show larger gains over linear probes on the conflict task than on XNLI for cross-lingual transfer (EN$\to$ZH) in both models---a direction that would be expected if conflict signals contained task-specific non-linear structure. However, bootstrap confidence intervals include zero in both cases (Llama $+5.2$ pp, CI [$-2.2$, $+12.9$]; Qwen $+3.8$ pp, CI [$-3.6$, $+11.3$]), and we report this as a directional trend rather than a confirmed finding. The absence of a significant non-linear component is itself informative: it indicates that the conflict signal captured by cross-lingual probes is predominantly linear, consistent with the sufficiency of logistic regression probes for cross-lingual semantic classification \citep{belinkov2022probing}.

## Model-Specificity of Causal Interventions

The causal steering result is supported by Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, where layer 13 shows robust bidirectional effects that survive Bonferroni correction. Analogous experiments on Qwen3-8B at comparable relative depths (layers 14 and 23) yield no significant steering effects. This model-specificity does not invalidate the detection--decision dissociation---the absence of late-layer causal effects holds in both models---but it constrains the generality of mid-layer steering as an intervention strategy. The difference may reflect architectural factors that affect how conflict resolution is distributed across layers, such as differences in layer normalization, attention mechanisms, or pre-training language distributions. Model-specificity of causal interventions is itself an informative result: it suggests that mechanistic interpretability findings may not transfer across architectures as readily as probing patterns do, and that each model family may require independent causal validation even when probing results are qualitatively similar.

## Broader Implications

These results contribute to both the understanding of cross-lingual knowledge conflicts and the methodology of cross-lingual probing. \citet{zhao2026when} showed that in entity-centric factual conflicts, linguistic affinity determines which answer the model selects. Our analysis provides a partial mechanistic account: conflict signals are encoded in the model's shared multilingual representation space and inherit its directional biases, which in English-dominant models favor the English direction. The confound exclusion pipeline---combining translation quality stratification, token overlap analysis, and non-conflict baseline comparison---addresses methodological gaps that apply to any cross-lingual probing study where translation artifacts or general transfer properties could be mistaken for task-specific phenomena. The detection--decision dissociation carries practical implications for work on controlling model behavior in multilingual settings: interventions targeting the layer where a signal is most detectable may miss the computational stage where the decision is formed, and mid-layer representations deserve priority as intervention targets in future work.
