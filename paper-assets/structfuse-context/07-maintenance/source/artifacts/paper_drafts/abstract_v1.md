# Abstract

**What Makes Hard Negatives Work for Code Retrieval? A Systematic Study of Mining Strategies and Representation Geometry**

Hard negative mining is critical for training contrastive code retrievers, yet the field lacks systematic understanding of which mining strategies work and why. Prior work argues that non-differentiable embedding negatives are ineffective compared to gradient-based alternatives, but this claim has not been evaluated under controlled conditions in structural code retrieval settings. Meanwhile, code's rich structural properties—call graphs, data-flow dependencies—remain entirely unexploited for negative selection.

We conduct a controlled comparison of three hard negative mining strategies for repository-level code retrieval: random sampling, static embedding-distance mining, and dataflow-aware mining guided by inter-procedural data-flow analysis via CodeQL. All experiments use identical training pipelines across two decoder-only encoders (Qwen2.5-Coder-3B and StarCoder2-3B) with X random seeds each, enabling analysis of strategy–encoder interactions.

We report three findings. First, static embedding-distance mining provides a reliable retrieval improvement over random baselines (+X.X pp Recall@1 averaged across encoders and seeds) at zero additional computational cost, establishing a strong baseline for future work. Second, as a preliminary observation, dataflow-aware negatives do not consistently outperform embedding mining but exhibit an encoder-dependent interaction pattern: negative difficulty margin analysis suggests that encoder representation geometry—specifically, the degree of embedding space saturation—may mediate strategy effectiveness. Third, our CodeQL-based pipeline scales to X repositories with 0% timeout rate, demonstrating that structure-grounded negative mining is practically feasible at scale. All improvements are training-time interventions with zero inference overhead, validated on downstream SWE-bench Lite evaluation.
