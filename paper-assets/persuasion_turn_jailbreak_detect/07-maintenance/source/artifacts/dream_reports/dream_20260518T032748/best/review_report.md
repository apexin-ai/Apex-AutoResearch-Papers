## 1. Paper Summary

DisPeD fine-tunes DeBERTa-v3-base on 8-class persuasion strategy classification plus binary jailbreak-intent (Stage 1), then aggregates turn embeddings via GRU or mean pooling for conversation-level jailbreak detection (Stage 2). Trained on 480 topic-anchored Crescendo conversations, it achieves F1=1.0 on unseen Deceptive Delight attacks where a vanilla DeBERTa+GRU baseline collapses to F1=0.28 (+72pp). Systematic ablation shows the persuasion fine-tuning objective is the sole critical ingredient: detection is insensitive to label quality, granularity, architecture, and loss weighting.

---

## 2. Strengths (Consensus)

**S1. Large and clean headline delta (R1, R2).** The +72pp gap between treatment (F1=1.0) and baseline (F1=0.284) on DD is striking. The baseline's monotonic degradation with more turns (K=1: 0.575 to full: 0.284) is a genuinely interesting finding that reveals vanilla encoders actively accumulate misleading signal as DD's interleaving deceptive structure unfolds.

**S2. Unusually thorough and honest ablation (R1, R2).** Four independent ablation axes (label quality, granularity, lambda, architecture) all converge on the same conclusion. The scrambled-label control is a particularly strong causal probe. Many papers would bury the finding that scrambled labels achieve F1=0.99; the authors foreground it. This transparency strengthens trust.

**S3. Honest positioning against TF-IDF (R1, R2).** The paper does not hide that TF-IDF achieves F1=0.944 on DD. It explicitly acknowledges inherent lexical separability and positions DisPeD's value along three complementary axes (bridging vanilla temporal failure, perturbation robustness, interpretability). This candor is rare.

**S4. Perturbation experiment is well-designed (R1).** BERTScore=0.92 with lexical overlap=0.20 confirms semantic preservation with lexical disruption. Treatment's zero degradation at K>=2 vs baseline's -11.8pp provides credible evidence that learned features go beyond surface lexical cues.

**S5. LLM-as-judge comparison fills a practical gap (R1, R2).** Showing GPT-5.5 achieves at most F1=0.45 with 30-47% unparseable outputs establishes that frontier models analyzing content cannot detect process-level manipulation. Qwen3-8B's near-zero performance confirms this is not a model-size issue.

**S6. Clear experimental narrative (R2).** The paper reads as a coherent investigation. Each table answers a specific question, and the discussion synthesizes findings into a unified mechanistic story.

## 3. Weaknesses (Consensus)

### Critical

**W1. Entirely synthetic OOD evaluation data undermines the headline claim (R1, R2).** All OOD test data (80 FITD, 80 DD) was generated by the authors using a "controlled generation pipeline." The training data (480 conversations) is also synthetic. The headline claim of F1=1.0 on "unseen attack types" is F1=1.0 on *synthetic approximations* generated by the same team. The generation process likely embeds systematic stylistic signatures (from the generation LLM, playbook templates, or annotation pipeline) that make transfer artificially easy. Without evaluation on real-world multi-turn attacks (e.g., from JailbreakBench, red-teaming logs, or published attack datasets), the ecological validity of the cross-attack generalization claim remains unestablished. The Limitations section acknowledges this, but the framing throughout the paper does not reflect the caveat with appropriate prominence.

**W2. The core "method" is standard domain-adaptive fine-tuning, not a novel architecture (R2).** The technical contribution reduces to: fine-tune an encoder on a domain-relevant auxiliary task, then use the encoder for downstream classification. This is a well-established paradigm (Caruana 1997, STILTs/Phang et al. 2018, MT-DNN/Liu et al. 2019, "Don't Stop Pretraining"/Gururangan et al. 2020). The paper does not cite or position against this body of work at all. The ablation reveals that the "method" effectively reduces to "expose the encoder to persuasion-domain text during fine-tuning," since scrambled labels, binary labels, any lambda, and mean pooling all produce equivalent detection. The contribution is an empirical finding (persuasion-domain fine-tuning rescues OOD failure), not a methodological advance, but the paper is framed as the latter. Reframing as an empirical findings paper would be more honest and potentially stronger.

**W3. Scrambled labels achieving F1=0.99 raises fundamental questions about the claimed mechanism (R1, R2).** If random labels work almost as well as correct labels, the encoder is not learning persuasion strategies; it is learning distributional properties of the fine-tuning corpus. The "persuasion-aware" framing is potentially misleading. The method may more accurately be described as "domain-adaptive fine-tuning on jailbreak-adjacent text." The critical missing control: would fine-tuning on *any* conversational persuasion corpus (without the specific taxonomy), or even continued pretraining (MLM) on the same text, produce the same result?

### Major

**W4. Perfect F1=1.0 results and small test sets create ceiling effects (R1, R2).** Treatment achieves F1=1.0 with zero variance across 3 seeds on n=72 IID and n=80 OOD per type. A single misclassification on 40 positive DD samples would drop F1 to ~0.97. By the rule of three, the 95% CI upper bound for the error rate is 7.2%, meaning true F1 could be as low as ~0.93. The pervasive 1.0 results prevent meaningful comparison between configurations (treatment, binary, scrambled all within 1pp at F1=0.99-1.0). The evaluation does not discriminate between "the method works perfectly" and "the synthetic test set is too easy."

**W5. TF-IDF at F1=0.944 on DD makes incremental value questionable (R1, R2).** The treatment's advantage over TF-IDF reduces to: (a) 5.6pp on DD raw detection (within the confidence interval given n=80), (b) 7.0pp advantage at K=1 under perturbation, and (c) interpretability via strategy trajectories. A TF-IDF model is orders of magnitude cheaper, faster, and transparent. The paper needs stronger evidence that the neural approach provides value beyond what TF-IDF offers.

**W6. 8-class interpretability claim (Claim 2) is weakly supported (R2).** Strategy trajectory analysis (Table 7) provides descriptive statistics but no demonstration that trajectories are actionable or useful to human analysts. Given that binary labels suffice for detection, the taxonomy must justify itself through interpretability, but that justification is only sketched (transition entropy, mean index) with many missing entries in Table 7.

**W7. Related work is thin and misses key literatures (R2).** 25 references is below top-venue norms. Critical missing areas:
- Multi-task learning / auxiliary task selection (Caruana 1997, Phang et al. 2018 STILTs, Liu et al. 2019 MT-DNN)
- Domain-adaptive pretraining (Gururangan et al. ACL 2020 "Don't Stop Pretraining")
- Real-world multi-turn attack datasets (Li et al. 2024 "LLM Defenses Are Not Robust to Multi-Turn Human Jailbreaks Yet")
- FITD original paper (the attack type is evaluated but not cited)
- ActorAttack (Ren et al. 2024)
- Several bibliography entries are incomplete (BIID, G-Guard, SafeDream lack author names)
- Orphan references appear in bibliography but not in text (Austin 1962, Searle 1969)

### Minor

**W8. 8% FPR on hard negatives has underexplored deployment implications (R1).** At 1% attack base rate and 8% FPR, the positive predictive value would be ~11%. No precision-recall curve is provided.

**W9. DeepContext proxy is not a fair comparison (R1).** BERT-base (not BERT-large or actual DeepContext model) trained on the authors' dataset rather than original DeepContext data. This adds little beyond what the vanilla DeBERTa baseline already shows.

**W10. Tables have incomplete reporting (R1, R2).** Table 2 baseline std shown as "---" for K=2,3,5 without explanation. Table 3 FITD results for K=2,3,5 are "---". Table 7 has many "---" and "(see pattern)" entries.

**W11. The 8-class taxonomy is never enumerated (R2).** For a paper claiming the taxonomy as a contribution, the classes should be listed with descriptions in the main text.

**W12. No figures in the paper (R2).** For a 10+ page submission, at least an architecture diagram and a cross-attack comparison visualization would improve accessibility.

**W13. Missing reproducibility/ethics statements (R2).** Increasingly expected at top venues.

## 4. Questions for Authors

Q1. How exactly were the 80 FITD and 80 DD conversations generated? What LLM was used? Were persuasion labels assigned by the same pipeline that generated training data? Could systematic generation artifacts inflate cross-attack transfer?

Q2. Would fine-tuning DeBERTa on an existing persuasion corpus (e.g., ChangeMyView, PAP attack prompts from Zeng et al. 2024) produce the same generalization benefit? Would continued pretraining (MLM) on the same text, without any classification head, also bridge the OOD gap?

Q3. Can you report exact counts (TP, FP, FN, TN) for each condition, and exact binomial confidence intervals, rather than only F1?

Q4. The baseline's DD F1 decreases with more turns (0.575 at K=1 to 0.284 at full). Can you provide error analysis explaining what types of DD conversations the baseline misclassifies and why more turns make it worse?

Q5. Were benign conversations in OOD test sets topic-anchored to the OOD attack topics? If not, the DD result could partially reflect topic divergence, not persuasion detection.

Q6. Why are Llama Guard and WildGuard not evaluated directly as baselines, given they represent production standards?

Q7. The scrambled-label experiment shows F1=0.935 at K=1 vs 1.0 for proper labels (6.5pp gap). Is this gap statistically significant given 80 test conversations?

Q8. What happens if you fine-tune on a different auxiliary task entirely (e.g., sentiment, NLI) using the same persuasion-related text? This would isolate whether the taxonomy matters vs just training on text from the right domain.

## 5. Missing Experiments (Priority-Ordered)

1. **Evaluation on real-world multi-turn attacks** (e.g., JailbreakBench, MHJ dataset, red-teaming logs). This is the single most important addition.
2. **Domain-adaptive pretraining control:** Continued pretraining (MLM) on the same persuasion text without labels, to test whether supervision matters or just domain exposure.
3. **Alternative auxiliary task control:** Fine-tune on a non-persuasion task (sentiment, topic, NLI) using the same text to isolate domain vs task effects.
4. **Precision-recall curve on WildChat hard negatives** across thresholds.
5. **Larger OOD test sets** (at minimum 160 per type) to break ceiling effects.
6. **Enumeration and validation of the 8-class taxonomy** with inter-annotator agreement or user study.
7. **Comparison with BIID, G-Guard, SafeDream** (cited but not evaluated).

## 7. Roundtable Convergence Notes

**Agreement areas:** Both reviewers converge on synthetic-only OOD evaluation as the top concern, praise ablation thoroughness and honest self-assessment, identify ceiling effects from small test sets, and note TF-IDF competitiveness limits incremental value.

**Divergence:** R1 finds the applied contribution sufficient for a weak accept; R2 argues the novelty is insufficient without engagement with transfer learning / domain-adaptive pretraining literature. After cross-sharing, R1 acknowledged R2's novelty concerns have merit (the missing positioning against multi-task learning literature is a real gap), while R2 acknowledged R1's point that the +72pp delta is large enough to have practical value even if the mechanism is well-known.

**Consensus resolution:** The panel converges at **3/6 (Weak Reject)** because:
1. The synthetic-only evaluation makes the headline claim unverifiable on real attacks (both reviewers)
2. The novelty gap (standard domain-adaptive fine-tuning without proper positioning) is a real issue for a top venue (R2, acknowledged by R1)
3. The ceiling effects prevent meaningful differentiation between method variants (both reviewers)
4. The paper would be stronger reframed as an empirical findings paper with real-world evaluation data

The paper is close to the acceptance boundary. A revision addressing (1) real-world OOD evaluation, (2) domain-adaptive pretraining controls, and (3) reframing with proper multi-task learning positioning could move it to a 4 or 5.

## 8. Actionable Revision Recommendations

**For acceptance at a top venue, the following changes are recommended (priority order):**

1. **Add real-world OOD evaluation.** Source real multi-turn jailbreak conversations (JailbreakBench, MHJ dataset, published red-teaming logs). Even a small set (30-50 conversations) would dramatically strengthen the generalization claim.

2. **Add domain-adaptive pretraining control.** Run continued pretraining (MLM, no labels) on the same persuasion text. If this matches the supervised version, reframe the contribution accordingly. If it doesn't, the supervision signal claim is strengthened.

3. **Reframe as empirical findings.** The honest framing is: "We discover that persuasion-domain fine-tuning is a sufficient supervision signal for cross-attack generalization. We systematically ablate this finding across four axes." This is a stronger pitch than the current method-paper framing, because the ablation shows every design choice is interchangeable.

4. **Expand Related Work with transfer learning literature.** Cite and position against Caruana 1997, Phang et al. 2018 (STILTs), Gururangan et al. 2020 ("Don't Stop Pretraining"), Liu et al. 2019 (MT-DNN). Acknowledge the paradigm explicitly.

5. **Report exact counts and confidence intervals.** Provide TP/FP/FN/TN for each condition. Report exact binomial CIs. This addresses the statistical power concern without requiring more data.

6. **Enumerate the 8-class taxonomy.** List all 8 classes with brief descriptions in the main text.

7. **Complete bibliography.** Add author names for BIID, G-Guard, SafeDream. Cite the FITD original paper. Remove orphan references (Austin, Searle) if not cited in text. Target 35-40 references.

8. **Add at least one figure.** Architecture overview and/or cross-attack F1 comparison bar chart.
