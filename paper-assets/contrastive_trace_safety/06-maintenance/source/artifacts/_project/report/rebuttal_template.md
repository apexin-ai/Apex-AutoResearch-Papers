# Anticipated Reviewer Concerns --- Rebuttal Template

## Concern 1: Single Benchmark (Generalizability)

**Anticipated question:** The paper relies primarily on HarmThoughts with only partial AdvBench validation. How confident can we be that the metric-induced layer selection bias and the HS advantage generalize to other jailbreak datasets, attack taxonomies, or multilingual settings?

**Response:** Our central contribution (Contribution 1) is a *methodological* finding about how metric choice reverses probe conclusions, which is an evaluation-procedure property rather than a data-dependent effect. We provide four lines of cross-dataset and replication evidence: (1) the geometric precondition underlying the bias---shallow-layer cosine compression---is confirmed on AdvBench with a 1.67x volatility ratio, consistent with the 1.4--2.7x range on HarmThoughts (Appendix B, Table 5); (2) split-half replication on OT-7B shows both halves independently reproduce the CR-to-shallow / threshold-to-deep divergence with consistent HS advantage direction (Appendix Q, Table 24); (3) random-label controls on two architectures (QwQ-32B and R1-8B) confirm that the metric disagreement requires genuine safety labels (Appendix A.1, Tables 3--4); and (4) the LLM-based IAA proxy on AdvBench (kappa=0.606; Appendix G.1, Table 10) provides a lower-bound estimate of annotation transferability. We explicitly acknowledge in the Limitations section that empirical effect sizes (Contribution 2) are sample-dependent and that generalization to other datasets and multilingual settings is untested.

## Concern 2: CP Annotation Quality

**Anticipated question:** HarmThoughts annotations lack formal inter-annotator agreement. The LLM proxy achieves only moderate agreement (kappa=0.606) with low CP position consistency (37% exact match). How reliable are the paper's conclusions given this annotation uncertainty?

**Response:** Three complementary analyses bound annotation sensitivity. First, random-label controls (Appendix A.1, Tables 3--4) demonstrate that the probe signal *requires* genuine CP labels: under random labels, threshold-based metrics on R1-8B degenerate entirely (precision and inverse FPR select L0 in all 10 repeats with zero variance), confirming the probe captures real structure rather than artifacts. Second, the CP annotation perturbation analysis (Appendix G, Table 9) shows that shifting all CP labels by up to +/-3 steps produces smooth, gradual degradation in precision without cliff effects---within +/-2 steps, BA changes by less than 2.5pp for 7--8B models, and all models retain precision well above their text baselines even at the +/-3-step worst case. Third, the moderate LLM kappa of 0.606 (Appendix G.1, Table 10) reflects annotator disagreement primarily on jailbreak *classification* (GPT-4o-mini identifies nearly twice as many jailbreaks as Qwen3-32B), which affects dataset composition but not the within-trace binary pre/post-CP classification task that our probes address. We acknowledge that formal multi-annotator human IAA on HarmThoughts is needed and flag this as a limitation.

## Concern 3: Limited Novelty / "Just Linear Probes"

**Anticipated question:** The paper uses standard linear probes (logistic regression) without introducing new probe architectures or training methods. Is the contribution sufficiently novel for a top venue?

**Response:** The contribution is the *finding*, not the method. Our five-metric ablation (Section 4.1, Table 2, Figure 2) reveals that the choice of layer selection metric---typically treated as a minor implementation detail---entirely determines whether an HS advantage is observed: for QwQ-32B, the CR-composite layer (L2, 3% depth) yields p=0.83 (non-significant), while the threshold-based layer (L63, 98% depth) yields p<0.005, a qualitative reversal of the study's central conclusion. This is a meta-methodological contribution about how probe evaluation is conducted, demonstrating that published hidden-state results may warrant re-examination (Discussion, paragraph 1). The simplicity of the probing method is deliberate: seven more complex alternatives all fail structurally (Table 14; Section 4.5)---BOCPD/CUSUM achieve 0% detection on 3/4 models, Mahalanobis yields near-zero separation, RepE is model-dependent, MLP probes overfit, CCS misaligns temporally, and cross-model transfer fails. These negative results (the most comprehensive systematic comparison for safety commitment detection) define method boundaries and demonstrate this is not a simple probing exercise. The three-layer ensemble (Appendix R, Table 25) further eliminates metric pre-selection entirely, achieving significance across all four models (p <= 0.002).

## Concern 4: Architecture Coverage (3/4 Qwen)

**Anticipated question:** Three of the four models share the Qwen architecture. How can the paper claim cross-architecture generality with only one Llama-based model (R1-8B)?

**Response:** We explicitly frame architecture coverage as a limitation (Limitations, paragraph 3) and classify results accordingly: only findings replicated on R1-8B (Llama) support cross-architecture claims. Crucially, the random-label controls (Appendix A.1, Tables 3--4) *decompose* the architecture-specific vs. universal components of our findings. On QwQ-32B (Qwen), crossing-rate shallow-layer preference is label-independent (100% of random-label repeats still select shallow layers), confirming a Qwen-specific geometric artifact. On R1-8B (Llama), even crossing-rate selection becomes label-dependent under random labels (0% shallow), demonstrating that the geometric bias mechanism differs across architectures. This decomposition is itself a contribution: we identify the Qwen-specific geometric bias rather than incorrectly generalizing it as universal. R1-8B independently validates the core finding (metric-induced layer selection bias) with a 16-layer gap between crossing-rate L0 and threshold-based L14 (Appendix A, Table 2), and maintains significant HS advantage across all 25 layers and window sizes (precision gap ~15--17pp), unlike the Qwen models where significance drops at shallow layers.

## Concern 5: No Causal Validation

**Anticipated question:** The results are purely correlational. Without causal interventions (DAS, activation patching, circuit analysis), how can we know whether the probes detect genuine safety representations versus confounds like position?

**Response:** We agree that causal validation is needed and explicitly flag DAS, ITI, and circuit breakers as future work (Discussion, paragraph 3). However, three sets of controls provide partial mechanistic evidence that goes beyond pure correlation. First, position controls (Appendix O, Table 20) show that under quadratic position residualization, both 7--8B models retain significant non-positional HS advantage (+10.3pp and +9.8pp, both HB-corrected), with Cohen's d = 2.37 (R1-8B) and 0.85 (QwQ-32B), both exceeding the conventional "large" threshold (Appendix O). Second, control task selectivity (Appendix D) confirms genuine safety structure: selectivity scores of 11.1--16.8pp all exceed the 5pp threshold recommended by Hewitt and Liang (2019), and residualized selectivity (after position removal) remains above 5pp for 3/4 models. Third, the paper's scope is diagnostic---identifying that metric choice reverses conclusions about probe modality---not mechanistic. The metric bias finding (Contribution 1) holds regardless of the causal mechanism: whether the probes detect genuine safety representations or sophisticated positional correlates, the fact that metric choice reverses the conclusion is a methodological concern for the field that causal validation does not resolve.
